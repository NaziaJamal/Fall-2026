Lesson Overview

Students read the whole story in class and finish by judging whether the narrator can be trusted, using quotations as evidence. This one lesson covers the missing Unit 2 story before the midterm and introduces the unreliable narrator, which returns in Units 4 and 5 and the final exam.

CourseReading & Critical Thinking, Independent Level (A2+ to B1+)
WeekWeek 5 (one 110-minute class, no break)
Story"The Tell-Tale Heart" by Edgar Allan Poe (1843), Unit 2: Justice & Morality
Students7 adults: 6 Brazilian, 1 Colombian
MethodESA (Engage, Study, Activate)
Pre-readingNone. Students meet the story for the first time in class.

Literary focus (three ideas only): first-person narrator, unreliable narrator, and suspense.

Learning objectives

By the end of the class, students will be able to:

  1. Follow the full plot and explain the narrator's motive in their own words.
  2. Explain what an unreliable narrator is, with one example from the story.
  3. Find quotations that support an interpretation and use them when speaking.
  4. Write a short response that includes one quotation from the story.

Materials and preparation

  • Student page open on the projector and on students' phones or laptops (tabs: Adapted Story, Worksheet, Vocabulary Cards)
  • Short heartbeat sound clip, ready to play
  • Engage slide: the four narrator lines (also in the Worksheet tab)
  • Evidence chart: students use the Worksheet tab; optionally also one large sheet per group
  • Trial question and sentence frames on the board or a slide (also in the Worksheet tab)
  • Timer visible to students
  • Groups decided in advance: Group A (3 students) and Group B (4 students), each with a mix of levels

EngageWho Is Speaking? 6:00–6:15 · 15 min

Students hear the narrator's voice before they know the story, guess the situation, and rate how much they trust him. The trust ratings become the example you use to teach "unreliable narrator" later.

Agenda (3 min, whole class)

Show the agenda and the four objectives. Say: "Tonight we read a famous horror story from 1843. At the end, you will decide if the storyteller is telling the truth."

Who Is Speaking? (5 min, pairs + one group of 3)

Teacher
  1. Turn off the lights if possible. Play the heartbeat sound for 10 seconds.
  2. Show Line 1 only, with no title or author: "I am nervous. I have been very, very, terribly nervous, and I still am. But why do you say that I am mad?"
  3. Pairs answer four questions: Who is speaking? Who is he talking to? Where is he? What do you think he did?
  4. Take one quick guess from each pair. Write key guesses on the board. Do not confirm or correct.
Student instructions (slide)Read the line with your partner. Answer: Who? To whom? Where? What did he do? You have 3 minutes.

The Trust Scale (7 min, individual, then whole class)

Teacher
  1. Show Lines 2, 3 and 4 one at a time:
    • "Listen, and see how healthy, how calm I am as I tell you the whole story."
    • "I loved the old man. He had never done anything wrong to me."
    • "Ha! Would a madman be as wise as this?"
  2. After each line, students show 1 to 5 fingers (and mark it in the Worksheet tab): 1 = "I don't trust him at all," 5 = "I trust him completely."
  3. After Line 4, ask two or three students to explain their number: "Why did you choose 2?"
  4. Write the class's numbers on the board and leave them there for Study C.
Student instructions (slide)Read each line. How much do you trust this person? Show 1 to 5 fingers. Be ready to explain your number.
Useful language: I don't trust him because... / He says..., but... / Something is strange about...

Study AVocabulary Cards 6:15–6:23 · 8 min

Students preview the 8 words they most need to follow the plot, using the Vocabulary Cards tab in Practice Mode. The other 13 words stay available as tap-to-see glosses while they read.

Core 8 words: nervous, mad, vulture, lantern, suspect, groan, muffled, admit.

Card practice (5 min, pairs + one group of 3)

Teacher
  1. Students open the student page and choose Vocabulary Cards, then Practice Mode. Definitions and examples are now blurred.
  2. Write the core 8 words on the board. Pairs find these cards only.
  3. Partner A reads the word aloud using the stress shown, then guesses the meaning. Partner B taps "Show definition and examples" to check. They switch every card. In the group of 3, the third student presses Listen to check pronunciation.
  4. Monitor pronunciation, especially VUL·ture, LAN·tern and sus·PECT.
Student instructions (slide)Open Vocabulary Cards and press Practice Mode. Find the 8 words on the board. Say the word, guess the meaning, then tap to check. Take turns.

Three words to highlight (3 min, whole class)

  1. mad: In this story it means mentally ill. In American English today it usually means angry. Ask: "Which meaning does the narrator mean?"
  2. suspect: The verb is sus·PECT, but the noun is SUS·pect. Drill both quickly: "The police suSPECT him. He is the SUSpect."
  3. admit: Tell students to watch for this word at the very end of the story. Do not explain why.

Part 1, aloud (6 min)

Teacher: Assign readers before you start. Student 1 reads ¶1. Students 2 and 3 split ¶2 (Student 3 starts at "I think it was his eye!"). Ask readers to sound nervous, not calm.

Pause (pairs): Stop and Think, Part 1. Collect one answer to "Is that a good reason?" Most students will say no; that is the point.

Part 2, silent (6 min)

Teacher: Tell students to read ¶3 alone and tap any dotted words they need. Circulate and help weaker readers.

Pause (pairs): Stop and Think, Part 2. Push for the key idea: he describes his care because he wants to prove he is not mad.

Part 3, silent (11 min)

Teacher: This is the longest part. Mention first that it contains a violent moment. Give a 9-minute warning on the timer. Fast finishers reread ¶11 and count how many times the sound "grew louder."

Pause (pairs): Stop and Think, Part 3. Do not give the answer to "Is the sound real?" Students will return to it.

Part 4, aloud (6 min)

Teacher: Student 4 reads ¶13, Student 5 reads ¶14–15, Student 6 reads ¶16 to "Oh God! What could I do?", and Student 7 reads the rest of ¶16 and ¶17. Tell Students 6 and 7 to read faster and louder as the panic grows.

Pause (whole class): Stop and Think, Part 4. Then return to the Engage guesses on the board: "Who guessed correctly? What surprised you?"

Student instructions (slide)Read the part on the screen. When you see Stop and Think, discuss the questions with your partner and write short answers in the Worksheet tab. You have 2 minutes.

Study CThe Unreliable Narrator 6:52–7:07 · 15 min

Students learn two terms and immediately test them, building a chart of quotations that becomes their evidence for the trial.

Mini-input (5 min, whole class)

Teacher
  1. Ask: "Who tells this story?" Elicit: the killer himself, using "I." Write first-person narrator: the story is told by a character, using I.
  2. Point to the trust numbers still on the board from Engage. Ask: "Did your numbers change after reading?"
  3. Write unreliable narrator: a narrator we cannot completely believe. What he says and what he does do not match.
  4. Give one model from ¶1: he says "see how healthy, how calm I am," but he is telling a story about murder and repeats "nervous" three times.

Board: first-person narrator = I tell the story · unreliable narrator = says one thing, shows another

Evidence hunt (10 min, Group A of 3 and Group B of 4)

Teacher
  1. Students fill the two-column chart in the Worksheet tab: He says he is sane and This shows he is not.
  2. Each group needs at least three quotations per column, each with its paragraph number.
  3. Split the story so groups don't copy each other: Group A searches ¶1–8, Group B searches ¶9–17.
  4. Circulate. Push stronger students toward less obvious evidence; point weaker students to specific paragraphs.
  5. At 7:06, groups stop. Their charts are their notes for the trial.
Student instructions (slide)In your group, find quotations for each column. Write the words exactly and the paragraph number (¶). Find at least 3 for each column. You have 10 minutes.

Sample answers are in the Answer Key & Notes tab.

ActivateThe Trial 7:07–7:37 · 30 min

Students argue a real interpretive question in teams, using their evidence charts, while you play the narrator in the witness chair. Every student speaks at least twice.

The question for the court (slide)Is the narrator a cold killer who knew what he was doing and belongs in prison, or a mentally ill man who needs a hospital?

This framing avoids legal terms such as "insanity defense," which work differently in Brazil, Colombia and the US. Both sides agree he killed the old man; they argue about why and what should happen to him.

Preparation (10 min, teams)

Teacher: Show the team instructions on the slide. Every student must have a speaking job. Circulate and check that each point has a quotation.

Student instructions (slide) 1. Write 2 opening points. Each point needs one quotation and a paragraph number.
2. Write 2 questions to ask the narrator.
3. Choose jobs: one opener, one or two questioners, one closer. (Defense: two questioners.)

Sentence frames: The narrator says "..." (¶...). This shows that... / If he were really mad / sane, he would not... / Why did you...? / Can you explain why...? / In conclusion, the evidence shows that...

The trial (15 min, whole class)

Arrange the room: the witness chair in the center, Prosecution on one side, Defense on the other. You are both the judge and the narrator; switch voices when needed.

StepTimeWho speaks
Opening statements4 minProsecution opener, then Defense opener (2 min each)
Questions for the narrator6 minAlternate sides, one question at a time; you answer in character
Closing statements3 minProsecution closer, then Defense closer
Transitions2 min
Playing the narrator
  • Stay calm and proud at first; become more nervous as questions get closer to the truth.
  • Repeat his favorite argument: "Would a madman plan so carefully?"
  • Help weaker students: if a question is unclear, rephrase it yourself ("You are asking why I killed him? I'll tell you...").
  • Leave openings for the Defense: mention the sound, the eye, the "ringing in my ears."

Verdict and debrief (5 min, whole class)

  1. Everyone steps out of their role. Students vote on what they really believe: hand vote, or secret vote on paper if the group is shy. They also record it in the Worksheet tab.
  2. Ask: "Which quotation was the most convincing? Why?"
  3. Final question: "Why does Poe let the killer tell the story himself? What would we lose if the police told it?" (Steer toward: we are inside his mind, so we feel his panic and see that he cannot be trusted.)

Common difficulties

  • "Mad" means angry. Students may think the narrator is just angry. Return to the vocabulary note on old and modern meanings.
  • Who is "you"? Students may be confused by the narrator speaking to "you." Say: we don't know; maybe a judge, a doctor, or the reader.
  • The heartbeat. Some students will think the heart really beats. Accept it as a reading, then ask: "Could anyone else hear it?"
  • The violence. ¶12 describes cutting up the body. Mention before Part 3 that the story has a violent moment, and keep the reading moving.
  • Quiet students in the trial. Give them the opener or closer role; they can read prepared text.

1. Who Is Speaking? (Engage)

There are no correct answers here; students are guessing before they read. Listen for guesses you can return to later.

  • Who / where: a prisoner, a patient in a mental hospital, a criminal in a police station or courtroom. All are reasonable: the story never says where he is.
  • Who is he talking to: a judge, a doctor, a police officer, a lawyer. The story never says.
  • Trust Scale: Line 4 usually gets the lowest scores ("Ha!" sounds strange). Line 3 may get a higher score; after reading, it becomes the most shocking line, because he kills the man he says he loved.

2. Stop and Think (Study)

Suggested answers. Accept any answer that is supported by the text.

Part 1: The Eye (¶1–2)

Why does the narrator want to kill the old man? Is that a good reason?Because of the old man's eye: "the eye of a vulture," pale blue with a cloudy layer (¶2). It is not about money ("I did not want his gold") or anger ("There was no anger"). It is not a good reason, and that is the first sign that the narrator is not normal.
The narrator says he is not mad. Do you believe him?Open question. Strong answers notice the contradiction: he says he is "healthy" and "calm," but he repeats that he is "very, very, terribly nervous" and says he hears "many things in hell" (¶1).

Part 2: Seven Nights (¶3)

Why does the narrator tell us so much about how careful he was?He believes careful planning proves he is sane: "Would a madman be as wise as this?" (¶3).
What does he want us to think about him? Does it work?He wants us to see him as clever and wise. It does not work: spending an hour putting his head through a door, every night for seven nights, and being extra kind to the man by day, makes him seem more disturbed, not less.

Part 3: The Eighth Night (¶4–12)

What sound does the narrator hear? Do you think the sound is real?A "low, dull, quick sound," like a watch wrapped in cotton, which he believes is the old man's heart (¶10). Possible readings: (1) the frightened old man's real heartbeat, though hearing it across a dark room is unlikely; (2) his own heartbeat; (3) his imagination or guilt; (4) the death-watch beetles in the wall (¶6). Accept any reading with evidence.
Does the way he hid the body prove he is not mad?No. Hiding the body shows planning, but planning is not the same as sanity. His laughter, "A tub had caught it all. Ha! Ha!" (¶12), and his pride show that something is wrong. This is the key insight of the lesson.

Part 4: The Visitors (¶13–17)

Why does the narrator confess? Did the police really suspect him?He confesses because the sound grows louder and he believes the officers know and are laughing at him (¶16). The police probably did not suspect him: they "were satisfied" (¶15) and "chatted pleasantly and smiled" (¶16). Nothing outside his own mind forces the confession; his guilt does.
Whose heart is "telling the tale" (telling the secret)?Two good readings: (1) the old man's heart, as the narrator believes; (2) the narrator's own heart: his heartbeat, guilt or conscience gives away his secret. "Tell-tale" means something that reveals a secret. Strong students may argue for both.

Good questions for the narrator

  • If you loved the old man, why did you kill him?
  • Why did you hide the body if you did nothing wrong?
  • Why did you put your chair on top of the body?
  • The police were smiling. Why did you think they knew?
  • Can a sane person hear a dead man's heart?

5. Quick Write (Activate)

Model answer at A2+ level, using the frame. Share it after students write, not before.

I think the narrator is not reliable. He says, "So how can I be mad?" (¶1). However, he killed an old man only because of his eye. This shows that he is not thinking normally. Another reason is that he hears the dead man's heart under the floor. For these reasons, I believe we cannot trust him.57 words

Homework

A. Reading journal: sample

  • Summary: A man decides to kill an old man because he hates his pale, cloudy eye. After seven nights of watching, he kills him and hides the body under the floor. When the police visit, he hears a heartbeat getting louder, and he confesses.
  • Vocabulary: any 3 words with learner-level definitions (check against the Vocabulary Cards).
  • Personal response: any honest sentence, e.g., "The ending surprised me because the police did not suspect him."
  • Question: any genuine question, e.g., "Who is the narrator talking to?" These make good openers for next class.

B. Midterm practice paragraph: model (B1+)

The narrator of "The Tell-Tale Heart" is not reliable, because what he says and what he does do not match. From the first paragraph, he insists that he is calm and healthy, and he asks, "So how can I be mad?" (¶1). However, his only reason for killing the old man is his "vulture" eye, and he admits that he "loved the old man" (¶2). A sane person does not kill someone he loves because of an eye. He also believes that careful planning proves he is sane, but his laughter at the hidden body, "Ha! Ha!" (¶12), shows the opposite. Finally, he hears a dead man's heart through the floor and confesses. Poe lets the killer tell his own story so that readers can watch his mind break down.130 words

Notice the structure for the midterm: topic sentence (clear opinion), evidence (quotations with ¶), explanation (what each quotation shows), and a concluding sentence about the author's choice.

Literary Notes for the Teacher

Background for answering student questions. Only the first three terms are taught in this lesson.

TermIn this story
First-person narrator (taught)The killer tells his own story using "I." We only know what he chooses to tell us.
Unreliable narrator (taught)He insists he is sane, but his words and actions prove the opposite. The reader must read "against" him.
Suspense (taught)Poe slows time (an hour to put his head in the door), repeats words ("louder, louder"), and uses short, broken sentences to build tension.
Dramatic ironyHe is kindest to the old man in the week he plans to kill him; he says he is calm while he panics.
SymbolismThe eye can represent being watched or judged; the heart represents guilt or conscience.
Gothic atmosphereMidnight, a dark old house, a lantern's thin ray, death-watch beetles, a hidden body.

For later units: The Yellow Wallpaper (Unit 4) and An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge (Unit 5) return to unreliable narration. Remind students of this story when you get there.
