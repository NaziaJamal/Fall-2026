Before You Read

The author. Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) was an American writer. He is famous for dark stories of mystery, horror, and crime, and many people call him the father of the modern detective story.

The story. A man with no name tells us about something terrible that he did. He speaks directly to "you," the listener. We never learn who "you" is.

Reading tip: Pay attention to two things as you read: what the narrator says about himself, and what his actions show us. Are they the same?

Adapted Version

The Tell-Tale Heart

Edgar Allan Poe

Part 1The Eye

1It's true! I am nervous. I have been very, very, terribly nervous, and I still am. But why do you say that I am mad? My illness made my senses sharper. It did not destroy them, and it did not make them weaker. Most of all, my sense of hearing was very sharp. I heard everything in heaven and on earth. I heard many things in hell. So how can I be mad? Listen! Listen, and see how healthy, how calm I am as I tell you the whole story.

2It is impossible to say how the idea first came into my head. But once it was there, it stayed with me day and night, like a ghost. There was no reason for it. There was no anger. I loved the old man. He had never done anything wrong to me. He had never insulted me. I did not want his gold. I think it was his eye! Yes, that was it! He had the eye of a vulture: a pale blue eye, with a thin, cloudy layer over it. Whenever that eye looked at me, my blood went cold. And so, little by little, very slowly, I decided to kill the old man, and in this way free myself from the eye forever.

Stop and Think · Part 1
  1. Why does the narrator want to kill the old man? Is that a good reason?
  2. The narrator says he is not mad. Do you believe him? Why or why not?
Part 2Seven Nights

3Now this is the important point. You think I am mad. But mad people know nothing. You should have seen me. You should have seen how wisely I worked: how carefully, how well I planned everything, and how well I hid my true feelings! I was never kinder to the old man than during the whole week before I killed him. And every night, at about midnight, I turned the handle of his door and opened it, oh, so gently! Then, when the opening was big enough for my head, I put in a dark lantern. It was completely closed, so no light came out. Then I pushed my head in. Oh, you would have laughed to see how cleverly I pushed it in! I moved it slowly, very, very slowly, so I would not wake the old man. It took me an hour to put my whole head inside the opening, far enough to see him lying on his bed. Ha! Would a madman be as wise as this? And then, when my head was inside the room, I opened the lantern carefully, oh, so carefully, carefully, because the hinges made a noise. I opened it just enough for a single thin ray of light to fall on the vulture eye. And I did this for seven long nights, every night, exactly at midnight. But the eye was always closed, so it was impossible to do the job. It was not the old man who troubled me; it was his Evil Eye. And every morning, when the sun came up, I went boldly into his bedroom and spoke to him bravely. I called him by his name in a warm, friendly voice and asked him how he had slept. So you see, he would have to be a very wise old man to suspect that every night, at exactly twelve o'clock, I was looking at him while he slept.

Stop and Think · Part 2
  1. Why does the narrator tell us so much about how careful he was?
  2. What does he want us to think about him? Does it work?
Part 3The Eighth Night

4On the eighth night, I was even more careful than usual when I opened the door. The minute hand of a watch moves faster than my hand did. Never before that night had I felt how powerful I was, how clever I was. I could hardly hide my feeling of victory. Just think: there I was, opening the door little by little, and the old man could not even dream of my secret actions or thoughts. I actually laughed quietly at the idea, and perhaps the old man heard me, because he suddenly moved in the bed, as if something had frightened him. Now you may think that I pulled back. But no. His room was pitch-black in the thick darkness, because the shutters were tightly closed for fear of robbers. So I knew that he could not see the door opening, and I kept pushing it, slowly and steadily.

5My head was inside, and I was about to open the lantern, when my thumb slipped on the metal lock, and the old man jumped up in bed, crying out, "Who's there?"

6I stayed completely still and said nothing. For another hour, I did not move a single muscle, and during that time I did not hear the old man lie down again. He was still sitting up in bed, listening, just as I have done, night after night, listening to the death-watch beetles in the wall.

7Soon I heard a soft groan, and I knew it was a groan of deadly fear. It was not a groan of pain or of sadness. Oh, no! It was the low, choked sound that rises from the bottom of the soul when it is too full of fear. I knew that sound well. Many nights, exactly at midnight, when the whole world was sleeping, that sound has risen from my own chest, and its terrible echo has made my own fears even deeper. I say I knew it well. I knew what the old man was feeling, and I felt sorry for him, although inside I was laughing. I knew that he had been lying awake since the first small noise, when he had turned in the bed. Since then, his fears had been growing. He had been trying to believe that there was no reason to be afraid, but he could not. He had been saying to himself, "It's nothing but the wind in the chimney. It's only a mouse running across the floor," or "It's just a cricket that made one little sound." Yes, he had been trying to comfort himself with these ideas, but it was all useless. All useless, because Death, coming closer to the old man, had walked toward him with its black shadow and covered its victim. And it was the sad power of that invisible shadow that made him feel (although he could not see or hear anything), made him feel that my head was in the room.

8After I had waited a long time, very patiently, without hearing him lie down, I decided to open the lantern a little, just a very, very small crack. So I opened it (you cannot imagine how secretly, how quietly) until, finally, a single dim ray, as thin as a spider's thread, shot out of the crack and fell directly on the vulture eye.

9It was open, wide, wide open, and I became furious as I stared at it. I saw it perfectly clearly: all a dull blue, with a horrible cloudy layer over it that made me cold to the center of my bones. But I could see nothing else of the old man's face or body, because I had pointed the ray, as if by instinct, exactly on that cursed spot.

10And now (haven't I told you that what you think is madness is only senses that are too sharp?), now, I say, I heard a low, dull, quick sound, like the sound a watch makes when it is wrapped in cotton. I knew that sound well, too. It was the beating of the old man's heart. It made my anger stronger, just as the beating of a drum makes a soldier feel brave.

11But I still controlled myself and stayed still. I hardly breathed. I held the lantern without moving it. I tested how steadily I could keep the ray on the eye. Meanwhile, the terrible drumbeat of the heart grew stronger. It grew quicker, and louder and louder every second. The old man's fear must have been extreme! It grew louder, I tell you, louder every moment! Are you listening carefully? I have told you that I am nervous, and I am. And now, in the dead middle of the night, in the terrible silence of that old house, this strange noise filled me with anger I could not control. But for some minutes more, I controlled myself and stayed still. But the beating grew louder, louder! I thought the heart must burst! And now a new worry took hold of me: a neighbor would hear the sound! The old man's time had come! With a loud scream, I threw open the lantern and jumped into the room. He screamed once, only once. In a moment, I pulled him to the floor and pulled the heavy bed on top of him. Then I smiled happily to see that the job was almost done. But for many minutes, the heart continued to beat with a soft, muffled sound. This did not worry me, however; it would not be heard through the walls. Finally, it stopped. The old man was dead. I moved the bed and examined the body. Yes, he was dead, stone dead. I put my hand on his heart and held it there for many minutes. There was no heartbeat. He was stone dead. His eye would not trouble me anymore.

12If you still think I am mad, you will not think so anymore when I describe the wise steps I took to hide the body. The night was ending, and I worked quickly but silently. First of all, I cut the body into pieces. I cut off the head, the arms, and the legs. Then I took up three boards from the floor of the room and put everything in the space between the wooden beams under the floor. Then I put the boards back so cleverly, so skillfully, that no human eye (not even his) could see anything wrong. There was nothing to wash: no stain of any kind, no spot of blood at all. I had been too careful for that. A tub had caught it all. Ha! Ha!

Stop and Think · Part 3
  1. What sound does the narrator hear? Do you think the sound is real?
  2. The narrator says the way he hid the body proves he is not mad. Does it?
Part 4The Visitors

13When I had finished this work, it was four o'clock, still as dark as midnight. As the clock rang four, someone knocked at the front door. I went down to open it with a light heart, because what did I have to fear now? Three men came in and introduced themselves, very politely, as police officers. A neighbor had heard a scream during the night. Someone suspected a crime. The neighbor had reported it at the police station, and the officers had been sent to search the house.

14I smiled, because what did I have to fear? I welcomed the gentlemen. The scream, I said, was my own, in a dream. The old man, I explained, was away in the country. I took my visitors all over the house. I told them to search, to search well. Finally, I led them to his bedroom. I showed them his valuable things, safe and untouched. In my excitement and confidence, I brought chairs into the room and invited them to rest there from their tiring work. And I myself, in the wild boldness of my perfect victory, put my own chair on the exact spot under which the victim's body lay.

15The officers were satisfied. My behavior had convinced them. I was unusually relaxed. They sat, and while I answered cheerfully, they chatted about everyday things. But before long, I felt myself becoming pale, and I wished they would leave. My head hurt, and I imagined a ringing in my ears. But they still sat, and they still chatted. The ringing became clearer. I talked more freely to get rid of the feeling, but it continued and became stronger and clearer, until, finally, I realized that the noise was not inside my ears.

16No doubt I now became very pale, but I talked more easily, and in a louder voice. Still, the sound grew. And what could I do? It was a low, dull, quick sound, very much like the sound a watch makes when it is wrapped in cotton. I fought for breath, and still the officers did not hear it. I talked more quickly, more passionately, but the noise kept growing. I stood up and argued about unimportant things, in a high voice and waving my arms wildly, but the noise kept growing. Why wouldn't they leave? I walked back and forth across the floor with heavy steps, as if the men's comments were making me furious, but the noise kept growing. Oh God! What could I do? I foamed at the mouth, I shouted wildly, I cursed! I swung the chair I had been sitting on and scraped it across the boards, but the noise rose above everything and kept growing. It grew louder, louder, louder! And still the men chatted pleasantly and smiled. Was it possible that they did not hear it? Almighty God! No, no! They heard it! They suspected! They knew! They were laughing at my horror! This is what I thought, and this is what I still think. But anything was better than this pain! Anything was easier to bear than this mockery! I could not stand those false smiles any longer! I felt that I must scream or die! And now, again! Listen! Louder! Louder! Louder! Louder!

17"Villains!" I screamed. "Stop pretending! I admit what I did! Tear up the boards! Here, here! It is the beating of his horrible heart!"

      Background Notes

      Life in the 1840s

      The story takes place in an old house in the 1840s. There was no electricity. People used candles and oil lamps for light, and they carried pocket watches that made a loud ticking sound.

      The dark lantern

      A dark lantern was a small metal lamp with a cover that could slide open and closed. When it was closed, no light came out at all. When it was open a little, it made a thin beam of light, like a small flashlight today.

      The Evil Eye

      Many cultures around the world believe that a certain kind of look can bring bad luck, illness, or harm. This is called the Evil Eye. Poe writes "Evil Eye" with capital letters to show how important and frightening it is to the narrator. Does your culture have a similar belief?

      Death-watch beetles

      These small insects live inside old wooden walls and make a quiet ticking or tapping sound. In the past, many people believed that hearing them meant someone in the house would die soon.

      The narrator

      We never learn the narrator's name, and the story never says if the narrator is a man or a woman. Most readers imagine a man. We also never learn exactly who "you" is: maybe a judge, a doctor, a police officer, or the reader.

      This is Poe's original text, first published in The Pioneer magazine in January 1843. It uses older English and some words that are rare today. The paragraph numbers match the adapted version, so you can compare any paragraph with the original.

      Original Text · 1843

      The Tell-Tale Heart

      Edgar Allan Poe

      Part 1The Eye

      1TRUE! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been, and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses — not destroyed — not dulled them. Above all was the sense of hearing acute. I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell. How, then, am I mad? Hearken! and observe how healthily — how calmly I can tell you the whole story.

      2It is impossible to say how first the idea entered my brain; but, once conceived, it haunted me day and night. Object there was none. Passion there was none. I loved the old man. He had never wronged me. He had never given me insult. For his gold I had no desire. I think it was his eye! — yes, it was this! He had the eye of a vulture — a pale blue eye, with a film over it. Whenever it fell upon me, my blood ran cold; and so, by degrees — very gradually — I made up my mind to take the life of the old man, and thus rid myself of the eye forever.

      Part 2Seven Nights

      3Now this is the point. You fancy me mad. Madmen know nothing. But you should have seen me. You should have seen how wisely I proceeded — with what caution — with what foresight — with what dissimulation I went to work! I was never kinder to the old man than during the whole week before I killed him. And every night, about midnight, I turned the latch of his door and opened it — oh so gently! And then, when I had made an opening sufficient for my head, I put in a dark lantern, all closed, closed, that no light shone out, and then I thrust in my head. Oh, you would have laughed to see how cunningly I thrust it in! I moved it slowly — very, very slowly, so that I might not disturb the old man's sleep. It took me an hour to place my whole head within the opening so far that I could see him as he lay upon his bed. Ha! — would a madman have been so wise as this? And then, when my head was well in the room, I undid the lantern cautiously — oh, so cautiously — cautiously (for the hinges creaked) — I undid it just so much that a single thin ray fell upon the vulture eye. And this I did for seven long nights — every night just at midnight — but I found the eye always closed; and so it was impossible to do the work; for it was not the old man who vexed me, but his Evil Eye. And every morning, when the day broke, I went boldly into his chamber, and spoke courageously to him, calling him by name in a hearty tone, and inquiring how he has passed the night. So you see he would have been a very profound old man, indeed, to suspect that every night, just at twelve, I looked in upon him while he slept.

      Part 3The Eighth Night

      4Upon the eighth night I was more than usually cautious in opening the door. A watch's minute hand moves more quickly than did mine. Never before that night had I felt the extent of my own powers — of my sagacity. I could scarcely contain my feelings of triumph. To think that there I was, opening the door, little by little, and the old man not even to dream of my secret deeds or thoughts. I fairly chuckled at the idea; and perhaps the old man heard me; for he moved in the bed suddenly, as if startled. Now you may think that I drew back — but no. His room was as black as pitch with the thick darkness, (for the shutters were close fastened, through fear of robbers,) and so I knew that he could not see the opening of the door, and I kept pushing it on steadily, steadily.

      5I had got my head in, and was about to open the lantern, when my thumb slipped upon the tin fastening, and the old man sprang up in bed, crying out — "Who's there?"

      6I kept quite still and said nothing. For another hour I did not move a muscle, and in the meantime I did not hear the old man lie down. He was still sitting up in the bed, listening; — just as I have done, night after night, hearkening to the death-watches in the wall.

      7Presently I heard a slight groan, and I knew it was the groan of mortal terror. It was not a groan of pain or of grief — oh, no! — it was the low stifled sound that arises from the bottom of the soul when overcharged with awe. I knew the sound well. Many a night, just at midnight, when all the world slept, it has welled up from my own bosom, deepening, with its dreadful echo, the terrors that distracted me. I say I knew it well. I knew what the old man felt, and pitied him, although I chuckled at heart. I knew that he had been lying awake ever since the first slight noise, when he had turned in the bed. His fears had been, ever since, growing upon him. He had been trying to fancy them causeless, but could not. He had been saying to himself — "It is nothing but the wind in the chimney — it is only a mouse crossing the floor," or "It is merely a cricket which has made a single chirp." Yes, he had been trying to comfort himself with these suppositions; but he had found all in vain. All in vain: because death, in approaching the old man, had stalked with his black shadow before him, and enveloped the victim. And it was the mournful influence of the unperceived shadow that caused him to feel — although he neither saw nor heard — to feel the presence of my head within the room.

      8When I had waited a long time, very patiently, without hearing him lie down, I resolved to open a little — a very, very little crevice in the lantern. So I opened it — you cannot imagine how stealthily, stealthily — until, at length a simple dim ray, like the thread of the spider, shot from out the crevice and fell full upon the vulture eye.

      9It was open — wide, wide open — and I grew furious as I gazed upon it. I saw it with perfect distinctness — all a dull blue, with a hideous veil over it that chilled the very marrow in my bones; but I could see nothing else of the old man's face or person; for I had directed the ray as if by instinct, precisely upon the damned spot.

      10And now — have I not told you that what you mistake for madness is but over-acuteness of the senses? — now, I say, there came to my ears a low, dull, quick sound, such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton. I knew that sound well, too. It was the beating of the old man's heart. It increased my fury, as the beating of a drum stimulates the soldier into courage.

      11But even yet I refrained and kept still. I scarcely breathed. I held the lantern motionless. I tried how steadily I could maintain the ray upon the eye. Meantime the hellish tattoo of the heart increased. It grew quicker, and louder and louder every instant. The old man's terror must have been extreme! It grew louder, I say, louder every moment: — do you mark me well? I have told you that I am nervous: — so I am. And now, at the dead hour of the night, and amid the dreadful silence of that old house, so strange a noise as this excited me to uncontrollable wrath. Yet, for some minutes longer, I refrained and kept still. But the beating grew louder, louder! I thought the heart must burst! And now a new anxiety seized me — the sound would be heard by a neighbor! The old man's hour had come! With a loud yell, I threw open the lantern and leaped into the room. He shrieked once — once only. In an instant I dragged him to the floor, and pulled the heavy bed over him. I then smiled gaily, to find the deed so far done. But, for many minutes, the heart beat on with a muffled sound. This, however, did not vex me; it would not be heard through the walls. At length it ceased. The old man was dead. I removed the bed and examined the corpse. Yes, he was stone, stone dead. I placed my hand upon the heart and held it there many minutes. There was no pulsation. The old man was stone dead. His eye would trouble me no more.

      12If, still, you think me mad, you will think so no longer when I describe the wise precautions I took for the concealment of the body. The night waned, and I worked hastily, but in silence. First of all I dismembered the corpse. I cut off the head and the arms and the legs. I then took up three planks from the flooring of the chamber, and deposited all between the scantlings. I then replaced the boards so cleverly, so cunningly, that no human eye — not even his — could have detected anything wrong. There was nothing to wash out — no stain of any kind — no blood-spot whatever. I had been too wary for that. A tub had caught all — ha! ha!

      Part 4The Visitors

      13When I had made an end of these labors, it was four o'clock — still dark as midnight. As the bell sounded the hour, there came a knocking at the street door. I went down to open it with a light heart, — for what had I now to fear? There entered three men, who introduced themselves, with perfect suavity, as officers of the police. A shriek had been heard by a neighbour during the night; suspicion of foul play had been aroused; information had been lodged at the police-office, and they (the officers) had been deputed to search the premises.

      14I smiled, — for what had I to fear? I bade the gentlemen welcome. The shriek, I said, was my own in a dream. The old man, I mentioned, was absent in the country. I took my visitors all over the house. I bade them search — search well. I led them, at length, to his chamber. I showed them his treasures, secure, undisturbed. In the enthusiasm of my confidence, I brought chairs into the room, and desired them here to rest from their fatigues, while I myself, in the wild audacity of my perfect triumph, placed my own seat upon the very spot beneath which reposed the corpse of the victim.

      15The officers were satisfied. My manner had convinced them. I was singularly at ease. They sat, and while I answered cheerily, they chatted of familiar things. But, ere long, I felt myself getting pale and wished them gone. My head ached, and I fancied a ringing in my ears: but still they sat and still chatted. The ringing became more distinct: I talked more freely to get rid of the feeling; but it continued and gained definiteness — until, at length, I found that the noise was not within my ears.

      16No doubt I now grew very pale; — but I talked more fluently, and with a heightened voice. Yet the sound increased — and what could I do? It was a low, dull, quick sound — much such a sound as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton. I gasped for breath — and yet the officers heard it not. I talked more quickly — more vehemently; — but the noise steadily increased. I arose and argued about trifles, in a high key and with violent gesticulations; — but the noise steadily increased. Why would they not be gone? I paced the floor to and fro, with heavy strides, as if excited to fury by the observations of the men — but the noise steadily increased. Oh God! what could I do? I foamed — I raved — I swore! I swung the chair upon which I had sat, and grated it upon the boards; — but the noise arose over all and continually increased. It grew louder — louder — louder! And still the men chatted pleasantly, and smiled. Was it possible they heard not? Almighty God! — no, no! They heard! — they suspected! — they knew! — they were making a mockery of my horror! — this I thought, and this I think. But anything better than this agony! Anything was more tolerable than this derision! I could bear those hypocritical smiles no longer! I felt that I must scream or die! — and now — again! — hark! louder! louder! louder! louder! —

      17"Villains!" I shrieked, "dissemble no more! I admit the deed! — tear up the planks! — here, here! — it is the beating of his hideous heart!"
