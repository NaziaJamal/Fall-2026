Part D: Close Reading 8 points · 2 per question

1. A sentence that shows the police are not worried

Accept any of these:

  • "The officers were satisfied."
  • "My behavior had convinced them."
  • "They sat, and while I answered cheerfully, they chatted about everyday things."
  • "But they still sat, and they still chatted."
  • "And still the men chatted pleasantly and smiled."

2 points: a correct sentence, copied accurately. 1 point: a correct sentence with copying mistakes, only part of the sentence, or the right idea in the student's own words.

2. A sentence that shows he is not relaxed

Accept any of these (or other clear examples):

  • "But before long, I felt myself becoming pale, and I wished they would leave."
  • "My head hurt, and I imagined a ringing in my ears."
  • "No doubt I now became very pale, but I talked more easily, and in a louder voice."
  • "Almighty God! No, no!"
  • "They were laughing at my horror!"

2 points: a correct sentence, copied accurately. 1 point: a correct sentence with copying mistakes, only part of the sentence, or the right idea in the student's own words.

3. Why Poe repeats "louder, louder, louder!"

Key idea: the repetition shows the sound growing and the narrator's panic growing. It builds suspense, so the reader feels nervous, scared, or tense.

2 points: mentions the narrator's growing panic or fear and/or the effect on the reader (suspense, tension, fear). 1 point: only "to show the sound is very loud." 0: no answer or unrelated.

4. What "unreliable narrator" means

Key idea: a narrator we cannot completely believe; what he says and what he does (or shows) do not match.

2 points: the full idea, for example, "We can't trust him, because he says he is calm, but he is not." 1 point: a partial idea, for example, "He lies" or "He is crazy," with no link to believing or trusting the storyteller.

Fair marking at this level: grade the ideas and the use of evidence more than grammar. A paragraph with simple sentences but a clear opinion, a correct quotation, and a "This shows that..." explanation should score 12 or more out of 16. All three questions are equally valid; any opinion is acceptable if it is supported.

Model Answers for Part E

Written at about A2+/B1 level, so they show students what a realistic full-mark answer looks like.

Question 1: Is the narrator reliable?

I think the narrator of "The Tell-Tale Heart" is not reliable. He wants us to believe that he is sane. For example, he asks, "So how can I be mad?" (¶1). However, his actions show the opposite. He kills the old man only because of his eye, but he also says, "I loved the old man" (¶2). A normal person does not kill someone he loves for this reason. Also, after he hides the body, he laughs: "A tub had caught it all. Ha! Ha!" (¶12). This shows that he enjoys his terrible crime. For these reasons, we cannot believe what the narrator tells us.

Question 2: Prison or hospital?

I think the narrator should go to a hospital, not to prison, because he is mentally ill. First, he has no real reason to kill the old man. He says, "I loved the old man. He had never done anything wrong to me." (¶2). He kills him only because of his eye. Second, he hears things that are not real. At the end, he screams, "It is the beating of his horrible heart!" (¶17). This shows that he hears a dead man's heart, and that is impossible. However, he is also dangerous, so the hospital must keep him inside. In conclusion, he needs doctors more than a prison.

Question 3: Whose heart does he hear?

I think the narrator hears his own heart, not the old man's heart. The old man is dead, and his body is under the floor, so his heart cannot beat. The narrator is very nervous when the police are in the room. He says, "I have told you that I am nervous, and I am." (¶11). When a person is nervous and afraid, his heart beats fast and loud. Also, the police "chatted pleasantly and smiled" (¶16), so they did not hear any sound. This shows that the sound is only in the narrator's body and mind. In conclusion, his own heart tells his secret, and that is why the story is called "The Tell-Tale Heart."

Students who argue that it is the old man's heart can also score full marks, for example by quoting ¶17 ("It is the beating of his horrible heart!") and explaining that the narrator believes it is real.
