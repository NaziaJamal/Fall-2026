The First Five Seconds Movement

Step 1 · Warm-up · 15 min · Worksheet A1

Stand up. Walk around the room. In each round, greet two people and say:

"Hi! I have a story for you."

Round 1

Eyes on your phone. Look at your phone or the floor while you greet.

Then rate: how much did you trust them? (1–5)

Round 2

Eyes over their head. Look just above their head, at the wall behind them.

Rate again (1–5).

Round 3

The Connector. Stop. Two seconds of eye contact. Nod. A real smile. Then speak.

Rate again (1–5).

Round 4 · The "Try not to smile" challenge (pairs)

  • Partner A keeps a straight face. No smiling!
  • Partner B has 30 seconds to describe the best meal of their life, with a big, real smile and lots of eye contact.
  • Swap. Who smiled first?

Talk about it

  1. In which round did you trust the other person most? Why?
  2. What did you notice about the other person in the first five seconds?
  3. Why was it so hard not to smile?
  4. Predict: what will Chris Anderson tell speakers to do before their first sentence?

Story Stations Movement

Step 3 · Guided Reading · 25 min · Worksheet B2

There are five posters around the room (A–E). Your group spends 5 minutes at each station. When the timer rings, move to the next letter. After E, go to A.

Group roles: change roles at every station

RoleJob
ReaderReads the poster aloud (or shares the paragraphs with the group).
WriterWrites the group's answers in Worksheet B2.
TimekeeperWatches the timer. Says "Two minutes left!"
ReporterShares one answer with the class at the end.

The five stations

  • A · Eye Contact, Right from the Start: why the first seconds matter
  • B · Around the Campfire: why humans love stories
  • C · The Story Formula + Four Key Things: what makes a story work
  • D · Two Ways to Tell It: a great version and a messy version
  • E · Let It Mean Something: personal stories, truth, and parables
Useful language: "Let's read it together." · "What does ___ mean?" · "I think the answer is ___ because ___." · "Let's move on; we have one minute."
4

Story Rescue

Step 3 · after the stations · 7 min · Worksheet B3 · one card per group

Rescue it! 3 minutes to plan, then tell it in 45 seconds. ① Don't give away the point at the start. ② Cut three details we don't need. ③ Add one feeling and one thing we can see or hear. ④ Find a key line (something someone said). ⑤ End with a funny, moving, or revealing line. You can change small facts, but keep it believable.

1 · The Driving Test

This story is about never giving up. I took my driving test three times. The first time was on March 3, 2021, at the test center on Elm Street. The car was a gray 2015 Toyota Corolla, and the examiner was about 55 years old. I failed because of parking. The second time, I failed because of a stop sign. The third time, I passed. My instructor's name was Mr. Kim, and he charged $60 an hour. So I learned never to give up.

2 · The Lost Dog

I learned that neighbors are important when my dog got lost and a neighbor found him. My dog is a three-year-old beagle mix named Max. He weighs 25 pounds, and we adopted him from a shelter in 2020. One day in July, he ran out of the gate because the lock was broken. It was a cheap lock from a hardware store. We looked for him for six hours. Then a neighbor I had never talked to found him. Now we talk.

3 · The First Class

My first English class in the US was on a Monday at 9:00 a.m. in Room 204. There were 16 students from nine countries. The teacher asked me a question, and I didn't understand it, so I said "Yes." But the question was "What's your name?" Everyone laughed. It was embarrassing. Now my English is better. The lesson is that mistakes are OK.

4 · The Wrong Party

I learned to always check the address when I went to the wrong party. My friend's party was at 15 Oak Street, but I went to 51 Oak Street. I brought a chocolate cake that I bought at a supermarket for $19.99. The people at 51 were having a party too, for a 70-year-old man. I stayed for an hour before I realized. Then I went to the right party.

Human Timeline Movement

Step 5 · Language Focus · 8 min · Worksheet D4 · cut out; one card per student

Round 1 · Time order. Read your card. Without showing it, find your group and line up in the order the events happened. Tell the story: each person says one sentence in the past simple.
Round 2 · Story order. The ★ person steps to the front. Storytellers often start at the most exciting moment. Re-line: ★ first, then go back in time, then the ending. Say your sentence again with the right tense and a time phrase (I had…, I was …ing, The night before, …, Then…).

Cut the Fat + Campfire Circles Movement

Step 6 · Speaking · 35 min · Worksheet E

Cut the Fat (two lines)

  1. Stand in two lines facing a partner.
  2. Before you speak: look at your partner, pause, smile. Then begin.
  3. Round 1: 90 sec · Round 2: 75 sec · Round 3: 60 sec. Same story, a new partner each time (one line moves left).
  4. Each round, the listener gives one comment from the scorecard below.

Campfire Circles

  1. Groups of 4 sit in a circle around the "fire."
  2. Each person tells their best version (60–75 sec).
  3. Listeners score with 1–3 fingers for each line of the scorecard.
  4. Choose your Campfire Champion to tell the whole class.

Model story map · "The Umbrella"

Step 7 · Build Your Talk · Worksheet F · a model for your 2-minute story

The story (about 2 minutes)

time phrasepast continuouspast perfectkey line

Two years ago, I moved to Boston for a new job. I didn't know anyone, and I had never taken a city bus at night. One rainy Friday, I finished work late. It was almost eleven. I was standing at the bus stop, and the rain was coming down so hard that my shoes were full of water. Then I saw it: my bus. The last bus. I stepped forward and raised my hand. And it drove right past me.

[pause] I just stood there. I was cold, I was tired, and I was thinking, "Nobody in this city even sees me."

Then I heard a voice behind me. "Do you want to share?" An old man was holding a big black umbrella. His name was Walter. He had been waiting for the same bus. We started walking together, and after twenty minutes, we discovered something funny: Walter lived on my street. Three houses away.

Since then, Walter and I have had coffee every Sunday. He tells me stories about the city, and I tell him stories about my country.

That night, I learned something. A city doesn't start to feel like home when you know the streets. It starts to feel like home when one person shares an umbrella.
