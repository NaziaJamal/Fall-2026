Steps (35 min)

  1. Predict (3 min). Show the subtitle "The Gift in Every Great Talk." Pairs: what gift can a talk give?
  2. Expert groups (10 min). Groups A, B and C read their card and complete the top half of page 5. Check every expert can explain the message without reading.
  3. Teaching groups (10 min). New groups of three (one A, one B, one C). Each expert teaches for 3 minutes; the others write notes in the bottom half of page 5. The group writes one summary sentence.
  4. Check (4 min). Worksheet B4 true/false. Go back to the Sophie predictions from the warm-up.
  5. Find your idea (8 min). Students answer two questions from the box on Card C with a partner.

A

Sophie's gift

Reading card · Expert group A

B2+

The talk. In 2015, the laughter scientist Sophie Scott spoke at TED. She played recordings of people laughing, and the audience was soon laughing too. Laughter, she showed, is a strange sound: more like an animal call than speech.

The gift. The talk was fun, but for Anderson the real value came later. Her core idea (that laughter helps people turn social stress into a feeling of connection) stayed in his mind. Now, whenever he sees people laughing together, he understands it differently. That, he says, is what a great talk gives you: not just a good time, but an idea you keep.

Anderson's words"Your number-one mission as a speaker is to take something that matters deeply to you and to rebuild it inside the minds of your listeners."

His own example. Anderson describes the scariest talk of his life: he was taking over TED from its founder and had 15 minutes to win the audience's support. The talk wasn't perfect, but it worked because it gave people one clear idea: TED mattered because it brought people from every field together to understand each other, so it should belong to the public, not to one person. With that idea in their minds, the audience stopped worrying about the change in leadership.

The lesson: a talk succeeds when the audience leaves with one idea they can hold on to, value, and be changed by.

Word help
howling with laughter: laughing very loudly
phenomenon: something that happens or exists
social bonding: feeling close to other people
founder: the person who started an organization
field / discipline: an area of work or study
nonprofit: an organization that doesn't try to make money

Adapted from Chris Anderson, TED Talks (2016), Ch. 2.

B

What is an idea?

Reading card · Expert group B

B2

The main message of the book: if you have something worth saying, you can give a powerful talk. Confidence and smooth speaking matter much less than having a real idea.

What counts as an idea? Anderson uses the word very broadly. It doesn't have to be a big discovery or invention. It could be:

① a simple how-to
② an insight told through a story
③ an image with meaning
④ a hope for the future
⑤ a reminder of what matters in life

Anderson's words"An idea is anything that can change how people see the world."

Putting a strong idea into someone's mind is a valuable gift; a little of you becomes part of them.

Are your ideas good enough? Anderson says people judge this badly. Some speakers (often men, in his view) talk a lot but say little of value. Many others (often women, he says) think their work and experience are less important than they really are.

A warning: if you just want to be a star on stage, work on something worth sharing first. Style without substance is awful.

The good news: most people have more to share than they think. Your life and experiences are unique. The job is to find the parts that are worth sharing.

Word help
stage presence: looking confident on stage
insight: a clear, deep understanding
underestimate: think something is less important than it is
charisma: personal attraction that makes people follow you
substance: real, important content
work out: find the answer

Adapted from Chris Anderson, TED Talks (2016), Ch. 2.

C

"I have nothing to say"

Reading card · Expert group C

B2

The worry. Many people need to give a talk (for a class, at work, or for a community group) and feel they have nothing interesting to say. They think they aren't creative, clever, or passionate enough.

Anderson's honest answer. A talk does need some depth. For some people, the best choice is to keep exploring life and come back to speaking later.

But first, check yourself. Maybe the real problem is low confidence. Anderson points out a paradox: you can only see yourself from the inside, so you may miss what is special about you.

Anderson's words"The bits that others find remarkable in you may be completely invisible to you."

What to do. Ask people who know you well what they find interesting about you. And remember that your experience of life is completely your own; nobody else has lived your days. Many great talks are just a personal story with a simple lesson, so start noticing moments that surprise you.

Stories work. People love stories, and anyone can learn to tell one. A familiar lesson is fine, because people need reminding. An important idea in a fresh story can make a great talk.

Questions to help you find your idea • What stands out in your work or life in the last few years?
• What recently excited you, or made you angry?
• What are you most proud of?
• When did someone last tell you, "That's really interesting"?
• If you could spread one idea to everyone, what would it be?

Adapted from Chris Anderson, TED Talks (2016), Ch. 2.
