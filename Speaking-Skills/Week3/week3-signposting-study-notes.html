Signposting · Study Notes

Week 3 · Traps and Throughline · How to help your audience follow your talk

1What is signposting?

Signposts are short words and phrases that tell the audience where you are in your talk and where you are going next, like road signs on a highway.

Without signpostsCooking saves money. You make friends. It feels like home.
With signpostsFirst, cooking saves money. Second, you make friends. And finally, it feels like home.
Why it matters: a reader can look back at a page; a listener can't. Signposts are how listeners stay on your throughline. Without them, even a good talk can sound like a ramble.

2A model: how Matt Cutts signposts his talk

His talk is only about 3.5 minutes, but you always know where you are:

Main idea"The idea is actually pretty simple."
→
Preview"There's a few things I learned…"
→
Point 1"The first was, …"
→
Points 2–3"I also noticed…" / "I also figured out…"
→
Last point"So here's one last thing I'd like to mention."
→
Ending"So here's my question to you: …"

His throughline: trying something new for 30 days is a simple way to change your life.

4Tips for spoken signposts

  • Preview, then follow it. If you say "three things", give three, and number them.
  • Pause after a signpost so the audience can catch it: "Second, | it helps you make friends."
  • Keep them short and natural. Spoken signposts are simpler than written ones. "So, …" and "Now, …" are fine in a talk.
  • Don't overdo it. Every sentence doesn't need a signpost; use them at the turns of your talk.
  • Mix them. Not "and then… and then… and then…".
