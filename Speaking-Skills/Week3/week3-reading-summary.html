Week 3 · Reading Summary

Chris Anderson, TED Talks · Ch. 3 "Common Traps" + Ch. 4 "The Checklist"

The big idea

A talk should give something to the audience, not take from them. Avoid four common traps, then test your throughline with a checklist.

Part 1 · Four traps to avoid Ch. 3

1

The Sales Pitch

WhatThe speaker uses the talk to sell something: a service, a book, a company.
ExampleA famous consultant promised a talk on creative thinking, but only listed companies that succeeded after hiring him. Anderson interrupted him on stage, and the talk was never posted online.
Why badThe audience feels used, and the speaker loses respect. Even small "ads" (a book cover on a slide) are risky.
InsteadGive, don't take. Generosity gets a better response: Bryan Stevenson never asked for money in his talk, and the audience gave over $1.3 million.

"Give, don't take."

2

The Ramble

WhatAn unprepared talk that wanders with no clear direction.
ExampleA speaker began by saying he had thought about what to say on his drive to the event. He listed random thoughts about the future. People clapped politely but learned nothing.
Why badIt tells the audience their time doesn't matter. Their time and attention are a precious gift to the speaker.
InsteadPrepare. If you want to give people an idea, you have to put in the preparation time first.

"Rambling is not an option."

3

The Org Bore

WhatA talk about your organization: its history, structure, team and products.
Example"In 2005 we opened a new department in Dallas, and I put a vice president in charge…" (boring) vs. "In 2005 we discovered an office can cut its energy costs by 60%. Here's how…" (interesting).
Why badYour organization is fascinating to you and your team, but boring to almost everyone else.
InsteadTalk about the work and the ideas behind it, not the organization itself.

"One mode is a gift. The other is lazily self-serving."

4

The Inspiration Performance

WhatA speaker tries hard to look inspiring, with big emotion, jokes and applause, but with little real content.
ExampleA TED fan copied the "best bits" of famous talks, waited for applause, used weak evidence and even an edited photo, and ran over time. Some people stood up, but later the audience said they felt manipulated.
Why badInspiration can't be faked. When people notice, they pull back, like when someone chases love too hard.
InsteadEarn it. Do real, meaningful work, then share what you learned with humility.

"Inspiration can't be performed."

Part 2 · Test your throughline Ch. 4, "The Checklist"

Before you build your talk, ask yourself:

  1. Do I really care about this topic?
  2. Will it make people curious?
  3. Will knowing this make a difference to my audience?
  4. Is my talk a gift or an ask?
  5. Is the information new, or has everyone heard it before?
  6. Can I explain it well, with examples, in the time I have?
  7. Do I know enough to make it worth the audience's time?
  8. Am I the right person to talk about this?
  9. What are the 15 words that sum up my talk?
  10. Would those 15 words make someone want to hear my talk?

Two tips from the experts

Say it out loud

Speaking coach Abigail Tenembaum suggests testing your throughline on a typical audience member by saying it, not writing it. Speaking it shows you what is clear, what is missing, and how to make it sharper.

Talk to one person

Author Elizabeth Gilbert suggests preparing your talk for one real person you like: curious and smart, but not an expert in your field. And choose a topic you truly know and love, not one you picked just because it sounds new.

What comes next

Once you have a throughline, you build your talk with five tools. You can use one, several, or all five:

ConnectionNarrationExplanationPersuasionRevelation

Word help

trap: a mistake that's easy to fall into
pitch: words used to sell something
takeaway: a useful idea you remember
ramble: talk for a long time without a clear point
wing it: do something without preparing
self-serving: helping only yourself
manipulate: control people in an unfair way
earn: get something because you deserve it
throughline: the one idea that connects the whole talk

Think about your talk

1. Which trap do you think is the easiest for you to fall into? Why?

2. Write your idea in 15 words or fewer. Is it a gift or an ask?
