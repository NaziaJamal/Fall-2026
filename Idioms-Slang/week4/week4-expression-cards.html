EXPRESSION 1 / 5

The story

Door 1A · Rosa. Rosa and her friend Mei were best friends in high school. They talked every day. Then Mei moved to Chicago for work, and they both got busy. Now they only send a message on birthdays. There was no fight. They just grew apart.

grow apart

What do you think grow apart means? Talk with your partner. Then tap Reveal.

Meaning

to slowly stop being close to someone, usually without a fight

Source: Oxford Advanced American Dictionary: “to gradually lose a close connection”; Merriam-Webster

Examples

  • We were close in college, but we grew apart.
  • Since he moved, he's grown apart from his old friends.
  • It's normal for friends to grow apart sometimes.

How to use it

  • ✓ We grew apart. (no object)
  • ✓ grow apart from someone: She grew apart from her friends.
  • ✗ We grew up apart. (different meaning!)
  • ✓ past: grow → grew → grown

✗ We grew up apart after high school.
✓ We grew apart after high school.

grow up = become an adult. grow apart = stop being close. Don't mix them up!

Mini-dialogue

A: Do you still talk to your best friend from school?
B: Not really. We grew apart after college.
A: Did you have a fight?
B: No, nothing like that. We just got busy.

get along (with)

What do you think get along means? Talk with your partner. Then tap Reveal.

Meaning

to have a friendly, easy relationship with someone

Source: Oxford Advanced American Dictionary: “to have a friendly relationship with somebody”

Examples

  • I get along with everyone on my team.
  • My kids don't always get along.
  • Do you get along with your neighbors?

How to use it

  • ✓ get along with someone
  • ✗ I get along my boss.
  • ✓ We get along well / great / really well.
  • ✓ past: got along · present perfect: have gotten along

✗ I don't get along my roommate.
✓ I don't get along with my roommate.

US: get along is the everyday American choice. A great sentence for job interviews: “I get along well with my coworkers.”

EXPRESSION 3 / 5

The story

Door 3C · Lucy. Lucy made a dinner plan with a new friend from her yoga class: Friday, 7:00, at a Thai restaurant. Lucy waited for an hour. Her friend never came and never texted. Her friend stood her up. Lucy got stood up!

stand someone up

What do you think stand someone up means? Talk with your partner. Then tap Reveal.

Meaning

to not come to a meeting or a date you planned with someone (often without telling them)

Source: Oxford Advanced American Dictionary; Britannica Dictionary (both: informal)

Examples

  • My date stood me up last night.
  • I got stood up! I waited for an hour.
  • I'm so sorry. I didn't mean to stand you up.

How to use it

  • ✓ stand someone up: She stood me up.
  • ✗ She stood up me.
  • ✓ Passive (very common): I got stood up. · I was stood up.
  • ✓ past: stand → stood → stood

✗ He stood up me at the restaurant.
✓ He stood me up at the restaurant.

It's informal, and it often sounds like the person didn't care. For work, say: “Sorry, I missed the meeting.”

Mini-dialogue

A: How was your date on Saturday?
B: There was no date. She stood me up.
A: No way! She didn't come?
B: Nope. I got stood up. I waited for forty-five minutes!

Say it naturally

stood me UP · got stood UP → “stood-me-UP · gat-stoo-DUP”

Stress the small word. Link the words together. In American English, a t between vowels sounds like a soft d.

EXPRESSION 5 / 5

The story

Door 5E · Ana & Sofia. Ana and her sister Sofia had a big argument about a family party. They didn't talk for two months. Last Sunday, Ana called Sofia and said, “I'm sorry. I miss you.” They talked for an hour. They patched things up.

patch things up

What do you think patch things up means? Talk with your partner. Then tap Reveal.

Meaning

to become friends again after an argument; to fix a relationship

Source: Oxford Advanced American Dictionary: “to settle an argument… and become friends again”; Merriam-Webster

Examples

  • They had a fight, but they patched things up.
  • Have you tried patching things up with her?
  • I want to patch things up with my brother.

How to use it

  • ✓ patch things up with someone
  • ✓ patch it up · patch up your differences
  • ✗ patched up things up
  • ✓ past: patched (t sound: patcht)

✗ I want to patch things up my brother.
✓ I want to patch things up with my brother.

A patch is a small piece that fixes a hole in clothes. When you patch things up, you fix the “hole” in a relationship.

Mini-dialogue

A: Are you and Carlos still not talking?
B: No, we patched things up last weekend.
A: Oh good! What happened?
B: I called him and said sorry. We got coffee and talked for two hours.

Say it naturally

patch things UP → “patch-thing-ZUP”

Stress the small word. Link the words together. In American English, a t between vowels sounds like a soft d.

GRAMMAR CARD 1

stand someone up · got stood up

The person goes in the middle

  • ✓ She stood me up.
  • ✓ He stood his date up.
  • ✗ She stood up me.

Same rule as Week 3: figure it out · pick her up

Passive: very common!

  • ✓ I got stood up.
  • ✓ I was stood up.
  • ✓ She got stood up by her date.

We use the passive when the important person is the one who waited.

Don't forget “with”

  • ✓ get along with my boss
  • ✓ patch things up with my sister
  • ✓ grow apart from old friends
  • ✗ I get along my boss.
GRAMMAR CARD 2

American or British?

United States

We get along well.

The everyday American choice. Use it at work, with friends, and in interviews.

United Kingdom

We get on well.

Americans understand it, but it sounds British.

On the midterm and final

Both are correct. In conversation in the U.S., get along sounds more natural.

GRAMMAR CARD 3

Similar, but different

hit it off vs. get along

hit it off (Week 1) = like each other right away.

get along = a good relationship over time.

“We hit it off on the first day, and we've gotten along ever since.”

work it out vs. patch things up

work it out (Week 3) = solve a problem between people.

patch things up = become friends again after an argument.

“They worked out a cleaning schedule and patched things up.”

grow apart vs. break up

grow apart = slowly, usually no fight.

break up = end a romantic relationship (often suddenly).

For this class, focus on grow apart.
