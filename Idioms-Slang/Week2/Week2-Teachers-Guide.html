1 Week 2 at a glance

Topic

Phrasal Verbs I: Movement & Direction. Lesson theme: "Out & About", real errands, rides and chance meetings.

Target expressions (6)

pick up · drop off · turn around · run into · head out · come back

Language focus

Meaning is not literal · 3 word-order types (separable / inseparable / no object) · past tense (ran, came) · particle stress & linking

SLO (syllabus, A2+)

Explain that phrasal verb meaning is not literal, and use movement phrasal verbs (pick up, drop off, run into, head out) correctly.

Recycle (Week 1)

break the ice · make small talk · kill time · hit it off · shoot the breeze (recognition) · chat

Assessment link

Midterm (Wk 6): all six eligible; Midterm item 4 uses heads out; Speaking Card 10 "Leaving and Coming Back." Final (Wk 11): head out is a core expression. It gets extra speaking time today.
Expected outcome: By the end of class, students can tell a 2–3 minute story or plan about errands, rides or a chance meeting, using 2–3 of today's verbs with correct pronoun placement (pick her up, drop it off, ran into him) and choosing head out (not slang) to leave politely.
Why this concept? Movement phrasal verbs live in errands and logistics: rides, packages, appointments. That gives students a real reason to talk (negotiate a time, solve a problem). They don't just insert verbs into sentences. It also sets up Week 3: once students feel the "pronoun in the middle" rule with concrete verbs (pick it up), figure it out will feel familiar.

3 Step-by-step procedure

0:05 · 15 min Warm-up & Spiral Review

① Phrase Journal share (8 min). This is the first share-out of the course, so model it. Share one you heard ("My coworker said I gotta run at 5 p.m. Casual."). Pairs share 1 entry each: expression, where, meaning, register. Collect 3–4 good ones on the board. No journal? They tell their partner one English phrase they noticed anywhere this week.

② Week 1 Rescue (7 min, slide 5). Read each situation; pairs whisper, then call out the expression.

  1. Silence in the elevator with a new neighbor… you say something about the weather. → break the ice / make small talk
  2. Your train is 40 minutes late. You play a game on your phone. → kill time
  3. You meet someone at a party and talk for 2 hours. You're friends now. → hit it off
  4. Two old men on a bench talking about nothing important. → shoot the breeze / chat (remind: a little old-fashioned; chat is the everyday word)
  5. B1: Change one situation so a different expression fits.

0:20 · 10 min Engage: Literal or Real?

Slide 6 shows: "I ran into my boss at the supermarket." Students vote with fingers: 1 = I crashed into her with my cart 🛒💥 / 2 = I saw her by chance. Laugh about option 1, confirm 2. Repeat with "Can you drop me off?" (1 = drop me on the floor / 2 = let me out of the car).

"In English, these two-word verbs often have a hidden meaning. Today you're going to learn six that you hear every single day: in the car, at the store, at work."

Slide 7, "What would you text?": Your friend's car is broken. She needs to get to work at 8. Pairs write the text they'd send (any English). Collect 2–3 answers; if someone says "pick you up," celebrate it. If not, keep the situation in mind; you'll come back to it.

0:30 · 10 min Context: Lina's Busy Saturday

  1. Read the story aloud (slide 8) while students follow on their phones (Worksheet Part 1). Gist question: Was Lina's morning easy or stressful? Why?
  2. Students find and write the 6 movement verbs (+ the 2 Week 1 expressions as a bonus).
  3. Pairs retell Lina's morning without looking, using the verbs as a timeline: "First she dropped off Omar… then she headed out…"
A2+: retell with the 6 verbs written on the board in order.
B1: retell in the past and add one detail Lina might say to Dev.

0:40 · 15 min Expression Cards: Situation → Meaning → Notice

For each card (≈2 min): read the situation → students guess with a partner → Reveal → choral drill the pronunciation (stress the small word: pick UP, drop OFF, head OUT) → students read the mini-dialogue in pairs → point to the ✘ pattern.

Spend extra time on: head out (final-exam core expression; teach "I'm going to head out" as the polite way to leave work or a party) and run into (students confuse it with "crash").

Concept-check questions: If I run into you, did we plan to meet? (No.) If I drop you off, do I stay with you? (No.) Pick up and drop off — same or opposite? (Opposite.) Turn around — do you go forward or back? (Back.)

A2+: focus on meaning + 1 pattern per card.
B1: read the "B1" box: second meanings (nobody picked up, business dropped off, ran into traffic).

0:55 · 10 min Grammar Cards: Phrasal Verb Patterns

  1. Discovery first: Write on the board pick up her / pick her up and ran into her / ran her into. "Which one sounds right?" Let them vote, then show G2 and G3.
  2. G2 "Say it with a pronoun" as a quick choral round. The 🥪 sandwich image helps: the small pronoun goes in the middle.
  3. G3: show why ran him into sounds like a car crash (that pattern means crash something into something).
  4. G4 (no object): highlight come back home (no "to") and head out to + place.
  5. G5/G6 briefly: ran, came (irregular); headed = 2 syllables. Linking: "pi-kup," "hea-dout."
Syllabus link: this is the first contact with the separable/inseparable rule that becomes the core of Week 3 (figure it out, not figure out it). Tell students: "Next week you'll use this rule with new verbs."

1:05 · 35 min Controlled Practice I (Worksheet Parts 2–5)

Mixed-level pairs (pair a stronger B1 with an A2+). Phones: students use ✔ Check after each part. Paper: check together on slides 12.

  • Part 2 Match (5 min) · Part 3 Literal or Real? (7 min; B1 items 5–7) · Part 4 Fill in (10 min; watch ran / came / dropped) · Part 5 Where does it go? (8 min)
  • Whole-class check (5 min). Ask "why?" for Part 5 #4 and #5 (both correct with a noun).

Fast finishers: write 2 new "Literal or Real?" items for the class.

Block 2 · 0:00 · 15 min Controlled Practice II: Pronoun Race + Find & Fix

Pronoun Race (7 min, slide 14). Two or three teams. You show a sentence with a noun ("I'll pick up the kids at 3"); first team to say it with a pronoun ("I'll pick them up at 3") scores. Include traps: "I ran into my neighbor" → "I ran into her" (no split!), "We came back home" (no pronoun possible). 10 rounds:

  1. pick up the kids → pick them up
  2. drop off my car → drop it off
  3. ran into my cousin → ran into him/her
  4. pick up my mom → pick her up
  5. drop off the package → drop it off
  6. turn the car around → turn it around
  7. pick up your order → pick it up
  8. ran into Ms. Lopez → ran into her
  9. drop off me and Sam → drop us off
  10. pick up the dry cleaning → pick it up

Find & Fix (8 min): Worksheet Part 6 individually, then compare.

0:15 · 12 min Register: Would You Say This?

  1. Textbook vs. Real Life (Part 7A). Show an airport board: "DEPARTS 6:40." Does anyone say "I will depart now" to a coworker? Match formal words to phrasal verbs. Key message: today the phrasal verbs are the natural, neutral choice; single formal verbs (depart, return, collect, encounter) belong on signs, forms and formal writing.
  2. Would you say this here? (Part 7B). Pairs decide 👍/🤔/👎, then discuss. The interesting debates: collect my daughter (understood, but sounds formal or British to Americans) and bounce to your manager.
  3. Slang to recognize: bounce and dip = leave. Do not drill them. Ask: "Would you say this to your manager? To a stranger? To a close friend your age?"
"Knowing a word doesn't mean you have to use it. When you leave, head out is always safe."

0:27 · 13 min Listening: The Airport Favor (Worksheet Part 8)

  • Before (2 min): prediction. "Sofia's cousin is flying in tonight. What favor does she ask?" Elicit pick her up.
  • Listen 1 (2 min): check the phrasal verbs heard (all 6 appear).
  • Listen 2 (3 min): details (11:00, Terminal E, mechanic, 11:30, Marco's old roommate).
  • After (6 min): pairs find casual phrases (What's up? · No way! · I owe you one!), recall Week 1 (hit it off), and discuss: Would Marco say "Certainly, I will collect your cousin"? Then pairs re-perform the call with a new city and time.

Audio: press ▶ on the worksheet (device voice) or read it live with a student. Simpler alternative: show the script while playing at 🐢 slower speed. Script is in the Answer Key below.

0:40 · 20 min Speaking & Role-Play

Tell Your Partner (5 min): Worksheet Part 9 questions, 2 phrasal verbs per answer + 1 follow-up question. Remind: you can invent a character.

Role-play cards (15 min): Give each pair one card (R1–R6). 3–4 min per role-play, then rotate: one row moves one seat so everyone gets a new partner and a new card. Two rounds.

  • Before starting, give 1 minute to read and plan, but no writing the dialogue.
  • Walk around with the register checklist: note one great use and one word-order error per pair (pick up it, ran her into, came back to home).
  • R2 (Missing Package) is a register test: polite, no slang, with a stranger. R5 (Surprise at the Store) recycles Week 1 and practices ending a conversation with head out.
A2+: use the useful-language line; aim for 2 expressions.
B1: do the Challenge; aim for 3+ expressions and 2 follow-up questions.

1:00 · 15 min Real Conversation Challenge: Conversation Missions

  1. Round 1 (6 min): new partner + Round 1 card (situation, verbs, sentence starters). 2–3 min conversation, then swap roles. Students tick the Mission Tracker (Part 10).
  2. Feedback (2 min): share one natural sentence you heard.
  3. Round 2 (7 min): new partner, different card from Set C (no sentence starters). Halfway through, Partner B reads the 🔄 twist aloud and both must adapt. This is the spontaneous production moment.

This mirrors the Midterm (2–3 min dialogue, 3 expressions) and Final (3–4 min natural exchange).

1:15 · 10 min The Excuse Generator

Groups of 3–4 draw an E-card. 2 minutes to plan a creative but believable excuse with 3+ phrasal verbs from any week (bonus: one Week 1 idiom). One person tells it to the class as if to the person waiting. Class votes for most believable and most creative. Listen for: I ran into…, I had to turn around…, I dropped off…, I couldn't figure out… (Week 3 preview!)

1:25 · 15 min Quick Write, Exit Ticket & Phrase Journal

  1. Quick Write (6 min): Part 11, reply to the friend's text with 2 phrasal verbs, naturally. Read 2–3 aloud.
  2. Exit Ticket (4 min): Part 12. One True Sentence: everyone reads theirs aloud fast, "popcorn" style.
  3. Phrase Journal (5 min): show the Week 2 page. Assign: 3 expressions heard or used outside class, with register. Tip: "Listen in the car, at the pharmacy, at work at 5 p.m. You'll hear pick up and head out everywhere."

4 Language research report

All six expressions are high-frequency, current, neutral American English, appropriate for A2+/B1, and fine for active production. Pronunciation is General American.

pick up

Meaning
go somewhere to get someone/something and (usually) bring them back; buy or get something
Pronunciation
/ˌpɪk ˈʌp/ · linked "pi-kup"; stress on up; with pronoun "pick-er-UP"
Grammar
Transitive, separable: pick up my mom / pick my mom up / pick her up · ✘ pick up her. Also intransitive senses (phone, business).
Example
"I have to pick up my kids at school." (Britannica)
Collocations
pick up the kids / a prescription / groceries / an order / a shift; pick someone up from work, at the airport
Register
Neutral; safe everywhere
American usage
Very common. Americans say pick up where British English may also use collect ("collect the children").
Multiple meanings
answer the phone ("no one picked up"), learn informally ("picked up a few French phrases"), improve ("business picked up"), lift (literal)
Common mistake
pick up it / pick up her; confusing with pick (choose)
Active production?
✔ Yes, all levels
Source
Britannica Dictionary "pick"; Merriam-Webster "pick up"

drop off

Meaning
take someone/something to a place and leave them there
Pronunciation
/ˌdrɑp ˈɔf/ · "dra-POFF"
Grammar
Transitive, separable: drop off the package / drop the package off / drop it off · ✘ drop off it. Noun: drop-off (a drop-off area).
Example
"I drop the kids off at school in the morning." (Britannica)
Collocations
drop off the kids, a package, your car (at the mechanic), a key; drop someone off at work / at the corner
Register
Neutral
American usage
Very common; natural partner of pick up ("I drop them off at 8 and pick them up at 3").
Multiple meanings
intransitive: fall asleep ("I dropped off during the show", marked American English in Cambridge); decrease ("business drops off after the holidays")
Common mistake
drop off me; using drop = let fall
Active production?
✔ Yes. Teach "take & leave" sense; "fall asleep/decrease" = B1 recognition
Source
Britannica Dictionary "drop" (sense 10) & "drop off"; Cambridge Dictionary "drop off"

turn around

Meaning
turn to face the other way; go back in the direction you came from
Pronunciation
/ˌtɝn əˈraʊnd/ · "tur-na-ROUND"
Grammar
Usually intransitive (We turned around). Transitive + separable: turn the car around / turn it around.
Example
"Turn around and let me look at your back." (Oxford)
Collocations
turn around and go back · turn around at the next light · turn the car around
Register
Neutral
American usage
AmE says turn around; turn round is more common in British English (Oxford). Teach around.
Multiple meanings
turn something around = make a failing business/situation succeed; turnaround (noun). B1+ recognition; links to Week 8 "turn a corner."
Common mistake
Confusing with turn the corner or go around; "turn back around" (redundant)
Active production?
✔ Yes (direction meaning)
Source
Oxford Learner's Dictionaries "turn around"; Merriam-Webster "turn around"

run into

Meaning
meet someone by chance
Pronunciation
/ˌrʌn ˈɪntu/; in sentences into is light: "I RAN into my BOSS"
Grammar
Transitive, inseparable: ran into my neighbor / ran into her · ✘ ran her into (that pattern = crash something into something: "He ran his bike into a parked van"). Past: ran.
Example
"I ran into an old classmate the other day." (Britannica / Merriam-Webster)
Collocations
run into an old friend / a neighbor / someone at the store; run into problems / trouble / traffic / bad weather
Register
Oxford labels the "meet" sense informal; Britannica gives no label. In practice: everyday spoken English, fine with coworkers and managers. In formal writing: "met by chance."
American usage
Very common; synonym bump into.
Multiple meanings
collide with; experience a problem ("ran into some problems")
Common mistake
runned into; ran into at my friend; separating it
Active production?
✔ Yes
Source
Oxford Learner's Dictionaries "run into" & "bump into"; Britannica Dictionary "run into"; Merriam-Webster "run into"

head out

Meaning
leave; start going somewhere
Pronunciation
/ˌhɛd ˈaʊt/ · "hea-DOUT"; often "I'm gonna head out" in casual speech
Grammar
Intransitive (no object). head out (at 7) · head out to + place · head out of + place · head out the door. Home → head home.
Example
"She headed out early this morning." (Britannica)
Collocations
head out early · I'm heading out · head out to the beach / store · head out for the day
Register
Neutral-casual; no dictionary label. Natural at work and with strangers. Formal written equivalent: depart / leave.
American usage
Extremely common in AmE, especially to announce leaving work or a party. It focuses on leaving, not on the destination (Rachel's English). Current: Merriam-Webster's example sentences come from Sept–Oct 2026 news.
Pragmatics
"I'm going to head out" = polite, soft way to end a visit or say you're leaving work. Very useful for small talk (Week 1).
Common mistake
head out the meeting (→ of); head out to home (→ head home)
Active production?
✔ Yes. Final-exam core expression
Source
Merriam-Webster "head out"; Britannica Dictionary "head"; Dictionary.com "head out"; Rachel's English "Phrasal Verb: Head"

come back

Meaning
return to a place
Pronunciation
/ˌkʌm ˈbæk/ · stress on back; noun COMEback
Grammar
Intransitive. come back (from X) (to X); come back home (no to); past came. Object needed? → bring it back.
Example
"I hope you'll come back and see us again soon." (Britannica)
Collocations
come back home / later / tomorrow; come back from vacation; come back for someone
Register
Neutral (formal: return)
American usage
Very common; also "I'll be back in 10 minutes."
Multiple meanings
be remembered ("it's all coming back to me"), become popular again, recover; noun comeback
Common mistake
comed back; come back to home; come it back (→ bring it back)
Active production?
✔ Yes
Source
Britannica Dictionary "come back"; Merriam-Webster "come back"

Formal ↔ everyday pairs: depart ↔ head out/leave · return ↔ come back · collect ↔ pick up · deliver ↔ drop off · encounter (by chance) ↔ run into. Part 8: Listening

Listen 1: all six verbs occur. Listen 2: 1 eleven (11:00 p.m.) · 2 E · 3 mechanic · 4 11:30 · 5 b. After: casual phrases: What's up? · No way! · I owe you one! · Ha!; Week 1: hit it off. "Collect your cousin" is too formal between friends.

Script: The Airport Favor

Sofia: Hey Marco, it's Sofia. Do you have a minute?

Marco: Sure. What's up?

Sofia: So, my cousin is flying in from Lisbon tonight. Her flight lands at eleven. Could you pick her up at the airport? My car's at the mechanic.

Marco: Tonight? Hmm. I work early tomorrow… but okay, I can do it. Which terminal?

Sofia: Terminal E. Thank you so much!

Marco: No problem. Where should I drop her off?

Sofia: At my place. I'm heading out to my sister's for dinner, but I'll come back early. I'll be home by eleven thirty.

Marco: Okay. And if I miss the exit, I'll just turn around. Logan always confuses me.

Sofia: Ha! Me too. Oh, hey, guess who I ran into today? Your old roommate, Ben!

Marco: No way! We really hit it off back then. Tell him I said hi.

Sofia: I will. Thanks again, Marco. I owe you one!

Part 11: Quick Write (model)

"Hi! Yes, I'm still coming! 😊 I have to drop off my son at my mom's first, so I'll head out around 7. Could you pick me up at the bus stop on Main? Thanks!!"
