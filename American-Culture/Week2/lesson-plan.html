Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  • Describe how the United States grew from 13 colonies to 50 states
  • Explain the basic purpose of the U.S. Constitution in simple terms
  • Identify key U.S. states by geography, history, and cultural significance
  • Use descriptive and comparative language to talk about American states
  • Collaborate to research and present information about a specific state

Detailed Block Instructions

Block 1 — Warm-Up: State Brainstorm 2:00–2:15 Whole Class

Purpose: Activate prior knowledge, create energy, zero stakes.

Teacher Instructions
  • Project the blank, unlabeled U.S. map (Slide 3).
  • Say: "Look at this map. How many of these places can you name? Shout them out!"
  • As students call out state names, write them on the whiteboard.
  • After about 5 minutes of shouting, ask pairs to discuss: "Have you ever been to a U.S. state? What did you see or notice?" (3 min)
  • Bring it back to the whole class and take 2–3 quick shares.

Transition: "Most of you know the famous ones — California, New York, Florida. But do you know HOW the U.S. became 50 states? Let's find out."

Block 2 — Mini-Lesson: Visual Timeline 2:15–2:40 Whole Class

Purpose: Build background knowledge visually. No lecturing: the teacher tells the story from the slides.

Teacher Instructions
  • Use Slides 4–11. Each slide = one chapter of the story.
  • Keep it conversational: "Imagine it's 1607. You just crossed the Atlantic Ocean…"
  • Stop on the Constitution slide and ask: "What do you think a Constitution is? Turn and tell your partner — 30 seconds."
  • Reveal: "It's the rulebook for the whole country. Written in 1787. Still used today."
  • Stop on the Alaska/Hawaii slides: "Who can guess — which state joined LAST?" (Let them guess.)
Key Story Beats (Slides 5–11)
  • 1607 — First permanent English colony: Virginia (Jamestown)
  • 1776 — Declaration of Independence: 13 colonies become states
  • 1787 — The Constitution: the rulebook for the new country
  • 1803 — Louisiana Purchase: U.S. doubles in size overnight
  • 1845–1848 — Texas, California, and the Southwest join
  • 1867 — Alaska purchased from Russia for $7.2 million
  • 1959 — Hawaii: the 50th and final state

Transition: "Before we look at individual states, let's make sure the timeline makes sense. You're going to put the story in order with a partner."

Activity 1 — Put It in Order: Timeline Cards 2:40–2:55 Pairs

Purpose: Check comprehension of the timeline through a hands-on activity.

Materials: 6 printed event cards per pair (table below — print & cut). Students can also complete the Quick Check in Part 1 of their worksheet.

Student Instructions
  • You have 6 cards. Each card has one event from American history.
  • Work with your partner: put the cards in the correct time order.
  • You have 5 minutes.

  • Spend about 2.5 minutes per state: read the key facts, ask the 1 question at the bottom of the slide, move on.

Activity 2 — Map Labeling 3:25–3:40 Individual → Pairs

Purpose: Reinforce geography through active recall. Students name the numbered states on the worksheet map (Part 4) using the word bank.

Teacher Instructions
  • The map is numbered in alphabetical order (1 = Alabama … 50 = Wyoming). Show the clues on Slide 26.
  • Students work individually for 6 minutes to fill in as many state names as possible.
  • Then they compare with a partner for 4 minutes.
  • Review with Slide 27 (5 min). Call on students: "Who got #5? What do you know about California?"

Grouping note: If time allows, quickly review the states students found hardest.

Break 3:40–4:10 30 minutes

Before the break, announce the Jeopardy teams. Write 5 team names on the board and let students choose their own team name. (This builds investment before the game.) Show Slide 28 during the break. Ask teams to sit together when they return.

Block 4 — Language Focus: Describing & Comparing States 4:10–4:25 Pairs

New block for the longer afternoon session. It uses the existing Language Focus slide and the vocabulary at the end of this plan.

Purpose: Re-energize after the break and give students the language they need for Jeopardy and their State Spotlight presentation.

  • Show Slide 29. Model each sentence frame aloud with one example.
  • Pairs: each student chooses 2 states from the Deep Dive and says 3 sentences about them using the frames (one must use "Compared to…"). (7 min)
  • Take 3–4 volunteer sentences. Gently correct "The United States have" → "has".
  • The last 2–3 minutes: teams re-seat, and the teacher opens Jeopardy on the projector.

Block 5 — American States Jeopardy 4:25–5:15 5 Teams

Purpose: Competitive review game covering all lesson content. High energy, student-centered, cumulative.

Setup
  • Open the Jeopardy HTML file in a browser and project it full-screen (Slide 30 introduces it).
  • Enter the 5 team names in the scoreboard fields at the top.
  • Teams sit together. One spokesperson answers each question.
  • The teacher reads the question aloud and clicks the timer button. The 30 seconds start, and a bell rings at 0.
  • Correct answer = the team earns the points. Wrong answer = another team can steal.

Point values: 100 / 200 / 300 / 400 / 500 / 700 per category (harder questions = more points).
Steal rule: If a team answers wrong, any other team can buzz in within 5 seconds for half the points.

Block 6 — State Spotlight: Project Launch & Research 5:15–5:40 Groups

New block. The State Spotlight project was already on the worksheet, and "research and present information about a specific state" is a lesson objective. The longer afternoon gives time to launch it in class.
  • Show Slide 31. Students open the State Spotlight section of the worksheet and confirm their state and group.
  • Walk through "What to Include", the Personal Connection questions, and the rubric (3 min).
  • Groups research on their phones and type notes into the worksheet (about 20 min). Circulate and check sources and paraphrasing ("use your own words").
  • Remind students that the notes save on their own device. They should use the same phone or computer next time, or print / save as PDF.

Block 7 — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket 5:40–5:50 Individual

Students answer ONE of the following (spoken, or written in Part 5 of the worksheet) (Slide 32):

  • "Which state surprised you most today — and why?"
  • "If you could live in any U.S. state, which would you choose? Give two reasons."

Take 3–4 quick verbal shares. Close with: "Next class, we'll look at American values and how they show up in everyday life."

Sentence Frames for Speaking

  • "[State] is known for its ___________ and ___________."
  • "This state is located in the ___________ part of the United States."
  • "Compared to [State], [State] is much larger / more populated / colder."
  • "One surprising fact about [State] is that ___________."
  • "[State] joined the United States in [year], when ___________."

Teacher Notes

Timing Tips

  • If Block 2 runs long, trim the timeline to 5 key events instead of 7.
  • If Jeopardy runs over, skip the 700-point questions and go straight to Wild Card.
  • If time is short in Session 2, reduce State Spotlight research to 15 minutes and assign the rest as homework.
  • Start the break on time (3:40) and give a 2-minute warning at 4:08 so teams are seated for Language Focus.

Common Student Mistakes

  • Confusing colonies with states. Clarify: "The 13 colonies became the first 13 states."
  • Thinking the Constitution is the same as the Declaration of Independence. Show both titles on a slide.
  • Placing Alaska and Hawaii next to the continental states on the map. Clarify that they are not shown to scale or in their real location.
  • Saying "The United States have" instead of "The United States has". Gently correct in context.

Simplify (if needed)

  • Reduce the Deep Dive to 8 states instead of 12.
  • Give students the completed map as a reference during Jeopardy.
  • Allow students to use their worksheets during the game.

Extend (for stronger students)

  • Ask: "Why do you think the Constitution is still used today, almost 240 years later?"
  • Have students research one MORE fact about their favorite state and share it as a bonus round.
  • Add a "Daily Double" question to Jeopardy for extra engagement.
