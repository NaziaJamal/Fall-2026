How the Midterm Works

  1. Prepare your part this week. Each student has their own topic (see your group below).
  2. Present at the real place. Your group speaks for about 10 minutes (3 minutes each), then answers questions from the class.
  3. Listen and take notes on the Field Trip Worksheet when other groups present.
  4. Write a short reflection at the end of the trip.
Important: The questions on the Field Trip Worksheet come from the "Your talk must answer" lists in this guide. Make sure your talk answers them clearly — your classmates need your answers!

Stop 1 · 2:00 PM

Boston Public Library

Group: New, Hook, Raphaela · Copley Square
Big idea: Boston created one of America's first free public libraries — knowledge "Free to All." This connects to Week 4: free public education.

NewHistory: A Library Free to All

Your talk must answer
  1. When was the Boston Public Library founded?
  2. What "firsts" is it famous for?
  3. What do the words "Free to All" on the building mean?
Starter facts (check and add more)
  • Founded in 1848.
  • America's first large free city library, and the first public library to lend books to take home.
  • "Free to All" is carved on the front of the Central Library.

HookThe Building: A "Palace for the People"

Your talk must answer
  1. Who designed the main building, and when did it open?
  2. What is Bates Hall, and what does it look like?
  3. Name one piece of art inside the library.
Starter facts
  • The McKim Building was designed by Charles Follen McKim and opened in 1895.
  • Bates Hall is the huge reading room with a high arched ceiling and green lamps.
  • Inside: famous murals (for example, by John Singer Sargent) and a quiet courtyard.

RaphaelaThe Library Today

Your talk must answer
  1. What can people do at the library today (not only borrow books)?
  2. Who can get a library card, and how much does it cost?
  3. Compare: Are libraries free and popular in your country?
Starter facts
  • There is a newer building on Boylston Street (opened 1972, renovated 2016) and about 25 neighborhood branches.
  • Free events, computers, classes, and English-learning programs.
  • Check the BPL website for who can get a free card.

Sources: bpl.org/about · bpl.org/bpl-history

Stop 2 · 2:35 PM

The Public Garden

Group: Isabella, Beatriz, Wesley · Arlington Street entrance
Big idea: A swampy area became America's first public botanical garden — a beautiful place for everyone, full of history and stories.

IsabellaHistory of the Public Garden

Your talk must answer
  1. When was the Public Garden created, and what makes it a "first"?
  2. What was the land like before it became a garden?
  3. How is the Public Garden different from Boston Common next door?
Starter facts
  • Created in 1837 — the first public botanical garden in the United States.
  • It was built on marshy land (wet, muddy land) near the Charles River that was filled in.
  • Boston Common (1634) is older and was used for cows and public events; the Garden is planned with flowers and trees.

BeatrizThe George Washington Statue

Your talk must answer
  1. Who was George Washington?
  2. Who made this statue, and when?
  3. What is Washington's connection to Boston?
Starter facts
  • Washington led the American army in the Revolution and became the first U.S. President.
  • The statue (Washington on a horse) is by Thomas Ball and was unveiled in 1869.
  • In 1775–1776, Washington led the army that forced the British to leave Boston in March 1776.

WesleyThe Lagoon: Swan Boats & Ducklings

Your talk must answer
  1. What are the Swan Boats, and how long have they been on the Lagoon?
  2. What is Make Way for Ducklings?
  3. Why do families and visitors love this part of the Garden?
Starter facts
  • The pedal-powered Swan Boats have run on the Lagoon since 1877. They only run in spring and summer, so they will not be on the water during our trip — bring a photo!
  • Make Way for Ducklings is a 1941 children's book by Robert McCloskey set in the Public Garden.
  • Bronze duckling statues (by Nancy Schön, 1987) are near the corner of Beacon and Charles Streets.

Sources: City of Boston — Parks & Recreation · Friends of the Public Garden · swanboats.com

Stop 3 · 3:10 PM

The Massachusetts State House

Group: Bobe, MiMi, Rachel · Beacon Street
Big idea: The building with the gold dome is the home of Massachusetts state government. This connects to Week 3: government offices.

BobeHistory & the Golden Dome

Your talk must answer
  1. Who designed the State House, and when was it finished?
  2. Whose land was it built on?
  3. What is the dome made of, and what happened to it during World War II?
Starter facts
  • Designed by Charles Bulfinch; finished in 1798.
  • Built on land that was once John Hancock's cow pasture.
  • The dome was covered in copper by Paul Revere's company in 1802; today it is covered in 23-karat gold leaf. In World War II it was painted gray so enemy planes could not see it.

MiMiWhat Happens Inside?

Your talk must answer
  1. Who works in the State House?
  2. What is the difference between state government and city government (City Hall)?
  3. Can visitors go inside?
Starter facts
  • The Governor's office and the state legislature — the House of Representatives and the Senate — are here.
  • The state makes laws for all of Massachusetts; the city (Boston City Hall) runs local services.
  • Free tours are available on weekdays (check the official tours page).

RachelSymbols & Monuments

Your talk must answer
  1. What is the "Sacred Cod," and why is a fish a symbol of Massachusetts?
  2. What is the memorial across the street, and who does it honor?
  3. Why is that memorial important for the history of civil rights?
Starter facts
  • The Sacred Cod is a wooden fish that hangs in the House of Representatives — fishing was very important to the state's economy.
  • The Robert Gould Shaw and 54th Regiment Memorial (by Augustus Saint-Gaudens, 1897) honors one of the first Black regiments to fight in the Civil War.
  • Connect to Week 4: the long fight for equal rights.

Sources: Mass. Secretary of the Commonwealth — State House · National Park Service — Boston African American Historic Site

Stop 4 · 3:30 PM · Walking talk

The Freedom Trail

Group: Mina, Jihane, Keyla · Each person presents at a different place as we walk
Big idea: A red line on the sidewalk connects the places where the American Revolution began. This connects to Week 2: from 13 colonies to a new country.

MinaWhat Is the Freedom Trail? + Park Street & School Street

Where you present: Park Street Church / Granary Burying Ground (Tremont St.)

Your talk must answer
  1. What is the Freedom Trail? How long is it, and how many sites does it have?
  2. Which famous Americans are buried in the Granary Burying Ground?
  3. What school started on School Street, and why is it important? (Week 4!)
Starter facts
  • The Freedom Trail is about 2.5 miles long and connects 16 historic sites. It was created in 1951.
  • Granary Burying Ground: Paul Revere, Samuel Adams, John Hancock, and victims of the Boston Massacre.
  • Boston Latin School (1635) — the first public school in America — started on what is now School Street.

JihaneOld South Meeting House & the Boston Tea Party

Where you present: Old South Meeting House (Washington St. & Milk St.)

Your talk must answer
  1. What happened at the Old South Meeting House on December 16, 1773?
  2. What was the Boston Tea Party, and why were people angry?
  3. What did "No taxation without representation" mean?
Starter facts
  • Thousands of people met here to protest a British tax on tea.
  • That night, colonists dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor.
  • Colonists did not want to pay taxes to a government where they had no vote.

KeylaOld State House & the Boston Massacre

Where you present: Old State House (Washington St. & State St.)

Your talk must answer
  1. What was the Boston Massacre? When did it happen, and how many people died?
  2. Who was Crispus Attucks?
  3. What important document was read from the Old State House balcony in 1776?
Starter facts
  • On March 5, 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd; five colonists were killed.
  • Crispus Attucks, a man of African and Native American heritage, is often called the first person killed in the Revolution.
  • On July 18, 1776, the Declaration of Independence was read to Bostonians from the balcony.
Tip: Look down! Follow the red brick line on the sidewalk between your stops.

Sources: The Freedom Trail Foundation · Revolutionary Spaces (Old South & Old State House) · National Park Service — Boston National Historical Park

Stop 5 · 4:10 PM

Faneuil Hall & Quincy Market

Group: Nicole, Esteban, Merve, Anastas · Samuel Adams statue (Dock Square)
Big idea: For almost 300 years, this has been a place to buy, sell, and speak freely — from Revolution speeches to today's street performers.

NicoleFaneuil Hall: The "Cradle of Liberty"

Your talk must answer
  1. Who was Peter Faneuil, and when was the hall built?
  2. Why is Faneuil Hall called the "Cradle of Liberty"?
  3. Who is the statue in front of the hall?
Starter facts
  • Built in 1742, a gift to Boston from the merchant Peter Faneuil: a market downstairs and a meeting hall upstairs.
  • Samuel Adams and other colonists gave speeches here against British rule.
  • The statue in front shows Samuel Adams.

EstebanA Place for Free Speech

Your talk must answer
  1. After the Revolution, what important causes did people speak about here?
  2. Name one famous speaker who spoke at Faneuil Hall.
  3. What is the golden animal on top of the building?
Starter facts
  • Abolitionists (people who fought to end slavery), such as Frederick Douglass, spoke here; so did people working for women's rights.
  • The grasshopper weathervane on the roof dates from 1742 (made by Shem Drowne).

MerveQuincy Market: History

Your talk must answer
  1. When was Quincy Market built, and who is it named after?
  2. Why did Boston need a bigger market?
  3. What was sold here in the 1800s?
Starter facts
  • Built in 1824–1826 and named after Mayor Josiah Quincy.
  • Boston was growing quickly, and Faneuil Hall's market was too small.
  • It sold meat, fish, vegetables, and other food.

AnastasFaneuil Hall Marketplace Today

Your talk must answer
  1. What happened to the market area in 1976?
  2. What can visitors do here today?
  3. Compare: Is there a famous market or square like this in your country?
Starter facts
  • The old buildings were restored and reopened in 1976 as a "festival marketplace" with shops and restaurants.
  • Today: food hall, shops, and street performers; millions of visitors each year.

Sources: National Park Service — Boston National Historical Park · The Freedom Trail Foundation · faneuilhallmarketplace.com

How to Prepare & Present

Your 3-minute talk

  1. Hook (20 seconds): Start with a surprising fact or a question: "Can you guess how old this building is?"
  2. Answer your 3 questions (2 minutes): Point to things we can see. Use simple, clear English.
  3. Connect (30 seconds): Connect to our class (Weeks 1–4) or to your home country.
  4. Hand over: "Now ___ will tell you about…"

Rules for a great talk

  • Use your own words. Do not read from your phone. You may bring one small card with key words.
  • Speak loudly — we are outside and the city is noisy!
  • Bring one visual: an old photo or map on your phone or printed, to show the group.
  • At the end, your group asks the class one question.

Useful language

Pointing"If you look up/to your left, you can see…" · "This is where…"
Talking about the past"In 1773, …" · "This building was built in…" · "It used to be…"
Explaining why"This is important because…" · "This led to…"
Connecting"This reminds me of… in my country." · "Remember in Week 4 we learned…"
