Part One

Police Encounters

What to do, what to say, and what your rights are if you are ever stopped or questioned by police.

During a Police Encounter

Simple steps to stay safe and protect your rights

1
Stay calm and politeKeep your hands visible. Do not run or argue at the scene.
2
Show ID if askedIf you are driving, you must show your driver's license, registration, and proof of insurance. In other situations, you do not always have to answer questions.
3
You can ask if you are free to go"Am I being detained, or am I free to leave?" is a reasonable, respectful question.
4
You have the right to remain silentYou can say: "I am going to remain silent. I would like to speak to a lawyer."

Miranda Rights

The words police must say before questioning you in custody

“

You have the right to remain silent.
Anything you say can be used against you in court.
You have the right to an attorney.
If you cannot afford one, one will be appointed for you.

When does this apply?

  • Only required when you are in custody AND being questioned.
  • Police do not need to read it just to arrest you or ask for ID.
  • If you are questioned in custody without it, ask for a lawyer right away.
  • Saying clearly, "I want a lawyer," should stop police questioning.

Getting Legal Help

When to call a lawyer, and where to find free or low-cost help

When to Call a Lawyer

  • You are arrested or formally charged with a crime
  • Police ask to search your home, car, or phone
  • You are asked to sign any legal document
  • You are facing eviction or losing a job unfairly
  • You are unsure if you must answer a question

Free & Low-Cost Legal Aid

Legal aid organizations
Free civil legal help for low-income residents
Public Defender's Office
Free lawyer for criminal cases if you qualify
Local Bar Association
Lawyer referral services, often low-cost consults
Immigrant legal services
Nonprofits that help with immigration & rights

Part Two

Tenant Rights

Understanding your lease, your landlord's duties, and how to report problems in your home.

Lease Agreements

The written contract between you and your landlord

Read before you sign

Check the rent amount, due date, lease length, and rules about guests or pets.

Know what is included

Confirm which utilities (heat, water, electric) are included in the rent.

Get everything in writing

Verbal promises from a landlord are hard to prove — ask for changes in writing.

Keep a copy

Always keep a signed copy of your lease and any added pages for your records.

Heat & Security Deposits

Two of the most common landlord-tenant issues

Heat Requirements

  • Landlords must provide heat during cold months (dates vary by city/state).
  • Minimum indoor temperatures are set by local codes (often 64–68°F).
  • If the heat is broken, tell your landlord in writing and keep a copy.
  • If it is not fixed, contact code enforcement or the health department.

Security Deposits

  • Many states limit the deposit, often to 1–2 months' rent. Some states have no limit.
  • Landlords must return it within a set number of days after you move out (often 14–30).
  • Take photos of the apartment when you move in and when you move out.
  • If money is kept, the landlord must give you an itemized list of reasons.

Reporting Violations

What to do if your landlord is not following the law

1
Document the problemTake dated photos or videos and keep copies of any messages.
2
Notify your landlord in writingSend a letter or email describing the issue and asking for a repair date.
3
Contact code enforcementYour city or town's housing / code enforcement office can inspect the property.
4
Reach out for legal helpTenant unions and legal aid offices can advise you on next steps and your rights.

Part Three

Government Offices

Which office to visit for which need — City Hall, the RMV, Social Security, and USCIS.

City Hall & the RMV

Local and state offices you will likely visit often

City Hall

  • Marriage licenses
  • Birth & death certificates
  • Property taxes & permits
  • Voter registration
  • Parking tickets & city services

RMV / DMV

  • Driver's licenses & learner's permits
  • State ID cards
  • Vehicle registration & titles
  • License plates & renewals
  • Written & road driving tests

Social Security & USCIS

Two federal agencies that matter for work, benefits, and immigration status

Social Security Administration (SSA)

  • Social Security number (SSN) cards
  • Retirement, disability & survivor benefits
  • Medicare enrollment
  • Replacing a lost SSN card
  • Reporting a name change

U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Services (USCIS)

  • Green cards (permanent residency)
  • Work permits (EAD)
  • Naturalization / citizenship applications
  • Family-based immigration petitions
  • Status renewals & extensions
