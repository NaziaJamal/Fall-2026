Week 7 Overview: Civic Participation

LevelIndependent (A2+–B1)
Class 12:00 – 3:40
Break3:40 – 4:10
Class 24:10 – 5:50
Focus cityBoston, MA

Syllabus unit: Civic Participation — voting eligibility, how to register, local government, school committees, city council. Action step: check voter registration status at sec.state.ma.us (citizens only). Discussion theme: "What does civic participation look like in your home country? What is possible here?"

Learning objectives

  • Define civic participation and name at least 4 ways to take part.
  • Explain Massachusetts voting eligibility and the registration process, including the 10-day deadline.
  • Describe the three levels of government and match everyday problems to the right level.
  • Explain the roles of the Boston City Council and School Committee, and how residents can testify.
  • Use target vocabulary to give a 1-minute public comment and to write a comparison paragraph.

What changed from the original Zoom materials: the plan is adapted from a 6:00–10:00 PM Zoom class to your in-person 2:00–5:50 schedule. Facts are localized to Massachusetts and Boston: the registration deadline is 10 days (not "15–30 days"), there is no Election Day registration, Boston's School Committee is appointed by the mayor (not elected), and the City Council has 13 members. New activities, movement, and printable cards were added. The level label now reads "Independent" to match your syllabus.
Sensitivity: Many students are not U.S. citizens. Never ask a student about their own citizenship or immigration status. The "Can They Vote?" activity uses made-up characters for that reason. Class 1 — Step by Step

1. Welcome + Four Corners (2:00–2:15) · Slides 1–3

  • Show the title slide and the plan for the day (Slide 2).
  • Slide 3: students stand and walk to the corner that matches something they have done in any country. If they have done several, they choose their favorite.
  • Groups talk for 3 minutes ("I chose this corner because I once…"). One reporter per corner shares. Then students write one sentence in the worksheet warm-up.
  • If a corner is empty, ask: "Why do you think nobody chose this one?"

2. The big idea + vocabulary + Word Charades (2:15–2:30) · Slides 4–7

  • Slides 4–5: define civic participation. Quick pair question on Slide 5.
  • Slide 6: model pronunciation. Use the Vocabulary Cards tab for audio. Drill the stress: EL-i-gi-ble, REJ-is-ter, kuh-MIT-ee, KOWN-suhl.
  • Slide 7: two teams. One student clicks a card (keep the class from seeing it) and acts it out or explains it without saying the word. 5-minute timer.

3. Exercise 1 (2:30–2:40) · Slide 8

  • Students complete Ex. 1 on their device or on paper, then click Check. Review the tricky items: committee vs. council.

4. Who can vote? + Can They Vote? (2:40–2:55) · Slides 9–11

  • Slides 9–10: the four rules and the warning for non-citizens. Say it clearly: never register if you are not a citizen.
  • Slide 11: pairs read the 6 printed character cards (or use the worksheet), decide Yes / No / Not yet, and write why. Reveal each card on the slide and discuss.
  • Language: "She can vote because…" / "He can't vote yet because…"

5. How to register + Registration Relay (2:55–3:10) · Slides 12–14

  • Slides 12–13: three ways to register; the 10-day deadline; no Election Day registration; the exception for new citizens.
  • Slide 14: teams of 4 each get the 4 shuffled step cards. Each student holds one. Teams race to line up in order and raise their hands. Check the first team, then reveal on the slide. Students complete Ex. 5A.

6. Reading: Maria's story (3:10–3:30) · Slide 15

  • Students read silently (5 min) and answer questions 1–4 (8 min). Then pairs retell the story in 1 minute without looking (4 min). Check answers and discuss the main idea (3 min).

7. Class Election + exit ticket (3:30–3:40) · Slides 16–17

  • Give each student a printed ballot slip. Students write A, B, C, or D, fold the slip, and walk to the ballot box. Two volunteers count the ballots aloud while you click + on the slide. Click Announce the winner. That warm-up is next week's opener!
  • Ask: "Why is voting secret?" (privacy, freedom, no pressure). Students complete the exit ticket.

Class 2 — Step by Step

1. True or False Line (4:10–4:20) · Slide 19

  • Students stand in the middle. Click Next sentence. Students move left (false) or right (true), talk with their side for 30 seconds, then you click Reveal. Six sentences. Then they complete Ex. 4 quickly, fixing the false statements.

2. Three levels of government + Who Do You Call? (4:20–4:40) · Slides 20–22

  • Slide 20: connect to the field trip — the State House is state government, City Hall is local government.
  • Slide 21: introduce Boston 311.
  • Slide 22: groups sort the printed problem cards into three piles: National, State, Local (10 min). Reveal on the slide. Fastest accurate group wins. Students record answers in the worksheet.

3. School Committee & City Council + Exercise 5B (4:40–4:55) · Slides 23–27

  • Slide 23: Boston's School Committee is appointed by the mayor; most other towns elect theirs. Ask: "Elected or appointed — which is better?" (2 min)
  • Slides 25–26: 13 councilors; anyone can testify, in any language.
  • Slide 27: Ex. 5B (5 min).

4. Mock City Council Hearing (4:55–5:25) · Slides 28–30

  • Set up the room: 5 chairs facing the class (the council), and a "podium" spot in front. Hand out role cards.
  • Prep (5 min): residents write a 1-minute comment using Slide 30; councilors write 2 questions each.
  • Hearing (20 min): the Council President opens. Residents walk to the podium one by one (the Timekeeper holds up a hand at 1 minute). Councilors ask questions, then debate and vote out loud. The Reporter announces the result.
  • Debrief (5 min): "Did the comments change any councilor's mind? Which argument was strongest?"
  • For more or fewer students: add residents or remove councilors (keep an odd number of councilors to avoid a tie).

5. Compare & Discuss + Exercise 3 (5:25–5:40) · Slides 31–33

  • Groups of 3–4 discuss both questions (8 min); one idea per group. Then students start Ex. 3 (7 min) and finish at home if needed.

6. Action step + "I Will Remember" wall (5:40–5:50) · Slides 34–36

  • Students choose one action step. Each writes "One thing I will remember…" on a sticky note, walks to the board, sticks it up, and reads a classmate's note aloud.
Timing tips: If Class 1 runs long, shorten Word Charades to 3 minutes. If Class 2 runs long, cut the councilors' questions in the hearing, and assign Ex. 3 as homework.

Mock City Council Hearing: The Maple Square Lot — Role Cards

Option A: a park with a playground · Option B: a 150-space parking garage · Option C: a small park + 50 parking spaces

Council PresidentYou run the hearing. Open it: "Good afternoon. This hearing is now open. Today we discuss the Maple Square lot." Call each resident to the podium. Keep order. Call the vote at the end. You vote only if there is a tie.
Councilor — District 7 (Maple Square)You represent this neighborhood. You want to hear what your residents need most. Ask: "How would this option help families on your street?"
Councilor — At-Large (budget)You care about cost. A park is cheaper to build, but a garage can earn money from parking fees. Ask: "Who will pay for this?"
Councilor — At-Large (environment)You care about trees, clean air, and climate. Ask: "How will this affect our environment?"
Councilor — District 3 (small business)You want local shops to do well. Ask: "How will this help local businesses?"
Councilor — At-Large (youth & schools)You care about children and teenagers. Ask: "Where do young people in Maple Square spend time now?"
Resident — Parent of two young childrenThere is no safe place nearby for your kids to play. You support Option A.
Resident — Restaurant ownerYour customers can't find parking, and you are losing business. You support Option B.
Resident — Senior (age 72)You drive to doctor's appointments, and parking is very hard to find. You support Option B or C.
Resident — Community garden volunteerThe neighborhood is hot in summer and needs trees. You support Option A.
Resident — High school studentTeens need a basketball court and a place to meet friends. You support Option A or C.
Resident — Nurse who works night shiftsYou come home late and want a safe, well-lit place to park. You support Option C.
Resident — Bus rider and cyclistMore parking means more cars and traffic. You support Option A.
Resident — Landlord with two buildingsYou want what is best for property values. You are not sure, so ask others and support Option C.
TimekeeperEach resident has 1 minute. Raise your hand at 50 seconds and say "Time!" at 1 minute.
ReporterTake notes on the strongest arguments. At the end, announce the vote result and the best argument you heard.

How to Register

Three Ways to Register in Massachusetts

Online

On the state's website. You need a Massachusetts driver's license or state ID.

By Mail

Print the form, fill it out, and mail it to your local election office.

In Person

At the Boston Election Department (City Hall) or the RMV.

Then wait for your confirmation notice in the mail.

When to Register

Don't Miss the Deadline!

10

days before the election — the Massachusetts deadline

  • You cannot register on Election Day in Massachusetts.
  • Every state has different rules and deadlines.
  • New citizens sworn in after the deadline can register in person until 4 p.m. the day before.
  • Check your status at sec.state.ma.us

School Committees

What Is a School Committee?

A small group that makes decisions about local public schools: budgets, policies, and hiring the superintendent.

In Boston

The Mayor appoints the 7 voting members (plus 1 student member). Boston is the only fully appointed school committee in Massachusetts.

In most other towns

People elect their school committee members in local elections.

DiscussIs it better to elect or appoint a school committee? Why?

Teacher Study Notes

Background facts, likely student questions, and teaching tips. All facts were checked against official Massachusetts and Boston sources.

Key facts: voting in Massachusetts

  • Eligibility: U.S. citizen, Massachusetts resident, at least 18 on or before Election Day. Residents can pre-register at 16.
  • Deadline: 10 days before any election or town meeting, whether online, by mail (postmark), or in person. There is no Election Day registration.
  • Exception for new citizens: people naturalized after the deadline can register in person at their local election office until 4 p.m. the day before the election, with proof of the ceremony date.
  • How: online (needs a Massachusetts driver's license, permit, or state ID), by mail, or in person (local election office, or the RMV and some other state agencies). Status check: sec.state.ma.us.

Key facts: Boston

  • City Council: 13 councilors (9 district + 4 at-large), elected every 2 years. They create and amend local laws and approve the city budget.
  • Testimony: the public can testify at City Council hearings in person, on Zoom, by email, by mail, or by video (about 2 minutes), in any language. Hearings are livestreamed.
  • School Committee: 7 voting members appointed by the Mayor from a list prepared by a nominating panel, plus a non-voting student member. Boston is the only fully appointed school committee in Massachusetts. Whether to switch to an elected or hybrid committee has been debated for years (a 2021 non-binding ballot question favored an elected committee). Check the current status before class if students ask.
  • Boston 311: the city's service line and app for non-emergency problems (potholes, streetlights, trash).

Questions students may ask

"Can green card holders vote?"

No. Only U.S. citizens can vote in Massachusetts elections. Permanent residents can apply to naturalize, usually after 5 years (3 if married to a U.S. citizen).

"Do I need to show ID to vote?"

Usually not in Massachusetts, though first-time voters may be asked for ID. For anything specific to a student's own situation, send them to sec.state.ma.us rather than answering from memory.

"What happens if a non-citizen registers by mistake?"

It can create serious immigration consequences. Tell students to never register if they are not citizens, and to talk to an immigration attorney or legal aid office if it already happened. Do not give legal advice.

"Can I speak at a hearing if my English isn't perfect?"

Yes. The Boston City Council accepts testimony in any language and arranges translation.

"Who is my city councilor?"

Students can find their district on boston.gov by entering their address. This is a good homework step.

Sensitivity & inclusion

  • Never ask students about their citizenship or immigration status. Use the made-up characters.
  • Some students may come from countries where political participation was dangerous. Let them choose how much to share, and accept "In my country, it is complicated" as a full answer.
  • Keep the mock hearing local and neutral (a park vs. parking). Avoid real candidates and parties.

Differentiation

  • Support: give lower-level students resident roles with a fill-in script (Slide 30). Pair them with stronger readers for "Can They Vote?"
  • Challenge: stronger students can be councilors or the Council President, or write a 150-word email testimony to the City Council as homework.
