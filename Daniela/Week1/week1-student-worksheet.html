Text A: Write Emails Like a Military Officer

A B2 learning adaptation of an article by Kabir Sehgal, Harvard Business Review

AIn the armed forces, an unclear email is more than an annoyance: it can put a mission at risk. Kabir Sehgal, a U.S. Navy veteran who later worked in banking and corporate strategy, learned to structure his messages carefully during his military service. When he moved into the corporate world, he kept using those habits, and he noticed that colleagues and clients began to reply faster and more usefully. He recommends three techniques.

Section 1

BBefore opening an email, the recipient sees only two things: who sent it and the subject line. For this reason, Sehgal argues that the subject should tell the reader exactly what the email is for and what they need to do. Military writers start the subject with a keyword in capital letters, such as ACTION (the reader must do something), SIGN (a signature is needed), INFO (for information only; no reply needed), DECISION (the reader must decide), REQUEST (the writer is asking for permission or approval), and COORD (the writer and reader need to coordinate).

CA manager sending a weekly update might write “INFO – Status Update,” while an employee asking for time off could write “REQUEST – Vacation.” Sehgal admits that the capital letters may seem obvious or even a little loud. However, he believes they help an email stand out in a crowded inbox, and they save the reader from guessing. There is also a benefit for the writer: choosing a keyword forces you to decide what you really want before you add to someone's inbox clutter.

Section 2

DMilitary emails open with a short statement called the BLUF, which stands for “Bottom Line Up Front.” In one or two sentences, it states the purpose of the message and the action required, and it covers the essential who, what, where, when, and why. The idea is simple: most readers don't want the full history behind a decision. They want to know how the message affects them.

EIn his corporate job, Sehgal doesn't use the acronym, because many people wouldn't understand it. Instead, he begins with the words “Bottom line” in bold, and he sometimes even highlights them. Any supporting details come afterward, under a heading such as “Background.”

Section 3

FAccording to Sehgal, short emails work better than long ones, and military writers try to fit the whole message on one screen so that the reader doesn't need to scroll. They also prefer the active voice (“Our team finalized the proposal”) to the passive voice (“The proposal was finalized by our team”), because active sentences are shorter and show clearly who did what. When a message genuinely needs more explanation, the extra information should appear as bullet points after the bottom line.

GFinally, instead of attaching large files, military professionals usually share a link to the document. This keeps inboxes lighter, gives readers the most up-to-date version, and makes sure that only people with permission can open the file.

HHere is how the three techniques might look together in a sales context:

Subject: DECISION – Booth size for March trade show (by Friday)

Marcus,

Bottom line: We need your decision by Friday on whether to book the large booth or the standard booth for the March trade show.

Background:

  • The large booth costs 40% more but includes a product demo area.
  • Last year, most of our new leads came from live demos.
  • The organizers will release all unreserved booths on Monday.

ISehgal's conclusion is that these military habits can make your own messages, and the messages of the people you work with, noticeably clearer.

Text B: Don't Delete the Human Element

A B2 learning adaptation of a blog post by Rob Ashton, Emphasis

JRob Ashton, who founded a business-writing training company in the UK, agrees with part of Sehgal's advice. He praises the attention to subject lines, because deciding on a subject line helps writers understand their own goal, and he mostly supports the active voice. However, he warns that following the advice too strictly can damage professional relationships and even lead to misunderstandings.

KFirst, he questions the keywords. A label like “INFO – Status Update” doesn't tell the reader what the update is actually about; a specific subject such as “November marketing figures” is more useful. In addition, a code only works when everyone shares it, as soldiers do. Most people in the corporate world don't, so the capital letters can feel like orders.

LSecond, he argues that brevity shouldn't remove the human side of email. He describes a client whose team wrote accurate emails that came across as cold and abrupt. Customers felt no connection with the company, and it lost business. To illustrate his point, he returns to an example in Sehgal's original article: an employee learns, in an email with no greeting, that her work-from-home days are being cut and that no reply is needed. Ashton asks readers to imagine that she depends on that flexibility to care for her elderly father. How would she feel? Would she want to stay at that company?

MHis solution is not long, padded emails. It is short emails that still include small signals of goodwill: a greeting, a friendly line, an offer to help, a polite sign-off. Each one seems minor, he admits, but across the many emails people receive every day, these details shape working relationships and company culture.

8. Rewrite It: An Email That Gets Results

Activate

Your colleague Bruno wrote this email to a U.S. buyer he met at a trade fair. He asks you to improve it before he sends it. Rewrite it using the best ideas from both Text A and Text B. You have about 10 minutes.

Subject: Meeting

Dear Mr. Robert,

I hope this email finds you well. As we talked last week in the fair, our company is launching a new line of eco-friendly packaging in January and we think it can be very interesting for your stores, because many of your clients are worried about sustainability and the prices are very competitive. I would like to know if is possible to do a meeting to present the products, maybe on the next week, Tuesday or Wednesday in the afternoon, or if you prefer we can do it online. Follows attached the catalog with the informations. I also need to know how many stores would participate in the pilot until the end of the month because of the production planning. Any doubt I am at your disposal.

I wait your return.

Hugs,
Bruno

Recipient: Robert Hayes, Purchasing Manager, a U.S. retail chain
