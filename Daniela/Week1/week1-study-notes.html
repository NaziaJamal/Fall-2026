Emails That Get Results

Your reference for this week. Review it before Week 2, then try the self-check at the end.

1. The big ideas

Sehgal (Text A): be clear

Tell the reader the purpose in the subject line, put the bottom line first, keep it short, use the active voice, and put extra details in bullet points.

Ashton (Text B): be human

Short is good, but don't remove the greeting and friendly signals. Use specific subject lines instead of codes like “INFO –”. Cold emails can cost you business.

Your takeaway

Combine both: clear purpose and structure and a warm, professional tone that fits your reader.

2. Email blueprint

Subject
Topic + what you need (+ deadline). Eco-friendly packaging: meeting next week + pilot numbers
Greeting
Hi Robert, / Dear Mr. Hayes,
Friendly line
It was great meeting you at the trade fair. / Thanks for your quick reply.
Bottom line
The main point or request in one or two sentences.
Details
Short bullet points: questions, background, numbers.
Close
I look forward to hearing from you. / Please let me know if you have any questions.
Sign-off
Best regards, / Best, + your name

3. Key expressions

recipient

noun · Text A, para. B

The person who receives an email or message.

Check every recipient before you click “Reply all.”

Patternsthe recipient(s) of an email · email recipients · intended recipient

Note: More formal than reader. Common in written instructions and policies. Stress: re-CI-pi-ent.

state the purpose

verb phrase · Text A, para. D

To say clearly why you are writing.

State the purpose of your email in the first line.

Patternsstate the purpose / the reason / your request clearly

Note: State is more formal than say. In an email, you can simply write: I'm writing to…

stand out

phrasal verb · Text A, para. C

To look different or better than others, so people notice it.

Our new campaign needs to stand out from the competition.

Patternsstand out from (competitors) · stand out in (a crowded inbox / market) · make something stand out

Portuguese tip: similar to destacar-se. Not stand out of.

up front / lead with

adverb / verb phrase · Text A, para. D

At the beginning, where people see it first. Lead with = start with.

Put the price up front. / Our proposal leads with the client's main problem.

Patternsput something up front · be up front about (= be honest about) · lead with the key message / the benefits

Note: Be up front about something has a second meaning: to be open and honest. She was up front about the delay.

supporting details

noun phrase · Text A, para. E

Facts or information that explain or prove the main point.

Start with your recommendation, then add the supporting details.

Patternsprovide / include / add supporting details · supporting evidence / data

Coming up: In Week 2 you'll practice separating the main idea from supporting details in articles.

come across as

phrasal verb · Text B, para. L

To seem or appear to other people in a particular way.

I didn't want my email to come across as rude, so I added a friendly line.

Patternscome across as + adjective (cold, abrupt, friendly, professional, aggressive) · come across well / badly

Careful: come across as rude, not come across like rude. Come across something (no as) means find it by chance.

signals of goodwill

noun phrase · Text B, para. M

Small words or actions that show you are friendly and want to help.

A greeting and a “let me know if I can help” are small signals of goodwill.

Patternsshow / build goodwill · a gesture of goodwill · goodwill with customers

Coming up: a goodwill gesture (a small gift or discount to keep a customer happy) is a key expression in Week 3.

genuinely

adverb · Text A, para. F

Really; truly; not just because it might be nice.

Only copy people who genuinely need the information.

Patternsgenuinely need / interested / concerned / helpful

Note: Stronger and more sincere than really. I'm genuinely interested in your feedback.

get (straight) to the point

idiom · from Text B's ideas

To say the most important thing without delay.

I know you're busy, so I'll get straight to the point.

Patternsget to the point · come to the point · be to the point (= short and relevant)

Note: Great for meetings and calls. “Let me get straight to the point: we need a decision today.”

build rapport with

verb phrase · from Text B's ideas

To create a friendly, trusting relationship with someone.

Small talk at the start of a call helps you build rapport with new clients.

Patternsbuild / establish / develop rapport with · good rapport

Pronunciation: rap-POR (the final t is silent). Not build rapport to.

5. Register: how formal?

RegisterOpeningAttachmentRequestSign-off
FormalI am writing to inform you that…Please find attached…I would be grateful if you could…Sincerely, / Kind regards,
NeutralI'm writing about… / As we discussed…I've attached…Could you let me know…?Best regards, / Best,
InformalJust a quick note to say…Here's the…Can you…?Thanks! / Cheers,

For most U.S. business emails, neutral is the safe default, even with new clients.
