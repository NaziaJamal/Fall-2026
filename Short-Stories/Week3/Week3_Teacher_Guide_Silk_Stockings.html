1. The story at a glance

Story“A Pair of Silk Stockings,” Kate Chopin (1850–1904)
First publishedVogue, September 16, 1897 (written April 1896). Public domain.
Syllabus placementWeek 3, Unit II “New American Voices (1860s–1910s).” The syllabus pairs Twain’s “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” with this story. This week uses Chopin only (see the note on the Literary Lens below).
Literary Lens and styleSyllabus: Frame Narrator & Turning Point. Because Twain is not taught this week, the lens is Turning Point (the anthology’s own lens for this story), and the Author’s Style focus is Interior Monologue, which matches the syllabus homework question “How does Chopin use interior monologue?” Frame narration can be taught later in the course; an optional Twain extension is in section 12.
Class textApproach lightly adapted version, §1–§8, ≈ 1,950 words (original ≈ 2,000).
Reading time (B2)Reading 1 (§1–§3, ≈ 800 words): 8–10 min. Reading 2 (§4–§8, ≈ 1,150 words): 11–13 min. The whole story is read in Class A, so Class B is free for analysis.
Difficulty for B2–B2+Moderate. The language is clear and concrete; the challenge is inference: what Chopin does not say about Mrs. Sommers’s past, her husband, and the end of her day.
Plot in briefMrs. Sommers, a poor mother who once knew “better days,” unexpectedly receives $15 and carefully plans to spend it on her children. Exhausted and hungry at a department store, her hand rests on a pile of silk stockings. She buys a pair for herself and, guided by an “impulse” she does not question, spends the afternoon on boots, gloves, magazines, a fine lunch, and a theater matinee. On the cable car home, a stranger cannot read her face, which hides a longing that the ride would never end.
Why it fits Week 3It has a clear, debatable turning point; Chopin moves smoothly between reporting actions and entering her character’s mind; and it opens the syllabus focus on women’s autonomy in 19th-century America. It also mirrors Week 2: a character leaves home for a short time and returns changed, which gives students their first cross-text synthesis.

Section numbers (§)

§1 fifteen dollars · §2 at the counter · §3 the silk stockings · STOP & PREDICT · §4 the impulse (stockings on; boots) · §5 gloves and magazines · §6 the restaurant · §7 the theater · §8 the cable car

2. Adaptation strategy

Preserved (almost word for word)

  • The plot, every purchase, the order of events, and the ending.
  • Key lines: “luckily tomorrow never comes” · “glide serpent-like through her fingers” · “She was not thinking at all” · “some mechanical impulse that directed her actions and freed her of responsibility” · “How good was the touch of the raw silk to her flesh!” · “a feeling of assurance, a sense of belonging” · “as before a princess of royal blood” · “It was like a dream ended.”
  • The final sentence, with one word changed (poignant → “deep, painful”).
  • All interior monologue markers (exclamations, “no! when she came to think of it”).

Changed or explained

  • porte-monnaie → purse; percale → cotton cloth; shirt waists → shirts; darning → mending; luncheon → lunch; placard → sign; tiara → crown of diamonds; parcel → package; damask → white tablecloths; blue-points → oysters; crème frappée → a frozen dessert; Rhine wine → German white wine.
  • morbid retrospection → gloomy looking back; consternation → alarm; preposterous → ridiculous; decipher → understand.
  • “besieging breastworks of shirting and figured lawn” → “attacking walls of shirt cloth and printed cotton like soldiers attacking a fort” (the battle metaphor is kept and made explicit).
  • “Two hectic blotches” → “Two bright red spots.”

3. Content note

  • Money and poverty: the Engage task (Part 1) is hypothetical. Never ask students about their own income, debts, or family finances. Some adult students may support families at home on limited money; keep the discussion about the character and about cultural expectations in general.
  • Gender and parenting: Part 6 asks whether a mother may spend on herself. Expect strong, culturally varied views; the goal is to examine the standard students use, not to decide who is right.
  • The story mentions wine at lunch; nothing else in it is sensitive.

4. Learning objectives and syllabus alignment

By the end of Week 3, students will be able to:

  1. Read and understand a complete, authentic 19th-century American story with only light adaptation (SLO 23).
  2. Define turning point, distinguish it from the climax, and defend a choice of turning point with evidence (SLOs 24, 32).
  3. Distinguish narrator report, interior monologue, and another character’s perspective, and explain the effect of each (SLOs 24, 25).
  4. Evaluate Chopin’s choice to end through a stranger’s eyes (SLO 30).
  5. Make and grade the certainty of inferences about what the text does not say (SLO 32).
  6. Connect the story to women’s economic lives in the 1890s and to today’s debates about self-care and consumerism (SLO 29).
  7. Write a short creative-analytical scene consistent with textual evidence, and two 3-sentence analytical responses (SLO 26).
  8. Compare Weeks 2 and 3 in a first synthesis discussion (SLO 28).

6. Background notes for the teacher

ItemWhat students need to know
Kate ChopinBorn in St. Louis in 1850 (some older sources, including the anthology, give 1851). Widowed in her early thirties with six children, she began writing in St. Louis. The Awakening (1899) was condemned by many reviewers, and her reputation declined until it was rediscovered in the 20th century.
The value of $15One 1897 dollar ≈ $40 today, so $15 ≈ $600 and the stockings ($1.98) ≈ $80. This is an estimate; prices for goods were very different, but it helps students feel the scale.
Department storesLarge city department stores grew quickly in the late 1800s. They offered bargain counters for working-class shoppers and elegant departments, waiting rooms, and restaurants for wealthier women: two worlds in one building, which is exactly Mrs. Sommers’s journey from the bargain counter to the upper floor.
Women and moneyMost married women had no independent income. A mother’s spending on herself could be seen as selfish. Chopin never names Mr. Sommers or the source of the $15; do not let students treat their guesses as facts (Part 8).
Vogue in 1897A New York society and fashion magazine. Chopin published nineteen stories there. Publishing a story about the pleasure of shopping in a fashion magazine adds an ironic layer (Speed seminar, Round 3).
Author’s styleThe anthology notes that Chopin’s third-person narrators reveal feelings through key actions and interactions, that her stories are concise and rich in imagery, and that, influenced by Maupassant, she often ends with an ironic twist.

9. Answer key and model answers

2A Meaning in context

1 things bought cheaply · 2 giving it all her attention · 3 wise and sensible · 4 allow herself to enjoy · 5 calming and comfortable · 6 great comfort and pleasure · 7 worn out from long use

2B Word stress

ab-SORBED · ju-DI-cious · in-DULGE · LUX-u-ry · IM-pulse · fas-TID-i-ous · as-SUR-ance · MON-o-logue

2C Word families

luxurious · indulgence · impulsive · assure · craving · tempt · persistent · fastidiously

4A Sequence (in the order shown on the worksheet)

boots 5 · plans 1 · play 8 · stockings 3 · gloves 6 · sits down 2 · restaurant 7 · changes stockings 4

4B The plan vs. the day

Planned (§1)Actual (§3–§8)
Better shoes for Janie · cloth for new shirts for the boys, Janie, and Mag · a new dress for Mag · new stockings, two pairs each · caps for the boys, sailor hats for the girlsBlack silk stockings · stylish boots · fitted kid gloves · two expensive magazines · lunch with oysters, a chop, dessert, wine, coffee, and a tip · a theater matinee · the cable car home

Who disappears: the children. Spending moves from their needs to her pleasures, and from things to experiences. Also notice the shift from bargains to full price (“The price of it made no difference,” §6).

4C: (1) She forgot to eat because she was feeding the children and cleaning; her life is devoted to others, and she neglects herself. (2) The idea of buying luxury is so far from her life that the clerk’s question feels like a joke or a dream; it also shows secret pleasure at being treated as a customer who could afford it. (3) They give her “assurance” and “a sense of belonging to the well-dressed crowd”: clothes change how she carries herself and which social class she feels part of.

Part 5 Turning point

Mood graph (model): §1 ≈ 7 (“a feeling of importance,” but “gaunt monster”) · §2 ≈ 2–3 (“faint and tired,” “all-gone, limp feeling”) · §3 ≈ 6–7 (“two bright red spots”) · §4–5 ≈ 8 (“How good…!”, “assurance”) · §6 ≈ 9 (“very agreeable”) · §7 ≈ 9–10 (“absorbed it and enjoyed it”) · §8 ≈ 3–5 (“a dream ended,” “longing”). The sharpest changes are §2 → §3 and §7 → §8.

CornerStrengths and limits
A (touch)Strong: the change begins in her body before her mind decides (“her hand had touched something very soothing,” §2). Before: tired bargain hunter; after: a woman responding to pleasure.
B (“I’ll take this pair”)Strong: the first decision and the first money spent on herself. Before: all money is for the children.
C (the impulse)Strong: the point of no return. “Mrs. Sommers did not move in the direction of the bargain counter” and “She was not thinking at all” (§4).
D (the cable car)Weaker as a turning point: it is the ending or climax, where the dream ends. Use it to teach the difference between a turning point and the climax/resolution.

Accept A, B, or C when the “before/after” test is answered clearly.

Part 7 Interior monologue

7A: 1 M · 2 N · 3 M · 4 O · 5 M · 6 N · 7 O · 8 M (accept N with a good argument: the narrator and her mind blend here).

7B models: (1) “I mustn’t rush. What if I buy the wrong thing and regret it?” (2) “The raw silk felt pleasant against her skin.” What is lost: the intensity, the exclamation, her physical pleasure, and her own voice. (3) Any answer consistent with §8. (4) The stranger “saw nothing”: her inner life is invisible to the world, the ending stays open, and the reader, who can guess her longing, feels the irony. Students may also argue that the choice is frustrating; accept evaluation with reasons.

Part 9 Hot seat: listen for the limits of each perspective

  • Mrs. Sommers: pleasure, perhaps guilt; how much money is left is never stated.
  • Janie: knows nothing of the plan; sees only her mother come home (she can guess, not know).
  • The neighbor: knows gossip about “better days”; may judge her.
  • The glove clerk: sees an ordinary customer; knows nothing of her poverty.
  • The gaudy woman: sees a sensitive companion who cries at the tragedy.
  • The man on the cable car: “saw nothing,” only a small, pale face. He represents the outside world.

Part 10 Seminar guidance

  1. Selfish or reclaiming? Evidence on both sides: the plan in §1 and the vanished children vs. “better days,” her self-neglect (§2), and the brief, modest pleasures.
  2. Hopeful or sad? “luckily tomorrow never comes” is ironic: tomorrow will come. The longing in §8 suggests that she knows the dream must end.
  3. Criticism or celebration? Both readings are possible: consumer pleasure gives her dignity and joy, but only for one afternoon. Link to “self-care” marketing and impulse buying today.
  4. Synthesis: Brown’s night ends in lifelong gloom; Mrs. Sommers’s day ends in longing. Chopin lets us inside her mind; Hawthorne keeps Brown’s experience uncertain. Who is freer: the one who resisted, or the one who gave in?

Homework model answers (3 sentences each)

1. Interior monologue: Chopin uses interior monologue to let readers feel Mrs. Sommers’s pleasure from the inside instead of judging her from outside. When she changes into the stockings, the text exclaims, “How good was the touch of the raw silk to her flesh!” (§4). Because the exclamation is her own feeling, we share her rare moment of physical comfort and understand why she cannot stop.

2. Turning point: The turning point comes when Mrs. Sommers’s tired hand accidentally touches the silk stockings. Chopin writes that her hand “had touched something very soothing” (§2), and moments later “two bright red spots came suddenly into her pale cheeks” (§3). The change begins in her body before her mind decides, which suggests that her desire for pleasure is a sudden awakening rather than a planned choice.

11. Differentiation

B2 support

  • Read §1 and the first half of §4 aloud; keep the word notes visible.
  • For 4B, allow students to highlight items in the text first, then list them.
  • In Four Corners, give each corner one starter quotation.
  • For Part 11, offer a first line: “When the cable car stopped at her corner, Mrs. Sommers…”
  • Give the 3-sentence frame for homework: Chopin uses… · For example, “…” (§ ) · This shows/suggests…

B2+ extension

  • Style comparison (SLO 30): read the original §2–§3 online. How do words like “besieging breastworks” and “hectic blotches” change the tone? Was the adaptation a loss?
  • Chopin pair (SLO 28): read Chopin’s very short “The Story of an Hour” (1894). Compare its turning point and ironic ending with this story’s.
  • Twain extension (syllabus): read the opening frame of “The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County” (the first two paragraphs) and answer the syllabus question: “How does Twain’s frame narrator create irony?” Compare a frame narrator with Chopin’s close third-person narrator.
