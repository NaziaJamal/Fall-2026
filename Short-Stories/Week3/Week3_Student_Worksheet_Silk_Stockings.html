Part 5

Literary Lens: Finding the Turning Point

Pairsthen Four Corners

Study: What is a turning point?

A turning point is the moment when a story changes direction: a decision, a discovery, or an event after which the character cannot simply continue as before.

It is different from the climax, the moment of highest tension (although in short stories they are sometimes the same moment). To find a turning point, ask: What is true before this moment that is no longer true after it?

Part 7

Author’s Style: Interior Monologue

PairsControlled → Freer

Study: Whose voice is it?

Chopin uses a third-person narrator (“she”), but the narrator often moves very close to Mrs. Sommers’s mind. Sometimes the narrator reports actions; sometimes we hear her thoughts and feelings directly. This is interior monologue.

Clues to interior monologue: exclamations (“How good…!”), questions, sudden corrections (“no! when she came to think of it”), emotional or informal words, and judgments that only she would make.

At the end, Chopin does something different: she shows Mrs. Sommers through another person’s eyes.

7A · Sort the sentences

Choose N = the narrator reports an action or fact · M = Mrs. Sommers’s own thoughts or feelings (interior monologue) · O = another character’s view.

Sentence (§)N / M / O
1. “What a very small package it was!” (§3)
2. “She handed the girl a five-dollar bill and waited for her change.” (§3)
3. “How good was the touch of the raw silk to her flesh!” (§4)
4. “The clerk could not understand her; he could not make her shoes match her stockings.” (§4)
5. “No! when she came to think of it … she had actually forgotten to eat any lunch at all!” (§2)
6. “She took the elevator, which carried her to an upper floor.” (§4)
7. “It puzzled him to understand what he saw there.” (§8)
8. “It was all very agreeable.” (§6)

7B · Change the voice

  1. Narrator → interior monologue. Rewrite this sentence as Mrs. Sommers’s own thoughts (use “I,” a question, or an exclamation): “She did not wish to act hastily, to do anything she might afterward regret.” (§1)
  2. Interior monologue → narrator. Rewrite as a neutral report: “How good was the touch of the raw silk to her flesh!” What is lost?
  3. Write the missing monologue. Chopin does not let us hear Mrs. Sommers’s thoughts on the cable car (§8). Write 2–3 sentences of her interior monologue.
  4. Evaluate: Why might Chopin show the last moment through a stranger’s eyes instead of through Mrs. Sommers’s thoughts? Is this choice effective?

Clue (§)What can we infer?How certain?

She orders an elegant meal easily, and nobody is surprised by her (§6).
“A vision of the future like some dim, gaunt monster” (§1).
Mr. Sommers is never mentioned, and we never learn where the $15 came from.

Context: women and money in the 1890s

Most married women did not earn their own income, and family money usually came through the husband. A mother was expected to put her children first. Department stores and women’s magazines (such as Vogue, where this story first appeared) were also teaching women to see shopping as pleasure and self-expression.

Discuss: Is Mrs. Sommers’s day an act of freedom, or an escape that changes nothing?
