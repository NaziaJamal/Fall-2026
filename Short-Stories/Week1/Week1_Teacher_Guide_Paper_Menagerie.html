1. The story at a glance

Story“The Paper Menagerie,” Ken Liu
First publishedThe Magazine of Fantasy & Science Fiction, March/April 2011. Collected in The Paper Menagerie and Other Stories (Saga Press, 2016).
RecognitionFirst work of fiction to win the Nebula, Hugo, and World Fantasy Awards.
Free legal textio9/Gizmodo authorized reprint (2012)
Length / reading timeAbout 5,000 words (estimate). B2 readers: about 45–55 minutes in total. Reading 1 (§1–§7) ≈ 25 min; Reading 2 (§8–§13) ≈ 20 min.
Difficulty for B2–B2+Moderate. Language: clear, modern, mostly concrete. Culture: a few Chinese words and historical references (glossed below).
Plot in briefJack’s mother, a Chinese woman his American father chose from a marriage catalog, folds paper animals and breathes life into them. After racist bullying, Jack rejects her language, food, and love, and hides the animals in the attic. After her death, on Qingming, the paper tiger unfolds into a letter in Chinese telling her life story — which Jack must ask a stranger to read to him.
Why it opens the courseIt asks the course’s first question directly: Who is the “American” — and who is excluded? The paper animals give students a clear, trackable motif, and Liu’s style uses simile, metaphor, and personification in a way B2 students can identify and evaluate.

Section numbers (§)

The online text is divided by # marks into 13 sections. Students number them §1–§13 and use them as evidence references (the syllabus requires paragraph or page references).

§1 tiger is born · §2 the catalog · §3 the menagerie’s adventures · §4 new neighbors · §5 Mark · §6 the dinner · §7 the attic · §8 the hospital · §9 the shoebox · §10 Qingming · §11 the tour buses · §12 the letter · §13 ending

2. Content note (please read before class)

This is serious adult literature. Preview §4–§6 and §12 before teaching.

  • Racism: neighbor women describe the child in racist terms (§4); a classmate insults Jack’s mother’s culture (§5); Jack repeats an anti-Chinese slur at dinner (§6).
  • Mom’s letter (§12) refers to famine, the death of her parents (her mother takes her own life), child trafficking, forced domestic labor, physical beatings, and the threat of sexual abuse.
  • Illness and death: Mom dies of cancer (§8).

Suggested approach: Tell students before Reading 2 that the letter describes painful historical events. Read the letter aloud yourself, calmly. Keep discussion on the story’s meaning; never require students to share personal experiences of racism, migration, or loss. The “pass” option is always available.

3. Learning objectives

By the end of Week 1, students will be able to:

  1. Read and understand a complete, authentic contemporary American short story (SLO 23).
  2. Define motif and trace how a motif’s meaning changes across a story (SLO 24).
  3. Identify simile, metaphor, and personification and explain their effect, not only their meaning (SLOs 24, 25, 30).
  4. Use 12 story words and 3 literary terms accurately, with correct word stress.
  5. Make inferences and support them with Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (SLO 32).
  6. Analyze competing perspectives and identify assumptions and bias in characters (SLO 32).
  7. Contribute to a seminar discussion by building on classmates’ ideas with textual evidence (SLO 27).

5. Background notes for the teacher

ItemWhat students need to know
Chinese words in the storykan = look · laohu = tiger · xiao laohu = little tiger · zhezhi = paper folding (origami) · Xuexiao hao ma? = How was school? · Bu haochi? = Is it not tasty? · Fashao la? = Do you have a fever? · Haizi, mama ai ni = Child, Mama loves you · ai = love
QingmingA Chinese festival in early April when families honor their dead ancestors. Mom’s letter can only come to life on this day.
The marriage catalog (§2)In the 1970s, some “introduction services” sold catalogs of foreign women to American men seeking wives. The catalog lied about Mom’s age, interests, and English.
China, 1957–1970s (§12)The Great Famine (1959–61) and the Cultural Revolution (1966–76) caused mass suffering. Mom’s family is persecuted because a relative lives in Hong Kong, then a British colony.
SettingSuburban Connecticut, 1970s–1990s. Jack is born in a Year of the Tiger, which is why his first paper animal is a tiger.
GenreMagical realism: one magical element (the living paper animals) inside an otherwise realistic story. Useful for the Part 7 debate.

Short answers: (1) Zhezhi is Chinese paper folding; Mom breathes into her animals so they share her breath and live. (2) Mark calls Laohu trash; Jack suddenly sees the tiger, and his mother’s culture, through an outsider’s eyes as cheap and worthless. (3) Any three: Chinese language, Chinese food, homemade paper toys (he asks for store-bought toys), his mother’s affection, his own Chinese appearance.

Part 6: Reading 2

  1. Job recruiting season, résumés, interviews, his flight back to California. It shows his emotional distance and his full commitment to an all-American success story.
  2. Keep the box and take it out each Qingming to think of her. Years ago, they wrote a letter to her dead parents, folded it into a paper crane, and released it toward the west.
  3. They stop moving. Jack thinks the magic ended with her death, or that as a child he only imagined it.
  4. He stops Chinese tourists downtown, and a young woman reads it aloud. Irony: he rejected Chinese, and now needs a stranger to translate his own mother’s words.
  5. Any two: born in 1957 in Sigulu Village, Hebei; survived the Great Famine; her parents died during the Cultural Revolution; she was an orphan at ten; she was smuggled to Hong Kong; she worked as an abused servant for six years; she joined the catalog to escape.
  6. He traces the character for ai (love) again and again below her letter, refolds Laohu, and carries him home.

Part 7: Figurative language

§ToolEffect
§1Comparison (close to a simile) + sound wordsThe tiger’s growl is between a living cat and dry paper: it is both alive and paper, both real and fragile.
§2Simile (contempt like wine)Contempt is pleasant and intoxicating to the teenage narrator; the adult narrator is judging his younger self.
§6Simile (Mom like the flattened water buffalo)Links Mom to her own paper animals: Jack and Dad are pressing the life out of her. Motif and figurative language work together.
§7Metaphor / hyperbole (she might as well be from the moon)Emotional distance feels as large as outer space; she is completely “foreign” to him.
§11Metaphor / personification of wordsLanguage becomes physical: the forgotten Chinese enters his body and squeezes his heart, the place where Mom said she feels ai.

Think deeper: Accept either view with evidence. Strong answers notice that the story treats the magic as real, yet the adult Jack doubts his childhood memory (§9); the ambiguity lets the animals be both literal magic and a metaphor for Mom’s breath, love, and language.

Part 8: Sample claims

  • A: Jack’s anger at his mother is really shame created by racism; he attacks the part of himself others attack (§5–§6 mirror scene).
  • B: Dad is kind but takes the easy side: he pressures Mom to assimilate and cannot look Jack in the eye (§6–§7).
  • C: She writes in Chinese because she can only write from her heart in that language; the language itself is part of the message.

Part 9: Perspective notes

CharacterKey points to listen for
MomBeing American should not mean losing her child’s language; wants connection; fears losing her family a second time; silenced until §12.
Jack (teen)Being American = looking and sounding like others; wants acceptance; fears being an outsider; assumes his mother’s culture is inferior.
DadAmerican = speaking English and fitting in; wants peace; avoids conflict; assumes assimilation is necessary and natural.
Mark & neighborsAmerican = white, English-speaking, store-bought; open bias; their voices are loud and shape Jack’s view.
Jack (adult)Begins to see that his American self includes his mother’s story; regret and recognition.

10. Differentiation

B2 support

  • Give the Chinese-words glossary (section 5) with the reading.
  • Allow pairs to split Part 7: each does 2–3 rows, then they teach each other.
  • Provide the CER sentence frames on the board.
  • In the seminar, allow students to prepare one comment in writing first.

B2+ extension

  • Evaluate (SLO 30): Is the ending too sentimental, or earned? Defend with evidence.
  • Find a second motif (breath and air; language; folding and unfolding) and connect it to the paper animals.
  • Optional comparison: read the opening portrait of Ichabod Crane in Irving’s “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” (from the original syllabus). How do Irving and Liu each portray an outsider in a small American community? How do their styles differ?
