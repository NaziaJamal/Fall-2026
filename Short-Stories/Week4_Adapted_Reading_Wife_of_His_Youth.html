Before You Read

About the author

Charles W. Chesnutt (1858–1932) was born in Cleveland, Ohio, to free Black parents, and grew up in North Carolina. He became a teacher and school principal, then returned to Cleveland, passed the bar exam, and ran a successful court-reporting business. He was one of the first African American writers to win wide national recognition.

“The Wife of His Youth” first appeared in The Atlantic Monthly in July 1898. In 1899 it became the title story of his collection The Wife of His Youth and Other Stories of the Color Line. Chesnutt was of mixed race himself and wrote honestly about prejudice, including prejudice about skin color inside the Black community.

The world of the story

The story takes place in the 1890s in “Groveland,” a fictional Northern city (many readers connect it with Cleveland). About 25 years have passed since the end of the Civil War (1865) and the end of slavery.

Under slavery, marriages between enslaved people were not legal, and husbands and wives could be sold away from each other. After the war, thousands of formerly enslaved people searched for lost family members for decades, often by placing “Information Wanted” notices in newspapers.

Key words of the story’s world
the Blue VeinsA social club in the story for “colored” (the 1890s term for Black) people who were mostly light-skinned. The nickname suggests their skin was light enough to show blue veins.
“colored” · “mixed blood”Terms used in the 1890s for Black Americans and for people of both African and European ancestry. We use them only to understand the story; today they are considered outdated or offensive.
a slave marriageA marriage between enslaved people. It had no legal status unless the couple chose to make it legal after slavery ended.
“sold down the river”Sold to a plantation further south (down the Mississippi River), where conditions were usually even harsher. Families were often separated forever.
a ball · a toastA formal party with dancing · a short speech at a dinner, after which everyone drinks in honor of a person or idea.
Part I · §1–§3
§1The Blue Veins

Mr. Ryder was going to give a ball. There were several reasons why this was a good time for such an event.

Mr. Ryder could fairly be called the leader of the Blue Veins. The original Blue Veins were a small society of colored people organized in a certain Northern city shortly after the war. Its purpose was to establish and maintain correct social standards among a people whose social condition presented almost unlimited room for improvement. By accident, combined perhaps with some natural attraction, the society consisted of people who were, generally speaking, more white than black. Some envious outsider suggested that no one was eligible for membership who was not white enough to show blue veins. The suggestion was quickly repeated by those who were not among the favored few, and since then the society, although it had a longer and more impressive official name, had been known far and wide as the “Blue Vein Society,” and its members as the “Blue Veins.”

The Blue Veins denied that any such requirement existed. On the contrary, they declared that character and culture were the only things considered, and that if most of their members were light-colored, it was because such people had generally had better opportunities to prepare themselves for membership. Opinions differed about the usefulness of the society. Some people had attacked it violently as a glaring example of the very prejudice from which the colored race had suffered most. Later, when those same critics managed to get inside, they were heard to argue with great seriousness that the society was a lifeboat, an anchor, a wall of protection, and a shield to guide their people through the social wilderness.

Another supposed requirement for membership was free birth — that is, never having been enslaved. While there was really no such rule, it is true that very few of the members would have failed to meet it if there had been. If one or two of the older members had come up from the South and from slavery, their history included enough romantic circumstances to hide the harsher facts of where they came from.

Word noteseligible allowed to join because you meet the requirements · the favored few the small group with special advantages · denied said something was not true · glaring very obvious · prejudice an unfair opinion about a group of people, often based on race · free birth being born free, not enslaved
§2Mr. Ryder

Although there were no official tests for membership, the Blue Veins did have their opinions on these subjects, and not all of them were equally open-minded about the things they officially rejected. Mr. Ryder was one of the most conservative. He had not been one of the founders of the society, but had joined some years later. His talent for social leadership was so great that he had quickly become its recognized adviser and head, the keeper of its standards, and the protector of its traditions. He shaped its social policy and organized its entertainments, and when interest became weak, as it sometimes did, he blew on the dying fire until it burst again into a cheerful flame.

There were still other reasons for his popularity. Although he was not as white as some of the Blue Veins, his appearance brought them honor. His features were of a refined type, his hair was almost straight; he was always neatly dressed; his manners were perfect, and his morals above suspicion. He had come to Groveland as a young man and found a job as a messenger in the office of a railroad company. In time he had worked his way up to the position of stationery clerk, in charge of distributing office supplies for the whole company.

Although his lack of early education had slowed the development of a naturally fine mind, it had not stopped him from reading a great deal or from forming strong literary tastes. Poetry was his passion. He could recite whole pages of the great English poets, and if his pronunciation was sometimes wrong, his eyes, his voice, and his gestures followed the changing feelings of the poem so precisely that they revealed a poetic soul and stopped all criticism. He was careful with money and had saved; he owned a very comfortable house on a respectable street. It was handsomely furnished, with a good library especially rich in poetry, a piano, and some fine prints. He generally shared his house with a young couple, who took care of his needs and kept him company; for Mr. Ryder was a single man. In his early years with the Blue Veins he had been considered quite a catch, and young ladies and their mothers had made many clever plans to capture him. But not until Mrs. Molly Dixon visited Groveland had any woman ever made him wish to become a married man.

Word notesconservative wanting to keep old traditions and social rules · refined elegant, polished, and educated in manner or appearance · morals above suspicion so honest that no one could doubt him · stationery paper, pens, and office supplies · recite say a poem from memory · quite a catch a very desirable person to marry
§3Mrs. Dixon and the plan

Mrs. Dixon had come to Groveland from Washington in the spring, and before the summer was over she had won Mr. Ryder’s heart. She had many attractive qualities. She was much younger than he; in fact, he was old enough to be her father, though no one knew exactly how old he was. She was whiter than he, and better educated. She had moved in the best colored society of the country, in Washington, and had taught in the schools of that city. Such a superior person had been eagerly welcomed into the Blue Vein Society and had taken a leading part in its activities. Mr. Ryder had been attracted first by her good looks, for she was very pretty and not more than twenty-five; then by her refined manners and her lively wit. Her husband had been a government clerk, and at his death he had left her a large life insurance payment. She was visiting friends in Groveland, and, liking the town and the people, had extended her stay with no end date.

She had not seemed unhappy about Mr. Ryder’s attention. On the contrary, she had given him every proper encouragement; a younger and less careful man would have proposed long ago. But he had made up his mind, and had only to decide when he would ask her to be his wife. He decided to give a ball in her honor and, at some time during the evening, to offer her his heart and hand. He had no special fears about the result, but, with a little touch of romance, he wanted the surroundings to match his feelings when he received the answer he expected.

Mr. Ryder decided that this ball should be a historic moment in the social life of Groveland. His ball must be worthy of the lady it honored and must, by the quality of its guests, set an example for the future. He had noticed recently a growing openness — almost a carelessness — in social matters, even among his own group, and several times he had been forced to socialize with people whose complexions and jobs were hardly up to the standard he considered proper for the society. He had a theory of his own.

“I have no race prejudice,” he would say, “but we people of mixed blood are ground between the upper and the lower millstone. Our fate lies between absorption by the white race and extinction in the black. The one doesn’t want us yet, but may take us in time. The other would welcome us, but it would be for us a backward step. ‘With malice toward none, with charity for all,’ we must do the best we can for ourselves and for those who will follow us. Self-preservation is the first law of nature.”

His ball would help, by being exclusive, to slow down the mixing of social classes, and his marriage with Mrs. Dixon would help to move forward the upward process of “absorption” that he had been wishing and waiting for.

Word notescomplexion the natural color and appearance of the skin of the face · millstone one of two heavy stones that crush grain into flour (being “ground between” them = being crushed from both sides) · absorption being taken in completely, until you disappear into something larger · extinction disappearing completely · “With malice toward none, with charity for all” words from President Abraham Lincoln’s Second Inaugural Address (1865) · exclusive only for a small, selected group

Part II · §4–§7
§4The poet on the porch

The ball was to take place on Friday night. The house had been put in order, the carpets covered, and the halls and stairs decorated with palms and potted plants. In the afternoon, Mr. Ryder sat on his front porch, which a vine growing over wire netting made a cool and pleasant place to relax. He expected to respond to the toast “The Ladies” at supper, and from a book of poems by Tennyson — his favorite poet — he was preparing himself with suitable quotations. The book was open at “A Dream of Fair Women.” His eyes fell on these lines, and he read them aloud to judge their effect:

“At length I saw a lady within call,
Stiller than chisell’d marble, standing there;
A daughter of the gods, divinely tall,
And most divinely fair.”

He marked the verse and, turning the page, read the lines beginning:

“O sweet pale Margaret,
O rare pale Margaret.”

He thought about this passage for a moment and decided that it would not do. Mrs. Dixon was the palest lady he expected at the ball, but she had a rather rosy complexion, a lively personality, and a full figure. So he turned the pages until his eye rested on the description of Queen Guinevere:

“She seem’d a part of joyous Spring:
A gown of grass-green silk she wore …
She look’d so lovely, as she sway’d
The rein with dainty finger-tips,
A man had given all other bliss,
And all his worldly worth for this,
To waste his whole heart in one kiss
Upon her perfect lips.”

Word notesTennyson Alfred, Lord Tennyson, a famous English poet of the 1800s · fair (in old poetry) beautiful, and also light-skinned · divinely like a goddess · chisell’d marble a stone statue · rosy pink, healthy-looking · Guinevere the queen in the legends of King Arthur · bliss perfect happiness
§5The visitor

As Mr. Ryder murmured these words aloud, with a thrill of appreciation, he heard the latch of his gate click and a light footstep on the steps. He turned his head and saw a woman standing in front of his door.

She was a small woman, less than five feet tall. Although she stood straight and looked around with very bright, restless eyes, she seemed quite old, for her face was crossed and crossed again with a hundred wrinkles, and around the edges of her bonnet you could see short gray curls. She wore an old-fashioned blue cotton dress, a little red shawl fastened around her shoulders with an old brass pin, and a large bonnet decorated with faded red and yellow artificial flowers. And she was very black — so black that her toothless gums, when she opened her mouth to speak, were not red but blue. She looked like a bit of the old plantation life, called up from the past by the wave of a magician’s wand, just as the poet’s imagination had created the beautiful women Mr. Ryder had just been reading about.

He rose from his chair and came over to where she stood.

“Good afternoon, madam,” he said.

“Good evenin’, suh,” she answered, with a sudden, old-fashioned little curtsy. Her voice was high and thin, but softened somewhat by age. “Is this where Mister Ryder lives, suh?” she asked, looking around doubtfully and glancing through the open windows, where some of the preparations for the evening could be seen.

“Yes,” he replied, with a kind but patronizing manner, without realizing that he was flattered by her respect. “I am Mr. Ryder. Did you want to see me?”

“Yes, suh, if I ain’t disturbin’ you too much.”

“Not at all. Have a seat over here behind the vine, where it is cool. What can I do for you?”

“’Scuse me, suh,” she continued, when she had sat down on the edge of a chair. “’Scuse me, suh. I’m lookin’ for my husband. I heard you was a big man and had lived here a long time, and I thought you wouldn’t mind if I come round and asked if you ever heard of a light-skinned man by the name of Sam Taylor askin’ around in the churches for his wife, ’Liza Jane?”

Mr. Ryder seemed to think for a moment.

Word noteslatch the small metal lock on a gate · bonnet a woman’s hat tied under the chin · plantation a large Southern farm where enslaved people were forced to work · curtsy a small bow made by bending the knees, to show respect · suh “sir” · patronizing kind, but in a way that shows you feel superior
§6’Liza Jane’s story

“There used to be many such cases right after the war,” he said, “but it has been so long that I have forgotten them. There are very few now. But tell me your story, and it may refresh my memory.”

She sat back farther in her chair to be more comfortable and folded her thin, wrinkled hands in her lap.

“My name’s ’Liza,” she began, “’Liza Jane. When I was young I belonged to Master Bob Smith, down in old Missouri. I was born down there. When I was a girl I was married to a man named Jim. But Jim died, and after that I married a light-skinned man named Sam Taylor. Sam was born free, but his mama and daddy died, and the white folks made him work for my master till he was grown up. Sam worked in the field, and I was the cook. One day Mary Ann, the old mistress’s maid, came rushin’ out to the kitchen, and she says, ‘’Liza Jane, old master gonna sell your Sam down the river.’

“‘Go ’way from here,’ says I. ‘My husband’s free!’

“‘Don’t make no difference. I heard old master tell old mistress he was gonna take your Sam away with him tomorrow, ’cause he needed money, and he knew where he could get a thousand dollars for Sam and no questions asked.’

“When Sam come home from the field that night, I told him about old master plannin’ to steal him, and Sam ran away. His time was almost up, and he swore that when he was twenty-one he would come back and help me run away, or else save up the money to buy my freedom. And I know he’d have done it, ’cause he thought a lot of me, Sam did. But when he come back he didn’t find me, ’cause I wasn’t there. Old master had heard that I warned Sam, so he had me whipped and sold down the river.

“Then the war broke out, and when it was over the colored folks was scattered. I went back to the old home, but Sam wasn’t there, and I couldn’t learn nothin’ about him. But I knew he’d been there to look for me and hadn’t found me, and had gone away to hunt for me.

“I’ve been lookin’ for him ever since,” she added simply, as though twenty-five years were only a couple of weeks, “and I know he’s been lookin’ for me. ’Cause he thought the world of me, Sam did, and I know he’s been huntin’ for me all these years — unless he’s been sick or somethin’, so he couldn’t work, or out of his head, so he couldn’t remember his promise. I went back down the river, ’cause I thought he’d gone down there lookin’ for me. I’ve been to New Orleans, and Atlanta, and Charleston, and Richmond; and when I’d been all over the South, I come to the North. ’Cause I know I’ll find him one of these days,” she added softly, “or he’ll find me, and then we’ll both be as happy in freedom as we was in the old days before the war.” A smile spread over her wrinkled face as she paused for a moment, and her bright eyes softened into a faraway look.

This was the main part of the old woman’s story. She had wandered a little here and there. Mr. Ryder was looking at her curiously when she finished.

Word notesmaster / mistress the enslaver and his wife · sold down the river see the key words box above · His time was almost up Sam was almost old enough (21) to be free of his forced work · swore made a serious promise · scattered spread out in many different places · thought the world of me loved me very much
§7The picture and the mirror

“How have you lived all these years?” he asked.

“Cookin’, suh. I’m a good cook. Do you know anybody that needs a good cook, suh? I’m stayin’ with a colored family around the corner over there till I can get a job.”

“Do you really expect to find your husband? He may have died long ago.”

She shook her head firmly. “Oh no, he ain’t dead. The signs and the tokens tell me. I dreamed three nights in a row just this last week that I found him.”

“He may have married another woman. Your slave marriage would not have prevented him, for you never lived with him after the war, and without that your marriage doesn’t count.”

“Wouldn’t make no difference with Sam. He wouldn’t marry no other woman till he found out about me. I know it,” she added. “Somethin’s been tellin’ me all these years that I’m gonna find Sam before I die.”

“Perhaps he’s outgrown you, and climbed up in the world where he wouldn’t care to have you find him.”

“No, indeed, suh,” she replied. “Sam ain’t that kind of man. He was good to me, Sam was, but he wasn’t much good to nobody else, ’cause he was one of the laziest workers on the plantation. I expect I’ll have to support him when I find him, ’cause he never would work unless he had to. But then, he was free, and he didn’t get no pay for his work, and I don’t blame him much. Maybe he’s done better since he ran away, but I ain’t expectin’ much.”

“You may have passed him on the street a hundred times during the twenty-five years and not have known him; time works great changes.”

She smiled, not believing it. “I’d know him among a hundred men. ’Cause there wasn’t no other man like my man Sam, and I couldn’t be mistaken. I’ve carried his picture around with me twenty-five years.”

“May I see it?” asked Mr. Ryder. “It might help me to remember whether I have seen the original.”

She took a small package from inside her dress, and he saw that it was tied to a string around her neck. Removing several wrappings, she brought out an old-fashioned photograph in a black case. He looked long and intently at the portrait. It was faded with time, but the features were still clear, and it was easy to see what kind of man it had shown.

He closed the case, and with a slow movement handed it back to her.

“I don’t know of any man in town who goes by that name,” he said, “nor have I heard of anyone making such inquiries. But if you will leave me your address, I will give the matter some attention, and if I find out anything I will let you know.”

She gave him the number of a house in the neighborhood and went away, after thanking him warmly.

He wrote the address on the first blank page of the book of Tennyson, and when she had gone he stood up and watched her curiously. As she walked down the street with small, careful steps, he saw several people she passed turn and look back at her with a smile of kindly amusement.

When she had turned the corner, he went upstairs to his bedroom and stood for a long time before the mirror of his dresser, gazing thoughtfully at the reflection of his own face.

Part III · §8–§9
§8The toast

At eight o’clock the ballroom was a blaze of light and the guests had begun to arrive, for there was a literary program and some routine business of the society before the dancing. A Black servant in evening dress waited at the door and directed the guests to the dressing rooms.

The evening was long remembered among the colored people of the city, not only for the fine clothes and display, but for the high level of intelligence and culture of the gathering as a whole. There were a number of schoolteachers, several young doctors, three or four lawyers, some professional singers, a newspaper editor, a lieutenant in the United States Army on leave in the city, and others in various respectable professions. They were all colored, though most of them would not have attracted even a quick second look because of any noticeable difference from white people. Most of the ladies were in evening gowns, and the men wore formal coats and dancing shoes. A band of string musicians, placed in a corner behind a row of palms, played popular songs while the guests were gathering.

The dancing began at half past nine. At eleven o’clock supper was served. Mr. Ryder had left the ballroom a little while before the break, but he reappeared at the supper table. The meal was worthy of the occasion, and the guests enjoyed it fully. When the coffee had been served, the toastmaster, Mr. Solomon Sadler, tapped for silence. He made a short opening speech, praising the host and the guests, and then introduced the toasts of the evening in their order. The speakers responded with good after-dinner humor.

“The last toast,” said the toastmaster, when he reached the end of the list, “is one which must appeal to us all. There is no man among us who is not at some time dependent upon woman — in infancy for protection, in manhood for companionship, in old age for care and comfort. Our good host has been trying to live alone, but the lovely faces I see around me tonight prove that he too depends on the gentler sex for most of what makes life worth living — the company and love of friends — and rumor is wrong if he does not soon give his heart completely to one of them. Mr. Ryder will now respond to the toast: The Ladies.”

There was a thoughtful look in Mr. Ryder’s eyes as he stood up and adjusted his glasses. He began by speaking of woman as the gift of Heaven to man, and after some general remarks about men and women, he said: “But perhaps the quality which most distinguishes woman is her loyalty and devotion to those she loves. History is full of examples, but has recorded none more striking than one which came to my attention only today.”

He then told, simply but effectively, the story his visitor had told him that afternoon. He told it in the same soft Southern dialect, which came easily to his lips, while the guests listened with attention and sympathy. For the story had touched many hearts. Some of those present had seen, and others had heard their fathers and grandfathers describe, the wrongs and sufferings of that past generation, and all of them still felt, in their darker moments, the shadow hanging over them. Mr. Ryder went on:

“Such devotion and trust are rare even among women. There are many who would have searched for a year, some who would have waited five years, a few who might have hoped for ten years; but for twenty-five years this woman has kept her love for and her faith in a man she has not seen or heard of in all that time.

“She came to me today hoping that I might help her find this long-lost husband. And when she had gone, I let my imagination run free and imagined a case which I will put to you.

“Suppose that this husband, soon after his escape, had learned that his wife had been sold away, and that all the questions he could ask brought no information about where she was. Suppose that he was young, and she much older than he; that he was light, and she was black; that their marriage was a slave marriage, and legally binding only if they chose to make it so after the war. Suppose, too, that he made his way to the North, as some of us have done, and there, where he had greater opportunities, he used them, and over all these years became as different from the ignorant boy who ran away in fear of slavery as the day is from the night. Suppose, even, that by hard work, careful saving, and study he had made himself worthy of the friendship and company of such people as I see around me tonight, gracing my table and filling my heart with gladness — for I am old enough to remember the day when such a gathering would not have been possible in this land. Suppose, too, that as the years went by, this man’s memory of the past grew weaker and weaker, until at last the past rarely came back to his mind, except in his dreams.

“And then suppose that chance should bring him the news that the wife of his youth, the wife he had left behind him — not one who had walked by his side and kept pace with him in his upward struggle, but one on whom the years and a life of hard work had left their mark — was alive and searching for him, but that he was absolutely safe from recognition or discovery, unless he chose to reveal himself. My friends, what would the man do? I will assume that he was a man who loved honor and tried to deal fairly with all people. I will even take the case further and suppose that perhaps he had set his heart on another woman, whom he had hoped to call his own. What would he do, or rather, what ought he to do, in such a crisis of a lifetime?

“It seemed to me that he might hesitate, and I imagined that I was an old friend, a close friend, and that he had come to me for advice; and I argued the case with him. I tried to discuss it fairly. After we had looked at the matter from every point of view, I said to him, in words that we all know:

‘This above all: to thine own self be true,
And it must follow, as the night the day,
Thou canst not then be false to any man.’

“Then, finally, I asked him the question: ‘Shall you acknowledge her?’

“And now, ladies and gentlemen, friends and companions, I ask you, what should he have done?”

There was something in Mr. Ryder’s voice that moved the hearts of those who sat around him. It suggested more than sympathy with an imaginary situation; it seemed more like a personal appeal. People noticed, too, that his eyes rested especially on Mrs. Dixon, with a mixed expression of giving something up and asking a question.

Word notestoastmaster the person who introduces the speakers at a formal dinner · devotion great love and loyalty · gracing my table making my dinner more beautiful by being here · recognition being recognized (known) by someone · hesitate pause before deciding because you are unsure · “This above all…” famous lines from Shakespeare’s Hamlet: if you are honest with yourself, you will be honest with everyone · acknowledge to accept or admit publicly that something is true, or that someone belongs to you
§9The answer

She had listened with parted lips and streaming eyes. She was the first to speak: “He should have acknowledged her.”

“Yes,” they all repeated, “he should have acknowledged her.”

“My friends and companions,” replied Mr. Ryder, “I thank you, one and all. It is the answer I expected, for I knew your hearts.”

He turned and walked toward the closed door of a room next to the ballroom, while every eye followed him with surprised curiosity. He came back in a moment, leading by the hand his visitor of the afternoon, who stood startled and trembling at being suddenly thrown into this brilliant, joyful scene. She was neatly dressed in gray and wore the white cap of an elderly woman.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, “this is the woman, and I am the man, whose story I have told you. Permit me to introduce to you the wife of my youth.”

Word noteswith parted lips and streaming eyes with her mouth slightly open and tears running down her face · startled suddenly surprised and a little frightened · trembling shaking · Permit me allow me (formal)
