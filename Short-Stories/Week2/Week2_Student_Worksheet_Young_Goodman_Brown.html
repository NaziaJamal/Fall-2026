Part 4

Literary Lens: Setting as a Mirror

PairsSemi-controlled

Study: What is setting, and what can it do?

Setting = time + place + atmosphere (the mood the place creates).

In Hawthorne’s fiction, setting is never just a background. Dark, mysterious places mirror the inner life of the characters: what they fear, desire, and feel guilty about. In this story, it is almost impossible to separate the forest from Goodman Brown’s mind.

Three questions for any setting: (1) What sensory details does the writer choose (light, sound, color, space)? (2) What mood do they create? (3) What do they reveal about the character’s mind or moral state at this moment?

Part 7

Author’s Style: Symbol & Allegory

PairsSemi-controlled

Study: Symbol and allegory

A symbol is a person, object, place, or color that represents a bigger idea. Hawthorne’s symbols are often ambiguous: they can carry more than one meaning.

An allegory is a story in which characters and events represent moral or religious ideas. Names are often a clue: Goodman, Faith.

Analysis formula: Hawthorne uses [symbol] in [§ + key words]. It can represent [meaning 1], but it may also suggest [meaning 2]. This makes the reader [effect].

Literary Lens: From Topic to Theme

Individualthen PairsSemi-controlled

Study: Topic vs. theme

A topic is one word or phrase: evil, faith, hypocrisy. A theme is a complete statement about life that the story communicates: “Believing that everyone is secretly evil can destroy a person’s ability to love.”

A good theme statement is general (no character names), arguable, and supported by the whole story, especially the ending.

Rank these theme statements from 1 (best supported by the whole story) to 5 (least supported). Careful: one of them is what a character says, not necessarily what the story says.

Part 10

Seminar Discussion

Whole class

Seminar grades are based on the quality of your contributions: depth, evidence, and building on others’ ideas, not on how often you speak.

  1. Dream or reality? The narrator asks if it was all a dream, then says “Believe that if you wish” (§10). Why does Hawthorne refuse to answer? Does it matter?
  2. Did Faith resist the devil? What evidence is there on each side? Why do we never hear her answer?
  3. Brown says, “There is no good on earth” (§6). Is the story agreeing with him, or warning us about people who see only evil?
  4. Brown never questions his grandfather’s whipping of a Quaker woman or his father’s burning of a Native village (§3). Who is outside the Puritan “we” in this story? What does that tell us about the “American” self in early America?
  5. Today: We often learn the private secrets of public figures through leaks, investigations, and social media. Is Brown’s reaction (total distrust) familiar? What would be a wiser reaction?

Discussion moves

  • That’s true, but the setting in §__ suggests…
