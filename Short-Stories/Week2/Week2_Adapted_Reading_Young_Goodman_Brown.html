Before You Read

About the author

Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864) was born in Salem, Massachusetts. His great-great-grandfather, John Hathorne, was one of the judges in the Salem witch trials of 1692. Hawthorne added a “w” to the family name as a young man; many people believe he wanted to separate himself from his ancestor. Questions of guilt, sin, and hidden evil appear in much of his work, including his famous novel The Scarlet Letter (1850).

“Young Goodman Brown” first appeared in The New-England Magazine in April 1835 without Hawthorne’s name. In 1846 he included it in his collection Mosses from an Old Manse.

The historical setting

The story takes place in Salem village, Massachusetts, in the late 1600s, around the time of the witch trials. The people of Salem were Puritans: strict Protestant Christians who came from England. They believed that every person is born sinful and that the Devil was active in the world. To them, the dark forest around the village was a dangerous wilderness.

In 1692, about 200 people were accused of witchcraft, and 20 were executed. Three names in this story belong to real people who were accused: Goody Cloyse, Goody Cory, and Martha Carrier.

Words of the Puritan world
Goodman / GoodyPolite titles for an ordinary married man and woman (“Goody” is short for “Goodwife”). Notice that the name Goodman can also mean “a good man.”
meeting-houseThe Puritan church building, also used for town meetings.
minister · deaconThe minister leads the church. A deacon is a respected church officer who helps him.
communionA Christian ceremony of sharing bread and wine. To be “taken into communion” means to become a full member of a church.
catechismA set of questions and answers used to teach children religious beliefs.
SabbathThe holy day of rest and worship (Sunday for Puritans).
Indian · powwowThe story uses the 17th-century English words for Native Americans and their spiritual leaders. Notice how the Puritan characters imagine Native people. This is part of what we will analyze.

Reading 1 · §1–§5
§1Leaving Faith

Young Goodman Brown stepped out into the street of Salem village at sunset. After crossing the threshold, he turned his head back to give his young wife a goodbye kiss. And Faith, as his wife was fittingly named, leaned her pretty head out into the street, letting the wind play with the pink ribbons of her cap while she called to Goodman Brown.

“Dearest heart,” she whispered, softly and rather sadly, when her lips were close to his ear, “please put off your journey until sunrise and sleep in your own bed tonight. A woman alone is troubled by such dreams and such thoughts that she is sometimes afraid of herself. Please stay with me this night, dear husband, of all nights in the year.”

“My love and my Faith,” replied young Goodman Brown, “of all nights in the year, this one night I must stay away from you. My journey, as you call it, there and back again, must be done between now and sunrise. What, my sweet, pretty wife, do you doubt me already, when we have been married only three months?”

“Then God bless you!” said Faith, with the pink ribbons. “And may you find all well when you come back.”

“Amen!” cried Goodman Brown. “Say your prayers, dear Faith, and go to bed at dusk, and no harm will come to you.”

So they parted. The young man went on his way until, just before he turned the corner by the meeting-house, he looked back and saw Faith’s head still peeping after him with a melancholy look, in spite of her pink ribbons.

“Poor little Faith!” he thought, for his heart hurt him. “What a wretch I am to leave her on such an errand! She talks of dreams, too. As she spoke, I thought there was trouble in her face, as if a dream had warned her what work is to be done tonight. But no, no; it would kill her to think it. Well, she’s a blessed angel on earth, and after this one night I’ll cling to her skirts and follow her to heaven.”

With this excellent resolve for the future, Goodman Brown felt free to hurry on with his present evil purpose.

Word notesthreshold the bottom of a doorway · melancholy sad · wretch a bad or miserable person · errand a short trip to do a task · resolve a firm decision
§2The forest and the traveler

He had taken a dreary road, darkened by all the gloomiest trees of the forest, which barely stood aside to let the narrow path creep through and closed immediately behind. It was as lonely as could be. And there is something strange about such a solitude: the traveler does not know who may be hidden by the countless trunks and the thick branches overhead, so that, with lonely footsteps, he may be passing through an unseen crowd.

“There may be a devilish Indian behind every tree,” Goodman Brown said to himself; and he glanced fearfully behind him as he added, “What if the devil himself should be at my very elbow!”

While his head was turned back, he passed a bend in the road. Looking forward again, he saw the figure of a man, in serious and respectable clothes, seated at the foot of an old tree. The man stood up as Goodman Brown approached and walked on side by side with him.

“You are late, Goodman Brown,” he said. “The clock of the Old South church was striking as I came through Boston, and that is a full fifteen minutes ago.”

“Faith kept me back a while,” replied the young man, with a tremor in his voice, caused by the sudden appearance of his companion — though it was not entirely unexpected.

It was now deep dusk in the forest, and deepest in the part where these two were walking. As far as could be seen, the second traveler was about fifty years old, apparently from the same social class as Goodman Brown, and bearing a considerable resemblance to him, though perhaps more in expression than in features. They might have been taken for father and son. And yet, although the older man was dressed as simply as the younger, and was as simple in manner too, he had an indescribable air of someone who knew the world — someone who would not have felt embarrassed at the governor’s dinner table or in King William’s court. But the only truly remarkable thing about him was his staff. It looked like a great black snake, so cleverly carved that it almost seemed to twist and wriggle like a living serpent. This, of course, must have been a trick of the eyes, helped by the uncertain light.

§3“My father never went into the woods…”

“Come, Goodman Brown,” cried his fellow traveler, “this is a slow pace for the beginning of a journey. Take my staff, if you are tired so soon.”

“Friend,” said the young man, changing his slow pace to a full stop, “I have kept my promise by meeting you here. Now it is my purpose to go back the way I came. I have scruples about the matter you know of.”

“Is that so?” replied the man with the serpent staff, smiling to himself. “Let us walk on anyway, discussing it as we go. If I do not convince you, you shall turn back. We are only a little way into the forest yet.”

“Too far! Too far!” exclaimed Goodman Brown, unconsciously starting to walk again. “My father never went into the woods on such an errand, nor his father before him. We have been a family of honest men and good Christians since the days of the martyrs. And shall I be the first of the name of Brown who ever took this path and kept—”

“Such company, you were going to say,” observed the older man, finishing his sentence. “Well said, Goodman Brown! I have known your family as well as any family among the Puritans, and that is no small thing to say. I helped your grandfather, the constable, when he whipped the Quaker woman so smartly through the streets of Salem. And it was I who brought your father a burning pine torch, lit at my own fireplace, to set fire to an Indian village in King Philip’s War. They were both my good friends, and we had many a pleasant walk along this path and came home merrily after midnight. I would like to be friends with you, for their sake.”

“If it is as you say,” replied Goodman Brown, “I am amazed they never spoke of these matters. Or, truly, I am not amazed, since the smallest rumor of that kind would have driven them out of New England. We are a people of prayer and good works, and we do not tolerate such wickedness.”

“Wickedness or not,” said the traveler with the twisted staff, “I have a very wide circle of acquaintances here in New England. The deacons of many a church have drunk the communion wine with me. The leaders of various towns make me their chairman. And most of the members of the colony’s highest court are firm supporters of my interests. The governor and I, too — but these are state secrets.”

“Can this be so?” cried Goodman Brown, staring in amazement at his calm companion. “Well, I have nothing to do with the governor and council. They have their own ways and are no rule for a simple farmer like me. But if I went on with you, how could I look into the eyes of that good old man, our minister, at Salem village? Oh, his voice would make me tremble on both the Sabbath day and lecture day.”

Until now, the older traveler had listened seriously. But at this, he burst into a fit of laughter he could not control, shaking so violently that his snakelike staff actually seemed to wriggle along with him.

“Ha! ha! ha!” he shouted again and again. Then, calming himself, he said, “Well, go on, Goodman Brown, go on — but please, don’t kill me with laughing.”

“Well, then, to end the matter at once,” said Goodman Brown, considerably annoyed, “there is my wife, Faith. It would break her dear little heart, and I would rather break my own.”

“No, if that is the case,” answered the other, “go your own way, Goodman Brown. I would not let Faith come to any harm — not for twenty old women like the one hobbling ahead of us.”

Word notesscruples moral doubts that stop you from doing something wrong · martyrs people killed for their religious beliefs · constable a local police officer · Quaker a member of a Christian group that the Puritans punished severely · King Philip’s War a war (1675–76) between English colonists and Native American nations · lecture day a weekday sermon
§4Goody Cloyse

As he spoke, he pointed his staff at a female figure on the path. Goodman Brown recognized her as a very pious and respectable old woman who had taught him his catechism when he was a child, and who was still his moral and spiritual adviser, together with the minister and Deacon Gookin.

“It is truly amazing that Goody Cloyse should be so far into the wilderness at nightfall,” he said. “But with your permission, friend, I will take a shortcut through the woods until we have left this Christian woman behind. Since you are a stranger to her, she might ask who I was keeping company with and where I was going.”

“So be it,” said his fellow traveler. “You take the woods, and let me keep to the path.”

So the young man turned aside, but he was careful to watch his companion, who moved softly along the road until he was within a staff’s length of the old woman. She, meanwhile, was hurrying along with surprising speed for such an old woman, mumbling some unclear words — a prayer, no doubt — as she went. The traveler reached out his staff and touched her withered neck with what looked like the serpent’s tail.

“The devil!” screamed the pious old lady.

“Then Goody Cloyse knows her old friend?” said the traveler, facing her and leaning on his twisting stick.

“Ah, truly, is it your worship indeed?” cried the good woman. “Yes, it is, and in the very image of my old friend Goodman Brown, the grandfather of the silly fellow who lives now. But — would your worship believe it? — my broomstick has strangely disappeared, stolen, I suspect, by that witch Goody Cory, who should have been hanged — and that, too, when I was all covered with the oil of special herbs—”

“Mixed with fine wheat and the fat of a newborn baby,” said the shape of old Goodman Brown.

“Ah, your worship knows the recipe!” cried the old lady, cackling aloud. “So, as I was saying, being all ready for the meeting, and with no horse to ride, I decided to walk. They tell me there is a nice young man to be taken into communion tonight. But now your worship will lend me your arm, and we will be there in a moment.”

“That can hardly be,” answered her friend. “I may not spare you my arm, Goody Cloyse, but here is my staff, if you like.”

With that, he threw it down at her feet, where perhaps it came to life, since it was one of the rods its owner had once lent to the magicians of Egypt. Goodman Brown, however, could not see this. He had looked up in astonishment, and when he looked down again, he saw neither Goody Cloyse nor the serpent staff — only his fellow traveler, waiting for him as calmly as if nothing had happened.

“That old woman taught me my catechism,” said the young man. And there was a world of meaning in this simple comment.

Word notespious very religious · withered dry and wrinkled · your worship a respectful way to address an important person · cackling laughing in a loud, unpleasant way (like a witch in stories) · magicians of Egypt in the Bible, Egyptian magicians turn rods into snakes
§5Voices on the road

They continued walking, while the older traveler urged his companion to hurry and keep to the path. He argued so skillfully that his arguments seemed to grow up inside the listener’s own heart rather than come from the speaker. As they went, he broke off a maple branch to use as a walking stick and began to strip off its twigs, which were wet with evening dew. The moment his fingers touched them, they withered and dried up, as if they had been in the sun for a week. The pair walked on at a good pace until suddenly, in a gloomy hollow of the road, Goodman Brown sat down on a tree stump and refused to go any farther.

“Friend,” he said stubbornly, “my mind is made up. I will not move another step on this errand. What if a miserable old woman does choose to go to the devil when I thought she was going to heaven? Is that any reason why I should leave my dear Faith and go after her?”

“You will think better of this soon enough,” said his companion calmly. “Sit here and rest a while. And when you feel like moving again, there is my staff to help you along.”

Without another word, he threw his companion the maple stick and disappeared so quickly that he seemed to vanish into the deepening darkness. The young man sat by the roadside for a few moments, feeling very pleased with himself. He thought how clear his conscience would be when he met the minister on his morning walk, and how he would not need to avoid the eye of good old Deacon Gookin. And how calmly he would sleep that very night — a night that was meant to be spent so wickedly, but would now be spent so purely and sweetly in the arms of Faith!

In the middle of these pleasant and praiseworthy thoughts, Goodman Brown heard the sound of horses on the road. He decided it was wise to conceal himself at the edge of the forest, aware of the guilty purpose that had brought him there, even though he had now so happily turned away from it.

The hoofbeats came closer, along with the voices of the riders — two serious old voices, talking soberly as they approached. The sounds seemed to pass along the road, only a few yards from the young man’s hiding place. But, because of the deep darkness at that spot, neither the travelers nor their horses could be seen. Although their bodies brushed the small branches by the road, they did not block, even for a moment, the faint light from the strip of bright sky they must have passed beneath. Goodman Brown crouched down and then stood on tiptoe, pushing the branches aside and putting his head out as far as he dared, but he could not see even a shadow. This troubled him even more, because he could have sworn — if such a thing were possible — that he recognized the voices of the minister and Deacon Gookin, riding along quietly, as they usually did on their way to some ordination or church council.

While they were still within hearing, one of the riders stopped to break off a thin stick to use as a whip.

“Of the two, reverend sir,” said the voice like the deacon’s, “I would rather miss an ordination dinner than tonight’s meeting. They tell me that some of our community are coming from Falmouth and beyond, and others from Connecticut and Rhode Island, besides several of the Indian powwows, who, in their own way, know almost as much devilry as the best of us. Moreover, there is a fine young woman to be taken into communion.”

“Very good, Deacon Gookin!” replied the serious old voice of the minister. “Hurry up, or we shall be late. Nothing can be done, you know, until I arrive.”

The hooves clattered again, and the voices, talking so strangely in the empty air, passed on through the forest, where no church had ever gathered and no lone Christian had ever prayed. Where, then, could these holy men be going, so deep into the heathen wilderness? Young Goodman Brown grabbed a tree for support. He was ready to sink to the ground, faint and overburdened by the heavy sickness of his heart.

Reading 2 · §6–§10
§6The cloud and the pink ribbon

He looked up at the sky, doubting whether there really was a heaven above him. Yet there was the blue arch of the sky, with the stars brightening in it.

“With heaven above and Faith below, I will still stand firm against the devil!” cried Goodman Brown.

As he was still gazing up into the deep arch of the sky, with his hands lifted to pray, a cloud — though no wind was blowing — hurried across the highest point of the sky and hid the brightening stars. The blue sky was still visible, except directly overhead, where this black mass of cloud was sweeping quickly northward. High in the air, as if from deep inside the cloud, came a confused and uncertain sound of voices. At one moment, the listener thought he could recognize the voices of people from his own town, men and women, both pious and ungodly — many he had met at the communion table, and others he had seen drinking and partying at the tavern. The next moment, the sounds were so unclear that he doubted whether he had heard anything but the murmur of the old forest, whispering without a wind. Then came a stronger wave of those familiar voices, heard every day in the sunshine at Salem village, but never until now from a cloud at night. There was one uncertain voice of a young woman, crying out in sorrow — yet with an uncertain sorrow — and begging for some favor which, perhaps, it would grieve her to receive. And all the unseen crowd, both saints and sinners, seemed to encourage her onward.

“Faith!” shouted Goodman Brown, in a voice of agony and desperation. And the echoes of the forest mocked him, crying, “Faith! Faith!” as if lost, confused souls were searching for her all through the wilderness.

The cry of grief, rage, and terror was still piercing the night when the unhappy husband held his breath, waiting for an answer. There was a scream, immediately drowned out by a louder murmur of voices, fading into far-off laughter, as the dark cloud swept away, leaving the clear and silent sky above Goodman Brown. But something floated lightly down through the air and caught on the branch of a tree. The young man grabbed it and saw that it was a pink ribbon.

“My Faith is gone!” he cried, after one stunned moment. “There is no good on earth; and sin is but a name. Come, devil; for to thee is this world given.”

§7The chief horror of the scene

Mad with despair, laughing loud and long, Goodman Brown grabbed his staff and set off again, so fast that he seemed to fly along the forest path rather than walk or run. The road grew wilder and drearier and harder to follow, and at last it disappeared, leaving him in the heart of the dark wilderness, still rushing forward with the instinct that leads human beings toward evil. The whole forest was full of frightening sounds — the creaking of the trees, the howling of wild animals, and the yells of Indians — while sometimes the wind rang like a distant church bell, and sometimes roared all around the traveler, as if all Nature were laughing at him with scorn. But he himself was the chief horror of the scene, and he did not shrink from its other horrors.

“Ha! ha! ha!” roared Goodman Brown when the wind laughed at him. “Let us hear who will laugh loudest! Don’t think you can frighten me with your devilry. Come, witch! Come, wizard! Come, Indian powwow! Come, devil himself! And here comes Goodman Brown. You may as well fear him as he fears you.”

In truth, in all the haunted forest there could be nothing more frightening than the figure of Goodman Brown. On he flew among the black pines, waving his staff wildly, sometimes shouting out terrible insults against God, and sometimes laughing so loudly that all the echoes of the forest laughed like demons around him. The devil in his own shape is less horrible than when he rages in the heart of a man. So the possessed man raced on, until, shaking among the trees, he saw a red light ahead of him, like the light from a field where cut trees and branches have been set on fire, throwing their glowing red blaze against the midnight sky. He paused, in a quiet moment of the storm that had driven him forward, and heard what seemed to be a hymn, rolling solemnly from a distance with the weight of many voices. He knew the tune; it was a familiar one in the choir of the village meeting-house. The verse died away heavily and was continued by a chorus — not of human voices, but of all the sounds of the dark wilderness ringing out together in awful harmony. Goodman Brown cried out, and his own ears could not hear his cry, because it joined so perfectly with the cry of the wilderness.

Word notesdespair the feeling that there is no hope · scorn strong disrespect · shrink from to move back from something in fear · insults against God blasphemy · possessed controlled by an evil spirit · hymn a religious song
§8The meeting in the forest

In the silence that followed, he crept forward until the light shone full in his eyes. At one end of an open space, surrounded by the dark wall of the forest, rose a rock that looked roughly like an altar or a pulpit. It was surrounded by four blazing pine trees, their tops on fire, their trunks untouched, like candles at an evening church meeting. The leaves that had grown over the top of the rock were all on fire, blazing high into the night and lighting up the whole field unevenly. As the red light rose and fell, a large congregation appeared in the light, then disappeared into shadow, and then seemed to grow out of the darkness again, filling the heart of the lonely woods all at once.

“A grave and dark-clad company,” said Goodman Brown.

And so they were. Among them, flickering between darkness and brightness, were faces that would be seen the next day at the government council of the colony, and others which, Sunday after Sunday, looked devoutly up to heaven and kindly down on the crowded church benches from the holiest pulpits in the land. Some say that the governor’s wife was there. At least there were fine ladies who knew her well, and wives of honored husbands, and widows, a great crowd, and old unmarried women, all with excellent reputations, and pretty young girls, who trembled in case their mothers should see them. Either the sudden flashes of light over the dark field dazzled Goodman Brown, or he recognized many church members of Salem village who were famous for their holiness. Good old Deacon Gookin had arrived and was waiting beside his respected minister. But mixing with these serious, respectable, and pious people — these church leaders, these pure women and innocent young girls — there were men who lived wicked lives and women with bad reputations, miserable people who had given themselves to every kind of dirty sin and were even suspected of terrible crimes. It was strange to see that the good did not shrink from the wicked, nor were the sinners ashamed in front of the saints. Scattered among their pale-faced enemies were also the Indian priests, or powwows, who had often frightened their native forest with stranger spells than any known to English witchcraft.

“But where is Faith?” thought Goodman Brown; and, as hope came into his heart, he trembled.

Another verse of the hymn rose up, a slow and sad melody, the kind that religious people love, but joined to words that expressed every sin that human nature can imagine, and darkly hinted at far more. Verse after verse was sung, and still the chorus of the wilderness swelled between them like the deepest notes of a mighty organ. With the final sound of that terrible song, there came a noise as if the roaring wind, the rushing streams, the howling animals, and every other voice of the wild forest were joining with the voice of guilty humanity to honor the prince of all evil. The four blazing pines threw up a higher flame and dimly revealed horrible shapes and faces in the smoke above the wicked gathering. At the same moment, the fire on the rock shot out redly and formed a glowing arch above it, where a figure now appeared. With respect be it said, the figure looked very much like a serious minister of the New England churches, both in clothing and manner.

“Bring forth the converts!” cried a voice that echoed through the field and rolled into the forest.

At these words, Goodman Brown stepped out of the shadow of the trees and approached the congregation. He felt a disgusting brotherhood with them, through the sympathy of everything wicked in his own heart. He could almost have sworn that the shape of his own dead father was waving him forward, looking down from a cloud of smoke, while a woman, with dim features of despair, threw out her hand to warn him back. Was it his mother? But he had no power to step back, nor even to resist in his thoughts, when the minister and good old Deacon Gookin seized his arms and led him to the blazing rock. There also came the slender form of a veiled woman, led between Goody Cloyse, that pious teacher of the catechism, and Martha Carrier, who had received the devil’s promise to be queen of hell. A wild old witch she was. And there the converts stood beneath the canopy of fire.

§9“Welcome, my children”

“Welcome, my children,” said the dark figure, “to the communion of your race. You have discovered, so young, your true nature and your destiny. My children, look behind you!”

They turned, and in a flash of flame they saw the devil-worshippers. The smile of welcome shone darkly on every face.

“There,” continued the dark figure, “are all the people you have respected since you were children. You believed they were holier than you, and you were ashamed of your own sins when you compared them with their lives of goodness and prayer. Yet here they all are, in my worshipping congregation. Tonight you will be allowed to know their secret deeds: how gray-bearded church elders have whispered improper words to the young servant girls in their homes; how many women, eager to become widows, have given their husbands a drink at bedtime and let them sleep their last sleep; how young men have hurried to inherit their fathers’ wealth; and how young women have dug little graves in the garden and invited me, the only guest, to a baby’s funeral. Through the sympathy of your human hearts for sin, you will smell out every place — in church, bedroom, street, field, or forest — where crime has been committed, and you will be delighted to see the whole earth as one stain of guilt, one great spot of blood. Far more than this: you will be able to see into every heart, to the deep mystery of sin, the fountain of all wicked acts, which supplies endlessly more evil desires than human power — or even my power at its greatest — can turn into actions. And now, my children, look at each other.”

They did so. And by the light of the torches lit in hell, the miserable man saw his Faith, and the wife saw her husband, trembling before that unholy altar.

“Look, there you stand, my children,” said the figure, in a deep and serious voice, almost sad in its awful hopelessness, as if his once angelic nature could still feel sorrow for our miserable human race. “Depending on one another’s hearts, you still hoped that goodness was not just a dream. Now you know the truth. Evil is the nature of mankind. Evil must be your only happiness. Welcome again, my children, to the communion of your race.”

“Welcome,” repeated the devil-worshippers, in one cry of despair and triumph.

And there they stood, the only pair, it seemed, who were still hesitating at the edge of wickedness in this dark world. A basin had formed naturally in the rock. Did it contain water, reddened by the glowing light? Or was it blood? Or perhaps liquid flame? The shape of evil dipped his hand into it and prepared to lay the mark of baptism on their foreheads, so that they would share the mystery of sin, more aware of the secret guilt of others, in both deed and thought, than they could now be of their own. The husband cast one look at his pale wife, and Faith looked at him. What polluted wretches would the next glance show them to each other, both shuddering at what they revealed and what they saw!

“Faith! Faith!” cried the husband. “Look up to heaven, and resist the wicked one!”

Whether Faith obeyed, he never knew. As soon as he had spoken, he found himself alone in the calm night, listening to a roar of wind that died away heavily through the forest. He stumbled against the rock and felt that it was cold and damp, while a hanging twig, which had been on fire, sprinkled his cheek with the coldest dew.

Word notesdestiny what will certainly happen to you in the future · inherit to receive money or property after someone dies · baptism a Christian ceremony using water to welcome a person into the church · polluted made dirty or impure · shuddering shaking with fear or disgust
§10The next morning, and after

The next morning, young Goodman Brown came slowly into the street of Salem village, staring around him like a confused man. The good old minister was taking a walk beside the graveyard to build up an appetite for breakfast and think about his sermon, and he gave Goodman Brown a blessing as he passed. Brown shrank away from the respected holy man as if to avoid a curse. Old Deacon Gookin was praying at home, and the holy words of his prayer could be heard through the open window. “What God does the wizard pray to?” said Goodman Brown. Goody Cloyse, that excellent old Christian, stood in the early sunshine at her window, teaching the catechism to a little girl who had brought her a pint of morning milk. Goodman Brown snatched the child away, as if from the grip of the devil himself. Turning the corner by the meeting-house, he saw the head of Faith, with the pink ribbons, looking out anxiously. She burst into such joy at the sight of him that she skipped along the street and almost kissed her husband in front of the whole village. But Goodman Brown looked sternly and sadly into her face and walked past without a greeting.

Had Goodman Brown fallen asleep in the forest and only dreamed a wild dream of a witch-meeting?

Believe that if you wish. But, sadly, it was a dream of evil omen for young Goodman Brown. A stern, a sad, a darkly thoughtful, a distrustful, if not a desperate man he became from the night of that fearful dream. On the Sabbath day, when the congregation was singing a holy psalm, he could not listen, because a song of sin rushed loudly into his ears and drowned out all the holy music. When the minister spoke from the pulpit with power and passion, with his hand on the open Bible, about the sacred truths of our religion, about saintly lives and triumphant deaths, and about future happiness or unspeakable misery, Goodman Brown turned pale, afraid that the roof would thunder down on the gray-haired blasphemer and his listeners. Often, waking suddenly at midnight, he shrank away from Faith; and in the morning or evening, when the family knelt down to pray, he frowned and muttered to himself, stared sternly at his wife, and turned away. And when he had lived a long life and was carried to his grave as a gray-haired corpse, followed by Faith, an old woman, and by children and grandchildren — a large procession, with many neighbors too — they carved no hopeful verse on his tombstone, for his dying hour was gloom.
