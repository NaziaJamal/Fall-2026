1. The story at a glance

Story“Young Goodman Brown,” Nathaniel Hawthorne
First publishedThe New-England Magazine, April 1835, without the author’s name. Collected in Mosses from an Old Manse (1846). Public domain.
Syllabus placementWeek 2, Unit I “Finding an American Voice (1820s–1850s).” Literary Lens: Theme & Setting. Focus: Puritanism and moral darkness; American anxieties in early fiction. Skills: close reading, seminar, writing. HW: Reading Journal, Analytical Essay 1, Quiz 1.
Class textApproach adapted version (Student Reading Packet), divided into §1–§10. Original ≈ 5,000 words; adaptation ≈ 5,200 words (modern phrasing is slightly longer).
Reading time (B2)Reading 1 (§1–§5, ≈ 2,650 words): 20–23 min. Reading 2 (§6–§10, ≈ 2,650 words): 20–23 min, with you reading §9 aloud.
Difficulty for B2–B2+Challenging in the original (archaic forms, allegory, Puritan context). Moderate–challenging in the adaptation: the language is accessible; the ambiguity and symbolism remain fully intact.
Plot in briefAt sunset, a young Puritan leaves his new wife, Faith, for a secret night journey into the forest. A traveler with a serpent-shaped staff, who resembles Brown’s father, claims to know Brown’s ancestors and Salem’s leaders. Brown sees (or seems to see) his catechism teacher, his minister, and his deacon going to a midnight meeting. A pink ribbon falls from the sky; Brown despairs and rushes to a fire-lit forest “church” where all of Salem, good and wicked, worships the devil. He and Faith are presented as converts; he cries to her to resist, and suddenly he is alone. Back in Salem the next morning, he can trust no one, and he lives the rest of his life in gloom. The narrator never says whether it was a dream.
Why it fits Week 2Setting and inner life are inseparable here: the forest darkens as Brown’s doubt grows. The story is ideal for moving students from topic to theme, and its ambiguity (dream or reality? did Faith resist?) forces evidence-based argument. Its Puritan “we” and its excluded voices (Quakers, Native Americans, accused “witches”) continue Theme I’s question from Week 1.

2. Adaptation strategy (what changed, what did not)

Preserved

  • Every event, character, and the ending, in the original order.
  • All central ambiguities: the “trick of the eyes” staff; “uncertain” voices and sorrow; the basin of “water… blood… or liquid flame”; “Whether Faith obeyed, he never knew”; “Believe that if you wish.”
  • The key images and symbols: sunset, pink ribbons, the closing path, the serpent staff, the withering twigs, the cloud, the fire-lit “church.”
  • Lines kept very close to the original: “My Faith is gone! There is no good on earth; and sin is but a name. Come, devil; for to thee is this world given” · “A grave and dark-clad company” · “Evil is the nature of mankind” · “his dying hour was gloom.”

Changed

  • Thee/thou, prithee, wot’st, ’twixt, durst, quoth → modern English.
  • Long periodic sentences divided; Latinate words simplified where they block comprehension (firmament → sky; zenith → the highest point of the sky; benignantly → kindly).
  • Brief explanations built into the text only where essential (“a thin stick to use as a whip”); other terms are glossed after each section.
  • The devil’s list of secret sins (§9) keeps all four crimes, worded slightly less ornately.
  • The historical terms “Indian” and “powwow” are kept, with a note in the packet, because the Puritan view of Native people is part of the analysis.

Quoting the adaptation: Students quote the adapted text with § numbers. In formal essays they can add “(adapted text, §6)” once. B2+ students can check the original wording of their quotations on the University of Virginia online text.

3. Content note (please read before class)

  • Historical violence and prejudice (§2, §3, §5, §7, §8): the traveler describes the whipping of a Quaker woman and the burning of a Native village. Brown imagines “a devilish Indian behind every tree,” and Native spiritual leaders appear at the devil’s meeting. This reflects Puritan prejudice, and Part 9 asks students to analyze it critically.
  • Witchcraft imagery (§4): Goody Cloyse’s “recipe” includes “the fat of a newborn baby.”
  • The devil’s sermon (§9): it refers to church elders whispering improper words to servant girls, wives poisoning husbands, young men eager for inheritances, and young women burying babies. Read §9 aloud yourself, calmly and without drama.
  • Religion: the story concerns Puritan Christianity and shows religious leaders as possible hypocrites. Your students come from many faiths. Frame the discussion as analysis of a historical community and a literary text, not as a judgment of any religion. Students should never be asked to share their personal religious beliefs; the “pass” option is always available.

4. Learning objectives and syllabus alignment

By the end of Week 2, students will be able to:

6. Background notes for the teacher

ItemWhat students need to know
The PuritansEnglish Protestant reformers who settled Massachusetts Bay from 1630. They believed people are born sinful, only some are chosen by God for salvation (the “elect”), and nobody can be sure that they are among them. This uncertainty created constant self-examination and suspicion of others, which is central to Brown’s collapse.
Time of the storyLate 1600s. The traveler mentions King William (reigned 1689–1702) and King Philip’s War (1675–76) as past events, so the story is set around the time of the witch trials. Hawthorne was writing about 140 years later, looking back at his own ancestors (a link to Theme IV: the past and the present).
Salem witch trials (1692)About 200 people were accused; 20 were executed (19 by hanging; Giles Corey was pressed to death). Courts accepted spectral evidence: testimony that a person’s spirit had appeared in a dream or vision. Teaching point: Brown condemns his neighbors on dream-like, “uncertain” evidence, just as the judges did.
Real names in the storyGoody Cloyse (Sarah Cloyce): accused in 1692, imprisoned, survived. Goody Cory (Martha Corey): hanged September 22, 1692. Martha Carrier: hanged August 19, 1692; during the trials it was claimed that the devil had promised to make her “queen of hell.” Hawthorne places accused women at the devil’s meeting, which raises a question: is he repeating the accusations, or showing how such accusations are made?
John HathorneHawthorne’s great-great-grandfather, one of the most active judges in the 1692 trials. Hawthorne added a “w” to his surname in his twenties; this is often explained as distancing himself from that history, although this is debated.
Quakers; King Philip’s WarMassachusetts Puritans whipped, jailed, banished, and in a few cases executed Quakers in the 1650s–60s. King Philip’s War (1675–76) was a devastating conflict between colonists and a Native alliance led by Metacom (“King Philip”), with villages burned on both sides. The traveler presents both as the Brown family’s “good” deeds done with his help.
Goodman, Goody, Old South“Goodman” and “Goody” (Goodwife) were ordinary titles for married commoners. The Old South church in Boston (founded 1669) is a real place; the traveler covers the distance from Boston in about fifteen minutes, an early sign that he is not human.
Genre and styleA Romantic, Gothic allegory. The anthology’s Author’s Style note stresses that Hawthorne’s simple, precise language carries complex symbolic meaning, and that his dark settings mirror the characters’ emotional states.

Reading packet checkpoints (quick answers)

  • After §2: the devil: he expected Brown; serpent staff; walked from Boston in fifteen minutes; knows the world. The resemblance suggests that the evil Brown meets is part of himself and his family.
  • After §5: Goody Cloyse, the minister, Deacon Gookin. His “choice” is weak: his reasons are about reputation and others, and he still hides because he feels guilty.
  • After §8: the rock as altar/pulpit; pines as candles; a familiar hymn; a figure like a minister; “converts.” He trembles because he hopes Faith is not there but fears she will be.
  • After §10: no: “Believe that if you wish.” The village is unchanged; Brown has changed.

Short answers: (1) That after this one night he will “cling to her skirts and follow her to heaven.” It is ironic because a promise to be good later is used to justify doing evil now; “excellent” means the opposite. (2) It looks like a black snake that seems to twist like a living serpent. The narrator calls it a trick of the eyes in bad light, but the staff later acts magically (§4, §5), so the rational explanation is doubtful: an early example of Hawthorne’s ambiguity. (3) Any three: his father and grandfather never did this (family honor, §3); he could not face the minister (§3); it would break Faith’s heart (§3); why should he follow Goody Cloyse to the devil (§5). Inference to elicit: his reasons concern reputation and other people, not his own relationship with God or with right and wrong.

Part 5 and Part 6: Reading 2

Part 5: (1) His wife Faith has gone, perhaps to the meeting, and his religious faith, his belief in goodness, has gone. (2) A feeling: the voices are “uncertain” and the sorrow is “uncertain”; one ribbon is not proof. He overgeneralizes from one case to the whole world.

  1. A black cloud moves across the stars with no wind. Confused voices of townspeople, pious and ungodly, and one young woman’s voice, apparently encouraged by the crowd. Then a scream, laughter, and a pink ribbon.
  2. “But he himself was the chief horror of the scene” (also “The devil in his own shape is less horrible than when he rages in the heart of a man”).
  3. The rock looks like an altar or pulpit; the four burning pines are like candles; a familiar church hymn; a figure like a minister; converts and a baptism.
  4. Council members, ministers, fine ladies, pious women, young girls, and also wicked men, women with bad reputations, suspected criminals, and Native priests. It is strange because the good and the wicked mix without shame: there is no visible difference between saints and sinners.
  5. “Evil is the nature of mankind. Evil must be your only happiness.”
  6. “Faith! Faith! Look up to heaven, and resist the wicked one!” Immediately he is alone in the calm night; the rock is cold and damp; the twig sprinkles his cheek with cold dew.
  7. He shrinks from the minister’s blessing as if it were a curse; asks what God the “wizard” (the deacon) prays to; snatches a child away from Goody Cloyse; walks past Faith sternly without a greeting.
  8. Stern, sad, distrustful, desperate. He cannot hear hymns or sermons without fear; shrinks from Faith; dies old, surrounded by family, but with no hopeful verse on his tombstone: “his dying hour was gloom.”

Part 7: Symbols (model interpretations)

SymbolEvidencePossible meanings
Pink ribbons§1, §6, §10Innocence, youth, femininity, joy. Pink lies between white (purity) and red (sin): Faith is human, innocent but capable of sin. The ribbon falls from the sky in §6, yet Faith still wears her ribbons in §10, which deepens the dream-or-reality question.
Serpent staff§2, §4, §5, §7The devil and temptation (the serpent in Eden); the power of evil. When Brown grabs “his staff” in §7 (the traveler’s maple stick), he accepts evil’s help.
Forest / wilderness§2, §5, §7The unknown and temptation; the dark side of the human heart. For Puritans, the wilderness was the devil’s territory and the home of the people they feared and fought.
Fire and red light§7, §8, §9Hell, sin, passion; and also church candles: worship turned upside down, an image of hypocrisy.
Basin in the rock§9An inverted baptism, an initiation into shared human guilt. Hawthorne gives three options (water, blood, flame) and refuses to choose.

Think deeper: accept either view with reasons. Strong answers notice that allegory makes the story universal (an “everyman” losing faith) but costs Faith her individuality: we hear her voice only in §1, and we never learn her choice, which connects to Theme I’s question of who is silenced.

Part 8: Theme ranking

  • Best supported: C (the ending, §10: he shrinks from Faith; his “dying hour was gloom”) and B (Brown is “pleased with himself” in §5, judges others, and is shocked that “good” people sin).
  • Arguable: D (the council, the minister, the deacon; but the evidence comes from a possible dream). E (one night changes him forever, although he does resist at the rock).
  • Weakest: A. This is the devil’s message (§9). The story shows that a man who accepts it is destroyed, so it is unlikely to be Hawthorne’s theme. Use this to teach the difference between what a character says and what the story suggests.

Sample CER (C): Claim: Losing faith in other people can destroy a person’s own capacity for love. Evidence: “he shrank away from Faith” (§10); “his dying hour was gloom” (§10). Reasoning: Brown keeps his family, his church, and a long life, yet he cannot love or worship; the damage comes from his suspicion, not from any proven sin. Counter-view: someone might argue the devil is right, because Salem’s leaders were at the meeting (§8); however, the narrator says, “Believe that if you wish,” so the evidence is never confirmed.

Part 9: Perspective notes

GroupKey points to listen for
Goodman BrownPublic: a good young Puritan. Forest: curious about evil, proud, quick to judge. We see almost everything through his eyes. Bias: he assumes he is better than others. Silent to Faith at the end.
FaithPublic: an innocent, loving wife. Forest: maybe a convert, but known only through uncertain voices and a ribbon. Speaks only in §1; we never learn whether she resisted. In §10 she is joyful to see him.
Minister and DeaconPublic: holy leaders. Forest: voices in the dark (§5) and faces in flickering light (§8). Evidence is weak, yet Brown condemns them for life.
Goody CloysePublic: pious teacher. Forest: a witch who knows the devil. She disappears when Brown looks away. Historically, a real woman accused in 1692: Brown judges her the way the court did, on “spectral” evidence.
The travelerRespectable clothes, looks like Brown’s father, says exactly what Brown fears. A manipulator, or a projection of Brown’s own doubts? His claims about Salem’s leaders are never proven.
Outside the Puritan “we”The Quaker woman (whipped), the Native village (burned), “a devilish Indian behind every tree,” the powwows, and the accused “witches.” They never speak. Brown is shocked that his family knew the devil, but not that a woman was whipped or a village burned. The Puritan “we” (“We are a people of prayer,” §3) excludes them.

Responsibility ranking: no single answer. Strong arguments for #1: Brown himself (he chose to go, and he chooses to believe the worst); Puritan beliefs (a doctrine of universal sin makes suspicion natural). Push students who rank “the devil” first: is the devil real in the story, or a voice inside Brown?

Part 10: Seminar guidance

  1. Dream or reality: Hawthorne’s ambiguity moves attention from what happened to what Brown does with it: either way, his response destroys him.
  2. Faith: for resistance, her joy in §10 and the ribbons still on her cap; against, the voice and the ribbon in §6 and her presence at the rock in §9. Her silence is part of the meaning.
  3. “No good on earth”: most evidence suggests a warning: the narrator calls Brown “the chief horror,” and his life ends in gloom.
  4. The excluded: see Part 9. Link to Week 1: who is the “American” self, and who is outside it?
  5. Today: encourage specific, non-personal examples (public scandals, leaked messages, social media). A wiser response distinguishes evidence from rumor and individuals from whole groups.

Model paragraph (B2+ target, 138 words)

Hawthorne uses the forest to show that Brown’s journey is a moral trap that he cannot escape. At the beginning, the path is “darkened by all the gloomiest trees of the forest, which barely stood aside to let the narrow path creep through and closed immediately behind” (§2). The verb “creep” makes the path seem weak and secret, while “closed immediately behind” suggests that once Brown chooses evil, there is no way back. This creates moral dread, because the reader senses that one night of sin may have permanent results. Later, the forest becomes so dark that Brown hears the minister’s voice but “could not see even a shadow” (§5). Here the setting creates psychological dread: Brown must judge the people he trusts using only uncertain sounds. Therefore, the forest mirrors both his guilt and his growing doubt.

  • Read §1–§2 aloud together before silent reading.
  • Keep the word notes visible; use “Highlight key quotations” in the packet to focus evidence hunts.
  • Allow pairs to split Part 7 and Part 6 (A: odd, B: even), then teach each other.
  • Give the topic-sentence frame on the board: Hawthorne uses the ___ to show that Brown’s ___ .
  • In the seminar, allow students to prepare one comment in writing first.

B2+ extension

  • Style comparison: read the original §6 (“My Faith is gone!…”) online. What do thee, inverted word order, and long sentences add to the effect? What is lost in the adaptation? (SLO 30)
  • Evaluate: Is Hawthorne’s refusal to answer the dream question effective or frustrating? Defend with evidence.
  • Context: research spectral evidence (1692) and argue whether Brown “judges like a witch-trial judge.” (SLO 29)
  • Comparison essay (SLOs 28, 31): read Poe’s “The Masque of the Red Death” (from the original syllabus) and write the full syllabus prompt in 400–500 words: “How do Hawthorne and Poe use setting to create moral and psychological dread?”
