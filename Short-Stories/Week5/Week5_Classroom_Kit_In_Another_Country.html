“In Another Country” · §8

Ernest Hemingway, 1927 · original text

§8“I cannot resign myself”

He went back into the other room for the light treatment and the massage. Then I heard him ask the doctor if he might use his telephone and he shut the door. When he came back into the room, I was sitting in another machine. He was wearing his cape and had his cap on, and he came directly toward my machine and put his arm on my shoulder.

“I am sorry,” he said, and patted me on the shoulder with his good hand. “I would not be rude. My wife has just died. You must forgive me.”

“Oh—” I said, feeling sick for him. “I am so sorry.”

He stood there biting his lower lip. “It is very difficult,” he said. “I cannot resign myself.”

He looked straight past me and out through the window. Then he began to cry. “I am utterly unable to resign myself,” he said and choked. And then crying, his head up looking at nothing, carrying himself straight and soldierly, with tears on both cheeks and biting his lips, he walked past the machines and out the door.

The doctor told me that the major’s wife, who was very young and whom he had not married until he was definitely invalided out of the war, had died of pneumonia. She had been sick only a few days. No one expected her to die. The major did not come to the hospital for three days. Then he came at the usual hour, wearing a black band on the sleeve of his uniform. When he came back, there were large framed photographs around the wall, of all sorts of wounds before and after they had been cured by the machines. In front of the machine the major used were three photographs of hands like his that were completely restored. I do not know where the doctor got them. I always understood we were the first to use the machines. The photographs did not make much difference to the major because he only looked out of the window.

Word notescape a coat without sleeves · resign myself accept something bad that cannot be changed · utterly completely · choked could not speak because of emotion · soldierly like a disciplined soldier · invalided out of the war sent home from the army because of injury · pneumonia a serious lung infection · a black band a black cloth worn on the arm to show that a family member has died · restored returned to its original condition

Week 5 · “In Another Country” · Classroom Kit
Station
1

The Title

“In Another Country” is the title, but Hemingway never uses the phrase in the story. He took it from an old English play (Christopher Marlowe, The Jew of Malta, about 1590), where a character dismisses a past wrong because it happened “in another country,” and the woman involved is now dead.
  1. Who in the story is “in another country”? Find at least three different meanings.
  2. How can war, the past, or death be “another country”?
  3. Which meaning is the strongest? Give evidence (§).
Before you leave: write your group’s best answer with evidence in Part 11 of your worksheet. You have 5 minutes.
Station
2

The Machines and the Photographs

Reread: “the machines that were to make so much difference” (§2) · “a theory, like another” (§7) · the photographs and “I do not know where the doctor got them” (§8).
  1. What do the machines represent? (Think about modern science and promises.)
  2. Is the doctor lying, hoping, or trying to be kind?
  3. Why does the major “only look out of the window”? What can no machine restore?
Before you leave: write your group’s best answer with evidence in Part 11 of your worksheet. You have 5 minutes.
Station
3

Medals and Hawks

Reread §5 and §6: the citations “with the adjectives removed” and “The three with the medals were like hunting-hawks; and I was not a hawk.”
  1. What does the hawk image mean?
  2. Is the narrator a coward, or simply honest? Is honesty a kind of courage?
  3. Is the narrator an antihero? Why?
Before you leave: write your group’s best answer with evidence in Part 11 of your worksheet. You have 5 minutes.
Station
4

The Major’s Code

Reread §7–§8: grammar, coming to the hospital every day, sitting “straight up,” “He should find things he cannot lose,” “I cannot resign myself.”
  1. What rules does the major live by? Why might he need them?
  2. Some readers connect the story to existentialism: in a world without guaranteed meaning, people must create their own. How does the major try to create meaning?
  3. Do you agree that a person should “find things he cannot lose”?
Before you leave: write your group’s best answer with evidence in Part 11 of your worksheet. You have 5 minutes.
