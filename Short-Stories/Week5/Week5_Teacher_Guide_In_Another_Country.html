1. The story at a glance

Story“In Another Country,” Ernest Hemingway (1899–1961)
First publishedScribner’s Magazine, 1927; collected in Men Without Women (Scribner’s, October 1927). Public domain in the United States since January 1, 2023 (outside the U.S., copyright rules may differ).
Syllabus placementWeek 5, Unit III “Voices of Modernism (1920s–1940s).” The syllabus pairs it with Thurber’s “The Secret Life of Walter Mitty”; this week uses Hemingway only. Literary Lens: Mood & Antihero. Focus: Hemingway’s iceberg principle, minimalism and understatement, “escape, fantasy, and the modern man.” Next week: Midterm (Units I–III).
Class textOriginal, unadapted text (≈ 2,140 words), divided into §1–§8 with word notes. §8 is hidden in the HTML packet and printed separately in the Classroom Kit.
Reading planClass A: §1 read aloud; §2–§6 in reciprocal reading groups; §7 individually. Class B: you read §8 aloud as the opening “reveal.”
Difficulty for B2–B2+Moderate language, challenging meaning. Vocabulary and syntax are simple; the difficulty is inference, irony, and historical context. Students often say “nothing happens” until they learn to read beneath the surface.
Plot in briefIn the fall of 1918, in Milan, a wounded young American officer and several Italian officers go to a hospital every afternoon to sit in new therapy machines. The doctor promises full recovery. The Italians have medals for real heroism; the narrator’s citations reveal that he got his because he is American, and he admits he is afraid to die. An Italian major, once the greatest fencer in Italy, does not believe in the machines or in bravery; he teaches the narrator grammar. One day he angrily says a man must not marry, because he will lose everything. He then apologizes: his young wife has just died. Three days later, photographs of “restored” hands appear on the walls; the major only looks out of the window.
Why Hemingway this weekMost of the Week 5 focus (iceberg, minimalism, understatement, mood) belongs to Hemingway, and the iceberg principle is the most powerful inference tool students can take into the Midterm. The antihero lens continues from Week 4 in a new form: a narrator who honestly admits fear.

Section numbers (§)

§1 Milan in the fall · §2 the hospital and the machines · §3 the major and the photograph · §4 the boys and the Cova · §5 the medals · §6 hawks · §7 grammar (the major’s anger) · §8 “I cannot resign myself” (the reveal)

2. The text: why it is not adapted

Hemingway’s style is the lesson: short declarative sentences, repetition, “and…and…and” chains, and missing explanations. Adapting it would remove what students must analyze. Support is given through word notes (Italian phrases, places, military terms), the Before You Read page, reciprocal reading roles, and the Taboo vocabulary activity. The packet text has been checked word for word against the anthology.

3. Content note

  • War injuries and disfigurement (§2–§4): a leg without a calf muscle, a withered hand, a face without a nose.
  • Death of a spouse (§8): read it aloud yourself, calmly. Allow silence afterward. Some adult students may have lost a partner or family member; never ask students to share personal losses.
  • Fear of death (§5) and anti-war feeling: keep discussion on the characters.
  • Author biography: the anthology’s “Before You Read” page mentions Hemingway’s death by suicide. The student packet does not include this. If students ask, answer briefly and factually, and move on; follow your institution’s guidance.
  • Tableaux (Part 10): participation is voluntary; any student can be the “director” instead of a statue. Avoid tableaux that imitate injuries.

  1. Read and understand a complete, authentic modernist story in the original (SLO 23).

6. Background notes for the teacher

ItemNotes
Hemingway in ItalyAn American Red Cross volunteer, he was wounded by an Austrian mortar on July 8, 1918, and recovered for about ten weeks in the American Red Cross Hospital in Milan. The narrator is close to Hemingway, but the story is fiction.
Italy in 1918Italy entered the war in 1915. By 1918 there was strong anti-war feeling among workers; officers were often blamed. Hence “A basso gli ufficiali!” (“Down with the officers!”).
The machinesEarly mechanical physical therapy (often called mechanotherapy). In the story, they are new and unproven: “it was we who were to prove them.” They become a symbol of modern science’s promises.
ArditiItalian shock troops given the most dangerous assaults.
The titleHemingway took the title from Christopher Marlowe’s play The Jew of Malta (c. 1590), where a character dismisses a past wrong because it happened “in another country” and the woman involved “is dead.” In the story, the phrase suggests the narrator as a foreigner, war and the past as “another country,” and death as the place the major’s wife has gone.
Iceberg principleHemingway described it in Death in the Afternoon (1932): if a writer knows enough and writes truly, he can leave things out and the reader will feel them. (The “six-word story” used in Part 1 is popularly attributed to Hemingway, but there is no evidence that he wrote it. Tell students this.)
Anthology questionsThe anthology’s “Read and Think Critically” asks about mood in the first paragraph (Part 3), existentialism (Station 4), themes beyond “the futility of war” (Station 2), and Hemingway’s “one true sentence” (Part 13).

9. Activity tips

ActivityTips
Mood WalkStart pairs at different strips. Encourage precise words (melancholy, numb, tense) rather than “sad.” The word bank in Part 1B helps B2 students.
Taboo PairsModel one card first (“fortunate”: “you won something by chance…”). Keep it brisk; this is an explanation activity, not a competition.
Reciprocal readingDisplay the four roles. Give each section a time limit (≈4 minutes). The Iceberg Diver is the key role; make sure every student has it at least once.
Iceberg WallTwo colors make “above” and “below” visible. Duplicate quotation cards are fine: different pairs often find different depths.
Hypothesis BoardKeep the notes for Class B. Students’ wrong guesses are useful: Hemingway wants us to feel that something is missing.
Hemingway EditStudents often keep emotion words (grief, sorrow). Ask: “Can you show it with an action instead?”
TableauxEncourage facial expression and distance between bodies (the outsider, the officers together). Offer the director role to anyone uncomfortable being a statue.
StationsOne group per station; with more than four groups, duplicate stations 2 and 4.
Lens RelayRunners place one card per turn; the team can correct a wrong card instead of placing a new one. Celebrate the finished grid; it is the Midterm study map.

Part 1 Mood Walk: expected words

1 numb, weary, distant · 2 cold, lifeless, empty · 3 warm, tender, comforting (a rare contrast) · 4 gloomy, ominous · 5 pitiful, ironic · 6 shocking, grim · 7 anxious, honest · 8 bitter, protective. Predictions should mention war, wounds, loss, and a cold city.

Part 3 Mood in §1

DetailPossible analysis
“the war was always there”Constant, unavoidable presence; the war is in the background of every moment, even far from the front.
“electric lights … pleasant”A small comfort and normal city life; contrast with what follows. The mood is not only sad, but quiet and detached.
“The deer hung stiff and heavy and empty”Death made ordinary; “empty” suggests the soldiers’ inner emptiness. Repetition of “and” creates a flat, numb rhythm.
“the wind came down from the mountains”Cold coming from the direction of the front (the mountains of the north); the war reaches the city in the form of weather.

Think: the dead animals are an objective correlative: they let the reader feel death and damaged bodies before any soldier appears.

Part 6 Iceberg cards: “below the surface” (model)

QuotationBelow the surface
“You are a fortunate young man. You will play football again like a champion.” (§2)Empty optimism; the doctor’s words contradict the leg “without a calf.” Irony about authority and science.
“You have confidence?” “No,” said the major. (§3)The major does not believe the cure; perhaps he does not believe in anything anymore.
“The people hated us because we were officers.” (§4)The officers are outsiders even at home; society blames them for the war.
“But this was a long time ago, and then we did not any of us know how it was going to be afterward.” (§4)The narrator looks back from the future; the war changed their lives permanently; quiet regret.
“…with the adjectives removed, that I had been given the medals because I was an American.” (§5)Official language hides the truth; the narrator feels like a fraud; Hemingway’s own distrust of “beautiful language.”
“I was a friend, but I was never really one of them…” (§5)Loneliness; he is “in another country” both as a foreigner and as someone without real heroism.
“So we took up the use of grammar…” (§7)The major uses rules and order to control a world that has become chaotic and painful.
“He was a small man and he sat straight up … and looked straight ahead at the wall…” (§7)Discipline hiding enormous emotion; he cannot look at anyone. A clue to the reveal.

Part 7 / Part 8 strongest clues

“A man must not marry” after asking if the narrator is married; “He should not place himself in a position to lose”; looking “straight ahead at the wall”; slapping his hand; asking to use the telephone (§8). Accept any hypothesis connected to a personal loss.

Part 8 Q4: “It is very difficult” is an extreme understatement: the smaller the words, the larger the grief we feel.

Part 9 Hemingway Edit (model edit, 45 words)

He began to cry. He did not wipe his face. He stood up straight, like a soldier, and looked at nothing. Then he walked past the machines and out the door. Nobody in the room said anything. The straps on his machine were still moving.

Compare with Hemingway’s 58 words: he keeps physical facts (tears on both cheeks, biting his lips, head up, “straight and soldierly”) and the one direct statement (“I am utterly unable to resign myself”). He cuts every adjective of emotion. Students often find his version more moving because the reader supplies the feeling.

Part 10 Tableaux: what to elicit

A: closeness of the group vs. hostile crowd · B: the doctor’s eager hope vs. the major’s polite disbelief · C: the boys’ cooling attitude; the narrator’s embarrassment · D: anger as a mask for grief · E: dignity and collapse at the same time.

Part 11 Stations (model)

  1. Title: the narrator is a foreigner in Italy; all the wounded men now live in “another country” separated from civilians and from their pre-war selves; death is another country (the major’s wife); the past is another country (“this was a long time ago”). Marlowe allusion: a past wrong dismissed because it happened elsewhere and “the wench is dead” (avoid quoting the full line).
  2. Machines and photographs: modern science’s promises; false hope; a “theory, like another.” The photographs appear after the major’s loss, perhaps as kindness, perhaps as propaganda; the narrator doubts them (“I do not know where the doctor got them”). The major looks out of the window: no machine can restore what he has lost.
  3. Medals and hawks: the three Italians are fearless fighters (“hunting-hawks”); the narrator only “seems” a hawk to people who have never hunted. He is honest about fear; an antihero whose courage is honesty.
  4. The major’s code: discipline (grammar, coming every day, sitting straight), avoiding attachment (“find things he cannot lose”), refusing false hope. Link to the anthology’s question on existentialism: in a world without guaranteed meaning, people create their own rules to survive.

Part 12 Diamond Ranking: arguments to expect

The major is often ranked first: he keeps coming, keeps his dignity, and admits his grief. The tall boy has the most battlefield courage. The narrator may be ranked high for honesty or low for fear. The boy with the handkerchief was wounded in his first hour and faces life disfigured. The doctor is often last, but some argue he shows a quiet courage in giving hope. Accept all rankings with evidence.

Part 13 model response (3 sentences)

The narrator’s inner life reveals an America that sent young men abroad with ideals they could not live up to. Although he wears medals, he admits that “with the adjectives removed” he received them “because I was an American” (§5), and he is “very much afraid to die.” His honesty suggests that the heroic American image of the war hid a more uncertain, lonely, and frightened reality.

12. Differentiation

B2 support

  • Allow B2 students to take the Summarizer and Clarifier roles first in reciprocal reading.
  • Use the mood word bank in Part 1B.
  • Give iceberg sentence frames: On the surface, … But below the surface, he feels…
  • For the Hemingway Edit, give a target of 80 words first, then 60.

B2+ extension

  • Evaluate the style (SLO 30): Is Hemingway’s minimalism more powerful than Chesnutt’s or Hawthorne’s ornate style, or simply different? Use one passage from each.
  • Thurber comparison (syllabus): read “The Secret Life of Walter Mitty” in the anthology and answer the syllabus question for both stories: two men escape their reality, one through fantasy, one through rules. (SLO 28)
  • Existentialism: answer the anthology question: how does the story reflect the idea that people must create their own meaning?
