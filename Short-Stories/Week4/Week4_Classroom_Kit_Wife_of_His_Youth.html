Week 4 · “The Wife of His Youth” · Classroom Kit
Gallery Walk
1

Charles W. Chesnutt (1858–1932)

  • Born in Cleveland, Ohio, to free Black parents; grew up in Fayetteville, North Carolina.
  • Teacher and school principal by his mid-twenties; later a lawyer and the owner of a successful court-reporting business in Cleveland.
  • One of the first African American fiction writers to be published regularly in major national magazines such as The Atlantic Monthly.
  • He had mostly European ancestry and could have lived as a white man, but he identified as Black and wrote about the “color line.”
Think: How might Chesnutt’s own life connect to Mr. Ryder’s?
Gallery Walk
2

Marriage Under Slavery

  • Marriages between enslaved people had no legal standing. Enslavers could separate husbands, wives, and children by selling them.
  • After emancipation (1865), the Freedmen’s Bureau and ministers recorded the marriages of formerly enslaved couples.
  • Between 1865 and 1867, most Southern states passed laws recognizing these unions; some required couples to register.
  • A couple separated before freedom often had no legal marriage at all.
Think: In the story, Mr. Ryder says, “without that your marriage doesn’t count.” Is he right? Does it matter?
Source: U.S. National Archives, Prologue magazine, “Freedmen’s Bureau marriage records.”
Gallery Walk
3

“Information Wanted”: Families Searching

  • After the Civil War, formerly enslaved people placed notices in newspapers, such as Philadelphia’s Christian Recorder, to find husbands, wives, children, and parents sold away from them.
  • A typical ad gave names, the enslaver’s name, and the place where the person was “last seen.”
  • Many ads were placed decades after the separation. Very few searches are known to have ended in reunion.
  • Today, the Last Seen project (Villanova University and Mother Bethel AME Church) collects and studies these ads.
Think: ’Liza Jane has searched for 25 years. Is her story unusual, or typical of her time?
Source: NPR, “After Slavery, Searching for Loved Ones in Wanted Ads” (2017).
Gallery Walk
4

Skin Color Inside the Community

  • In the 1800s, many light-skinned Black Americans had more access to education and property, partly because some were born free or were the children of white fathers.
  • Some elite clubs and churches were said to favor light-skinned members. Chesnutt’s “Blue Vein Society” is fictional, but it reflects real debates of his time.
  • Discrimination based on skin tone within a group is now called colorism, a word made popular by the writer Alice Walker in the 1980s.
  • Colorism exists in many countries and communities today.
Think: Why might a group that suffers prejudice create its own hierarchy?
Gallery Walk
5

The 1890s: Progress and New Barriers

  • A growing Black middle class of teachers, doctors, lawyers, and newspaper editors lived in Northern cities like Cleveland.
  • At the same time, “Jim Crow” segregation laws spread across the South.
  • In 1896, the U.S. Supreme Court decision Plessy v. Ferguson approved “separate but equal” segregation.
  • The story was published in 1898: a time of real achievement, and of growing danger.
Think: Why might Mr. Ryder feel he must protect what he has built?

§8The toast

At eight o’clock the ballroom was a blaze of light and the guests had begun to arrive, for there was a literary program and some routine business of the society before the dancing. A Black servant in evening dress waited at the door and directed the guests to the dressing rooms.

The evening was long remembered among the colored people of the city, not only for the fine clothes and display, but for the high level of intelligence and culture of the gathering as a whole. There were a number of schoolteachers, several young doctors, three or four lawyers, some professional singers, a newspaper editor, a lieutenant in the United States Army on leave in the city, and others in various respectable professions. They were all colored, though most of them would not have attracted even a quick second look because of any noticeable difference from white people. Most of the ladies were in evening gowns, and the men wore formal coats and dancing shoes. A band of string musicians, placed in a corner behind a row of palms, played popular songs while the guests were gathering.

The dancing began at half past nine. At eleven o’clock supper was served. Mr. Ryder had left the ballroom a little while before the break, but he reappeared at the supper table. The meal was worthy of the occasion, and the guests enjoyed it fully. When the coffee had been served, the toastmaster, Mr. Solomon Sadler, tapped for silence. He made a short opening speech, praising the host and the guests, and then introduced the toasts of the evening in their order. The speakers responded with good after-dinner humor.

“The last toast,” said the toastmaster, when he reached the end of the list, “is one which must appeal to us all. There is no man among us who is not at some time dependent upon woman — in infancy for protection, in manhood for companionship, in old age for care and comfort. Our good host has been trying to live alone, but the lovely faces I see around me tonight prove that he too depends on the gentler sex for most of what makes life worth living — the company and love of friends — and rumor is wrong if he does not soon give his heart completely to one of them. Mr. Ryder will now respond to the toast: The Ladies.”

There was a thoughtful look in Mr. Ryder’s eyes as he stood up and adjusted his glasses. He began by speaking of woman as the gift of Heaven to man, and after some general remarks about men and women, he said: “But perhaps the quality which most distinguishes woman is her loyalty and devotion to those she loves. History is full of examples, but has recorded none more striking than one which came to my attention only today.”

He then told, simply but effectively, the story his visitor had told him that afternoon. He told it in the same soft Southern dialect, which came easily to his lips, while the guests listened with attention and sympathy. For the story had touched many hearts. Some of those present had seen, and others had heard their fathers and grandfathers describe, the wrongs and sufferings of that past generation, and all of them still felt, in their darker moments, the shadow hanging over them. Mr. Ryder went on:

“Such devotion and trust are rare even among women. There are many who would have searched for a year, some who would have waited five years, a few who might have hoped for ten years; but for twenty-five years this woman has kept her love for and her faith in a man she has not seen or heard of in all that time.

“She came to me today hoping that I might help her find this long-lost husband. And when she had gone, I let my imagination run free and imagined a case which I will put to you.

“Suppose that this husband, soon after his escape, had learned that his wife had been sold away, and that all the questions he could ask brought no information about where she was. Suppose that he was young, and she much older than he; that he was light, and she was black; that their marriage was a slave marriage, and legally binding only if they chose to make it so after the war. Suppose, too, that he made his way to the North, as some of us have done, and there, where he had greater opportunities, he used them, and over all these years became as different from the ignorant boy who ran away in fear of slavery as the day is from the night. Suppose, even, that by hard work, careful saving, and study he had made himself worthy of the friendship and company of such people as I see around me tonight, gracing my table and filling my heart with gladness — for I am old enough to remember the day when such a gathering would not have been possible in this land. Suppose, too, that as the years went by, this man’s memory of the past grew weaker and weaker, until at last the past rarely came back to his mind, except in his dreams.

“And then suppose that chance should bring him the news that the wife of his youth, the wife he had left behind him — not one who had walked by his side and kept pace with him in his upward struggle, but one on whom the years and a life of hard work had left their mark — was alive and searching for him, but that he was absolutely safe from recognition or discovery, unless he chose to reveal himself. My friends, what would the man do? I will assume that he was a man who loved honor and tried to deal fairly with all people. I will even take the case further and suppose that perhaps he had set his heart on another woman, whom he had hoped to call his own. What would he do, or rather, what ought he to do, in such a crisis of a lifetime?

“It seemed to me that he might hesitate, and I imagined that I was an old friend, a close friend, and that he had come to me for advice; and I argued the case with him. I tried to discuss it fairly. After we had looked at the matter from every point of view, I said to him, in words that we all know:

‘This above all: to thine own self be true,
And it must follow, as the night the day,
Thou canst not then be false to any man.’

“Then, finally, I asked him the question: ‘Shall you acknowledge her?’

“And now, ladies and gentlemen, friends and companions, I ask you, what should he have done?”

There was something in Mr. Ryder’s voice that moved the hearts of those who sat around him. It suggested more than sympathy with an imaginary situation; it seemed more like a personal appeal. People noticed, too, that his eyes rested especially on Mrs. Dixon, with a mixed expression of giving something up and asking a question.

Word notestoastmaster the person who introduces the speakers at a formal dinner · devotion great love and loyalty · gracing my table making my dinner more beautiful by being here · recognition being recognized (known) by someone · hesitate pause before deciding because you are unsure · “This above all…” famous lines from Shakespeare’s Hamlet: if you are honest with yourself, you will be honest with everyone · acknowledge to accept or admit publicly that something is true, or that someone belongs to you
§9The answer

She had listened with parted lips and streaming eyes. She was the first to speak: “He should have acknowledged her.”

“Yes,” they all repeated, “he should have acknowledged her.”

“My friends and companions,” replied Mr. Ryder, “I thank you, one and all. It is the answer I expected, for I knew your hearts.”

He turned and walked toward the closed door of a room next to the ballroom, while every eye followed him with surprised curiosity. He came back in a moment, leading by the hand his visitor of the afternoon, who stood startled and trembling at being suddenly thrown into this brilliant, joyful scene. She was neatly dressed in gray and wore the white cap of an elderly woman.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, “this is the woman, and I am the man, whose story I have told you. Permit me to introduce to you the wife of my youth.”
