Framework cardsClass 2 · 1 set per triad · cut

U · Utilitarianism — Bentham & Mill (Section 2.4)

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832): the right action produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Only results matter, not character or motive (consequentialism). Utility = the benefit or pleasure an action produces; his hedonic calculus measures intensity, duration, certainty and probability.

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) added rights and individual freedom. Harm principle: we may limit someone's freedom against their will only to prevent harm to others.

Business: cost-benefit analysis for expansion, store closings, hiring, layoffs; risk management. Problems: a majority can oppress a minority; "harm" is never clearly defined; emotional harm is ignored; who counts as "others"?

Read 2.4: "Maximizing Utility," "Synthesizing Rights and Utility," "The Role of Utilitarianism in Contemporary Business."

K · Deontology — Kant (Section 2.5)

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804): motive matters more than outcome. Act from duty and goodwill. (deon = duty.) The categorical imperative is an unconditional duty with two tests: (1) act only on a rule you could want everyone to follow; (2) never treat people merely as a means — treat them as ends in themselves. The question: how do I become worthy of happiness?

Example: Jean Valjean steals bread for his sister's starving family — legally guilty, perhaps morally right.

Business: respect for humanity and autonomy; ethical culture (the text cites Uber, VW, Theranos as failures). Problems: no exceptions for circumstances or cultures; most companies judge outcomes, not motives.

Read 2.5: "Aroused from 'Dogmatic Slumber,'" "Utilitarianism and Deontology," "Kantian Business Ethics."

R · Justice as fairness — Rawls (Section 2.6)

John Rawls (1921–2002), A Theory of Justice (1971): distribute goods by justice as fairness, not by maximizing happiness. In the original position, people agree on rules behind a veil of ignorance — not knowing their age, sex, ethnicity, education, income or abilities. Everyone must agree (unanimity); basic freedoms are included; the rules must maximize the welfare of the most disadvantaged. Example: the person who cuts the cake takes the last piece.

Business: fairness in global, diverse firms; a practical method for training; CSR and employee development. Problems: redistribution can be coercive; ignores history; may reduce initiative. Nozick (1974): the state may not take legally obtained property to give to others.

Read 2.6: "Justice Theory," "The Problem of Redistribution," "Justice in Business."

Northfield Components (fictional) makes parts for medical equipment. It employs 2,000 people at headquarters and three plants. It has lost its largest customer and must cut annual costs by about 10% or risk breaking its loan agreements.

B · Cut pay for everyone. All plants stay open. Staff take an 8% pay cut, executives 20%. Top engineers may leave for competitors.
C · Outsource one product line overseas. HQ and plants are protected, Ridgeway loses 120 jobs, and the new supplier is much cheaper, but its labor standards are hard to verify.
