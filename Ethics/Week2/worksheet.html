5 · Advise the board: Chiquita, 1997

It is 1997. You advise the board of a U.S. fruit company with banana plantations, employees and facilities in Colombia. Armed groups are demanding regular "protection" payments and threatening the company's people and property. Paying such groups may violate U.S. law.

A Pay quietly to protect employees and operations.
B Refuse to pay and stay, increasing security.
C Pay, but report the situation to the U.S. government and seek guidance.

This is a real case with real victims. You may choose a note-taker role.

Concepts you can use

Aristotle: prudence (practical wisdom) · honor · reputation · the golden mean · courage (between cowardice and recklessness) · greed vs. profit · never deceive, even for a good end

Confucius: junzi vs. small person · loyalty to truth, not blind loyalty · quan (weighing options fairly) · righteousness → justice · relationships and responsibility · li (proper order)

From the textbook (2.3): Chiquita Brands International paid "protection" money to right-wing and Marxist guerrilla groups in Colombia from 1997 to 2004. The payments violated the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977). Chiquita said it was extorted and had no choice; it paid $25 million in U.S. fines. In 2007 Colombians sued, alleging the company was complicit in killings and other crimes. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear that case in 2015.

Update (outside source, not in the textbook): In June 2024 a U.S. federal jury found Chiquita liable for knowingly financing the AUC paramilitary group and awarded about $38 million to 16 families of people who were killed. The judge confirmed the verdict in October 2024; Chiquita appealed. Sources: EarthRights International; Bloomberg Law.

Card U · Utilitarianism — Bentham & Mill (2.4)

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832): The right action produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Only results matter — not character or motive. This is called consequentialism.

Utility = the benefit or pleasure an action produces. Bentham wanted to measure it like science. His hedonic calculus looks at intensity, duration, certainty and probability.

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) added rights and individual freedom. His harm principle: we may only limit someone's freedom against their will to prevent harm to others.

In business: cost-benefit analysis for expansion, store closings, hiring and layoffs; risk management.

Problems: a majority can oppress a minority; "harm" was never clearly defined; emotional harm is ignored; who counts as "others" — all stakeholders, or only shareholders?

Read 2.4: "Maximizing Utility," "Synthesizing Rights and Utility," "The Role of Utilitarianism in Contemporary Business."

Card K · Deontology — Kant (2.5)

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804): Motive matters more than outcome. We must act from duty and goodwill, not only self-interest. Deontology comes from Greek deon = duty.

The categorical imperative — an unconditional duty with two tests:

  1. Act only on a rule you could want everyone to follow (a universal law).
  2. Never treat people merely as a means to your goals; treat them as ends in themselves.

The question is not "How do I become happy?" but "How do I become worthy of happiness?"

Example: Jean Valjean steals bread to feed his sister's starving family. Legally guilty — but perhaps morally right, because his motive was goodwill.

In business: respect for humanity and autonomy; building an ethical culture. The text mentions Uber, Volkswagen and Theranos as failures.

Problems: the rule is unconditional — no exceptions for circumstances or cultures, which is hard for global companies; most companies judge by outcomes, not motives.

Read 2.5: "Aroused from 'Dogmatic Slumber,'" "Utilitarianism and Deontology," "Kantian Business Ethics."

Card R · Justice as fairness — Rawls (2.6)

John Rawls (1921–2002), A Theory of Justice (1971): instead of maximizing happiness, distribute goods by justice as fairness.

The original position: imagine people agreeing on the rules of society behind a veil of ignorance — they don't know their age, sex, ethnicity, education, income or abilities. Because you might be anyone, you choose fair rules.

Everyone must agree (unanimity), basic freedoms are included, and the rules must maximize the welfare of the most disadvantaged.

Example: the person who cuts the cake takes the last piece — so they cut it fairly.

In business: fairness in global, diverse companies; a practical method for training; CSR, stakeholder relations and employee development.

Problems: redistribution can be coercive; it ignores history and how people got what they have; it may reduce initiative. Robert Nozick (1974) argued the state may not take legally obtained property to give to others.

Read 2.6: "Justice Theory," "The Problem of Redistribution," "Justice in Business."

Ethics Journal #2

150–200 words. Choose one prompt. Structure: Opinion → Reasons → Framework (at least two) → Counterargument → Conclusion.

The three cases

A · Yijing (2.2, fictional): Bei Li, a merchant who sold farming tools and agricultural products, had three sons with no interest in the business. His daughter Yijing was the most capable and asked to run it. He agreed — but only if she disguised herself as a man when traveling and doing business, because he feared she would be ridiculed and exploited. She agreed, and the business became very prosperous.

B · Jean Valjean (2.5): In Victor Hugo's novel Les Misérables, Jean Valjean steals bread to feed his sister's starving family. He is arrested and imprisoned. Legally he is guilty; from a Kantian view, his motive and his duty to his sister's family matter.

C · Rawls's thought experiment (2.6): Imagine a community where you don't know who you will become. Most people eventually become senior citizens. Behind this "veil of time," what conditions would you agree to now so that older people are cared for and secure? Should you pay into a system that covers people less health-conscious than you?

0 words · target 150–200
Model journal (Prompt A)

If I were Yijing, I would accept the disguise at first, but I would plan to remove it. My main reason is practical: without the disguise, the business might fail, and her father, her brothers and the workers would all suffer. From a utilitarian perspective, the disguise produces the greatest good for the greatest number, because the firm prospers. However, a Kantian would object that the disguise is a form of deception, and that it could not become a universal rule — if everyone hid who they were, trust in business would collapse. A Confucian view is more complicated: loyalty to her father matters, but Confucius valued loyalty to the truth over blind loyalty. One could argue that Yijing has no real choice and that it is unfair to judge her for adapting to an unjust system. I accept that, but I still think she owes something to the truth. In conclusion, the disguise is defensible in the short term, but a prudent leader would build a reputation strong enough to one day do business as herself.

Language bank

Key words in simple English
  • virtue / vice — a good / bad quality of character
  • prudence — good practical judgment (not "careful with money" only)
  • the golden mean — the balance between too little and too much
  • flourishing — living fully and well
  • harmony — a peaceful, balanced order between people
  • righteousness — doing what is morally right and just
  • utility — usefulness; the happiness an action produces (not the electricity bill!)
  • consequentialism — judging an action only by its results
  • duty — what you must do because it is right
  • categorical — absolute, with no exceptions
  • a means / an end — a tool / a goal in itself
  • the most disadvantaged — people in the weakest position
  • coercive — using force
  • craven — cowardly
  • liberal (Aristotle) — generous and open, not political
