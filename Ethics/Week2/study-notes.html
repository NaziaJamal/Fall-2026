Part 1 · Virtue ethics: Aristotle and Confucius (Class 1)

In one line: Virtue ethics asks "What kind of person should I be?" — not "What rule should I follow?" or "What result will I get?"

Aristotle (Athens, 4th century BCE) — Section 2.1

  • Everything has a purpose (telos). The human purpose is eudaimonia — flourishing, living well through reason. Not just "feeling happy."
  • Practical wisdom (phrónēsis, prudence) is the most important intellectual virtue. It finds the golden mean: courage between cowardice and recklessness.
  • Virtue is a habit. We become courageous by doing courageous things, again and again. Action comes before character.
  • Business: Aristotle didn't condemn profit — he condemned greed. An honorable merchant charges fair prices and never deceives, even for a good end. Honor and reputation matter.
  • Three forms of justice: legal (fair laws), commutative (fairness between individuals), distributive (fair sharing of benefits and burdens).
"Aristotle would ask your manager: not 'Did you follow the policy?' but 'Are you becoming the kind of person who does the right thing without a policy?'"

Confucius (China, 551–479 BCE) — Section 2.2

  • Context: a violent, divided society. His answer: order and humanity.
  • Li — proper order, kept through ritual. Modern example in the text: new-employee orientation.
  • Junzi — the exemplary person: balanced, self-controlled, humane. The opposite is the "small" person, obsessed with power and rivalries.
  • Five great relationships: parent/child, husband/wife, elder/younger sibling, master/apprentice, ruler/subject. Three are in the family — the school of character.
  • Loyalty to truth, not blind loyalty. You must give your ruler honest advice, even when it's unwelcome.
  • Quan — weighing options fairly, like a counterweight on a scale. Righteousness becomes justice in business deals.

Common confusions: "liberal" in Aristotle means generous, not political · eudaimonia ≠ pleasure · Confucius did not demand obedience — he rejected blind loyalty.

Part 2 · Results, duty and fairness (Class 2)

In one line: Utilitarians judge the results; Kant judges the motive and duty; Rawls judges the fairness of the system.

Utilitarianism — Bentham & Mill (Section 2.4)

  • Bentham (1748–1832): the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Only consequences count (consequentialism). Utility can be measured: intensity, duration, certainty, probability (the hedonic calculus).
  • Mill (1806–1873): adds rights and freedom. Harm principle: limit someone's freedom only to prevent harm to others. On Liberty (1859).
  • In business: cost-benefit analysis — expansions, closures, layoffs.
  • Weak points: the majority can crush a minority; "harm" is never clearly defined.
"Jon tried to kill and missed; Matt only meant to scare and killed. A pure utilitarian looks at the body count — so Matt is worse."

Deontology — Kant (Section 2.5)

  • Kant (1724–1804): what matters is duty and goodwill, not the outcome.
  • Categorical imperative — two tests: (1) Could everyone follow my rule? (2) Am I treating anyone merely as a means?
  • Not "How do I become happy?" but "How do I become worthy of happiness?"
  • Example: Jean Valjean steals bread for his sister's starving family — legally guilty, morally perhaps right.
  • Weak points: no exceptions for circumstances or cultures; most companies judge results, not motives.

Justice as fairness — Rawls (Section 2.6)

  • Rawls (1921–2002), A Theory of Justice (1971). Fairness, not maximum happiness, should guide how goods are shared.
  • Veil of ignorance: choose the rules without knowing who you will be (age, income, sex, ability…). Everyone must agree. Protect the most disadvantaged.
  • Cake example: the person who cuts takes the last piece.
  • Critics: redistribution can be coercive; Robert Nozick (1974, Anarchy, State, and Utopia) — the state may not take legally obtained property to give to others.
  • In business: HR, diverse global teams, CSR.

Common confusions: "utility" ≠ electricity bill · "categorical" = absolute · "means" = a tool · Kant isn't against happiness — he puts duty first.

Textbook errors to know: the book gives Bentham's death as 1842 (it was 1832) and calls Nozick's 1974 book A Theory of Entitlement (the real title is Anarchy, State, and Utopia).
