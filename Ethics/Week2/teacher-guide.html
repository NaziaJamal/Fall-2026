Week 2 at a glance

In Week 1 students met three lenses — consequences, duty, character. This week they meet the people behind them, and leave able to say "A Kantian would say X, but a utilitarian would say Y — and here's why they disagree."

Class 1 · "Who Should We Be?"Virtue ethics, West and East: Aristotle and Confucius (Sections 2.1–2.3). Trustworthy colleagues → golden mean → jigsaw → Chiquita board advice.
Class 2 · "Results, Rules, or Fairness?"Bentham & Mill, Kant, Rawls (Sections 2.4–2.6). Jon & Matt → framework triads → plant-closing case behind the veil → timeline → debate.
The threadClass 1 judges the person. Class 2 judges the action (results, duty) and the system (fairness). The debate asks whether 2,500-year-old answers still work.

Learning goals

By the end of Week 2, students can:

  1. Explain virtue ethics and find the golden mean (deficiency – virtue – excess) for a workplace virtue.
  2. Compare Aristotle and Confucius: aim (happiness vs. harmony), ideal person (magnanimous person vs. junzi), locus of ethics (individual vs. family).
  3. State the central question of utilitarianism, deontology and justice as fairness, and the test each one uses.
  4. Apply three frameworks to one business case and explain why they reach different conclusions.
  5. Report and compare viewpoints using B2+ language: "A Kantian would object that…", "If I were behind the veil, I would…"

Big ethical questions

  • Should we judge a businessperson by who they are, what they intended, what happened, or whether the system was fair?
  • Is the center of ethics the individual (Aristotle) or our relationships (Confucius)? Which does your workplace assume?
  • When is loyalty a virtue, and when is it "blind"?
  • Can 2,500-year-old ideas guide decisions about AI, global supply chains and high-frequency trading?
  • Is there one ethic for every culture, or only a shared method?

Engage 9:00–9:14 · The colleague you'd trust

PurposeStudents discover that they already judge people by character. That is virtue ethics before the name. It draws directly on their professional experience. GroupingIndividual → pairs → whole class MaterialsWorksheet §1; board with two columns: "Built trust" / "Destroyed trust"
  1. (3 min) Individually: Think of the most trustworthy colleague you have ever worked with. Write three things they did — specific behaviors, not adjectives. Then one thing a colleague did that destroyed your trust.
  2. (4 min) Pairs compare. Push them: Would this behavior build trust in every workplace you've known, or only some?
  3. (5 min) Harvest 8–10 behaviors onto the board. As you write each one, ask the class for the quality behind it (e.g., "admitted her mistake to the client" → honesty, courage). Write the quality in gold/colored marker.
  4. (2 min) Point at the colored words.
"Look at what you just did. You didn't judge your colleagues by a rule or by a spreadsheet. You judged who they were. Twenty-five centuries ago, two men on opposite sides of the world built entire ethical systems on lists like this one. Today we meet them."

Debrief question (quick): In Week 1 we had three lenses — results, duty, character. Which lens were you using just now?

Expected outcome: A board of 8–10 virtues in students' own words, used again at 9:14 and in the exit ticket.

Language: "She was the kind of person who…" · "What made him trustworthy was…" · "I'd trust her with…" · "In my field, that would be seen as…"
Cross-cultural opportunity: Listen for disagreement about the same behavior — e.g., a colleague who openly criticized the boss. Some will call it courage, others disloyalty. Write it on the board with a "?" and say: "Hold that — Confucius has an answer."

Study 9:14–9:26 · Find the golden mean

PurposeTeach the golden mean through doing, not definition: every virtue sits between two vices — a deficiency and an excess. GroupingGroups of 3 MaterialsGolden-mean card set (handouts p.1, cut): 6 virtue cards + 12 extreme cards; worksheet §2 (self-check)
"Aristotle said courage is not the opposite of fear. Courage sits between two failures — too little and too much. Your job: find the two failures for each virtue."
  1. (1 min) Model one on the board: cowardice ← COURAGE → recklessness.
  2. (5 min) Groups build five more triplets from the cards.
  3. (2 min) Groups check with the worksheet self-check button.
  4. (4 min) Twist: each group picks Candor or Loyalty. "Where does the line sit between virtue and excess in your profession? In a workplace you know abroad? Give one real example." Take two or three answers.

Answer key

Deficiency (too little)VirtueExcess (too much)
CowardiceCourageRecklessness
StinginessGenerosityWastefulness
DisloyaltyLoyaltyBlind loyalty
EvasivenessCandorTactlessness
ComplacencyAmbitionGreed
IrritabilityPatiencePassivity
Source note: The golden mean, courage, generosity ("liberality") and patience are from 2.1. "Blind loyalty" is from 2.2 — Confucius opposed it. "Greed" as the excess that Aristotle condemned (rather than profit) is from 2.1. Candor and ambition are workplace adaptations for practice; say so if asked.

Debrief: Is "greed" just too much ambition, or something different? Can a whole company culture sit at the "excess" end?

Language: "There's a fine line between ___ and ___." · "Taken too far, ___ becomes ___." · "In my experience, ___ is valued more in…" · "That would come across as…"

Activate 10:08–10:32 · Advise the board: Chiquita, 1997

PurposeUse virtue ethics on a real, hard case (2.3 "What Would You Do?") — advising a board, not summarizing a theory. GroupingGroups of 3–4; half the groups are "Aristotle Advisory," half "Confucius Advisory" MaterialsBoard brief (handouts p.3; worksheet §5)
Sensitivity: This case involves killings by armed groups in Colombia. A student may have a personal or family connection. Before starting, say: "This is a real case with real victims. If anyone prefers to take a note-taker role, that's fine." Never ask a Colombian student to explain the conflict.

The brief (what students see — the outcome is hidden)

It is 1997. You advise the board of a U.S. fruit company with banana plantations, employees and facilities in Colombia. Armed groups are demanding regular "protection" payments and threatening the company's people and property. Paying such groups may violate U.S. law. The board is considering four options:

  1. Pay quietly to protect employees and operations.
  2. Refuse to pay and stay, increasing security.
  3. Pay, but report the situation to the U.S. government and seek guidance.
  4. Withdraw from the region (sell or close the operation).
  1. (2 min) Read the brief together. Assign advisory firms.
  2. (9 min) Groups prepare a 60-second recommendation. It must use at least three concepts from their thinker. (Aristotle: prudence, honor, golden mean, greed vs. profit, reputation, courage. Confucius: junzi, loyalty to truth, quan, righteousness/justice, relationships, li.) It must also name one risk of their own recommendation.
  3. (7 min) Pitches, 60 seconds each. The "board" (everyone else) may ask one question per group.
  4. (3 min) Reveal (slide): what actually happened.
  5. (3 min) Debrief with the textbook's own question.

The reveal (textbook + outside update)

  • Textbook (2.3): Chiquita Brands International paid protection money to right-wing and Marxist guerrilla groups in Colombia from 1997 to 2004 to protect employees, plantations and facilities. The payments violated the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977). Chiquita said it was extorted and had no choice, and paid $25 million in U.S. fines. In 2007 Colombians sued under the Alien Tort Claims Act, alleging complicity in killings, torture and disappearances. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear that case in 2015.
  • Outside update (not in the textbook): In June 2024 a U.S. federal jury found Chiquita liable for knowingly financing the AUC paramilitary group, a designated terrorist organization (over $1.7 million, 1997–2004), and awarded about $38 million to 16 plaintiffs whose relatives were killed. In October 2024 the judge rejected Chiquita's request to cut the award and entered judgment (EarthRights International). Chiquita has appealed (Bloomberg Law). Check for newer rulings before class.

Debrief questions:

  • The textbook asks: were the executives prudent, craven, or deceitful? (craven = cowardly) Defend one word.
  • Option C mixes honesty with payment. Does telling the truth make paying acceptable?
  • Who are the family here, in Confucian terms — employees, shareholders, local communities?
  • Did any group recommend withdrawal? Who would have paid the price for that "clean" choice?
Language for pitches: "We would advise the board to…, on the grounds that…" · "A prudent leader would…" · "The honorable course is…" · "The risk we acknowledge is…" · "We considered ___ but rejected it because…"

Reflect 10:32–10:40 · Exit ticket & bridge

  1. (5 min) Exit ticket (worksheet §6 or paper slip): (1) One virtue in your profession, with its deficiency and its excess. (2) One real difference between Aristotle and Confucius. (3) One question you still have.
  2. (3 min) Bridge. Show the Jon & Matt slide. Hand out vote slips.

Jon shoots at a man, meaning to kill him, and misses. Matt fires a shot only to scare a man, and kills him by accident. Who deserves the harsher punishment? Engage 11:10–11:20 · Bridge: Jon & Matt

  1. (2 min) Count the votes aloud. Write the result.
  2. (4 min) Pairs: Who is more guilty? Who did more harm? Are those the same question?
  3. (4 min) Whole class. Then reveal: the law usually punishes Matt more. Ask: "Which lens is the law using? Which lens were you using?"

  1. "Never use your employees merely as tools for profit." → 2

  3. "Design the bonus policy as if you didn't know whether you'd be a manager or a cleaner." → 3

Activate 11:45–12:10 · Northfield Components: three verdicts

PurposeDifferent frameworks, same facts, different conclusions — the core skill of the course. The veil-of-ignorance draw makes Rawls felt, not just understood. GroupingSame triads → whole class MaterialsNorthfield case (handouts p.6; worksheet §9); identity cards (handouts p.7, cut and shuffled in an envelope)

The case (fictional)

Northfield Components makes parts for medical equipment. It employs 2,000 people at headquarters and three plants. It has lost its largest customer and must cut annual costs by about 10% or risk breaking its loan agreements. The board has three options:

  1. Close the Ridgeway plant. 300 jobs lost. Ridgeway is a small town, and the plant is its largest employer. Everyone else keeps their job and pay.
  2. Cut pay for everyone. All plants stay open. Staff take an 8% pay cut, executives 20%. Top engineers may leave for competitors.
  3. Outsource one product line overseas. HQ and plants are protected, Ridgeway loses 120 jobs, and the new supplier is much cheaper, but its labor standards are hard to verify.
  1. (2 min) Individually, choose A, B or C — gut answer. Hands up; tally on the board ("Before").
  2. (8 min) Triads: each member gives the verdict of their framework, with reasons (2 min each), and the others challenge with one question. Then triads note where frameworks agree and disagree.
  3. (8 min) Veil of ignorance.
    • Say: "Vote again — but this time you will draw a random identity card after you vote, and you will live with the result as that person."
    • Secret vote on worksheet; then everyone draws a card and reads it.
    • Ask: "Stand up if you would now change your vote." Count. Re-tally on the board ("After the veil").
  4. (7 min) Debrief.

Model verdicts (no single right answer)

FrameworkLikely verdictReasoning
UtilitarianOften A or CFewest people harmed; company survives; protects most jobs. Mill would ask: who counts as "others"? Overseas workers are stakeholders too.
KantianOften BShares the burden; doesn't use Ridgeway workers merely as a means to save others. Could you universalize "close the weakest town's plant"? C is questionable if the supplier's workers are treated merely as means.
RawlsianOften BBehind the veil, you might be the 54-year-old machinist in Ridgeway; protect the most disadvantaged. But an engineer card shows B also has costs.
Virtue (from Class 1)DependsWhat would a prudent, honorable leader do? What would a junzi owe the Ridgeway "family"?

Debrief questions:

  • How many people changed their vote after the veil? What does that tell us about the first vote?
  • Did any two frameworks agree for different reasons?
  • Which framework would a real board most likely use? Which one should it use?
  • What information is missing? (Can Ridgeway workers transfer? What is the supplier's record?) — reward "We need more information."
Language: "If I were behind the veil, I'd choose ___, because I might turn out to be…" · "On utilitarian grounds, A is defensible; however, …" · "Both approaches lead to B, but for different reasons." · "Now that I know I'm ___, I would…"

PurposeSyllabus debate. Students build and defend an argument with textbook evidence, respond to rebuttals, and show changed thinking. GroupingOpinion line → two teams (Proposition / Opposition) MaterialsFloor line (1–10); debate evidence cards (handouts p.9); worksheet §11 prep sheet

Motion: "This house believes that the ethics of Aristotle and Confucius are more useful to today's business leaders than modern rules and regulations."

  1. (2 min) Opinion line. Students stand from 1 (strongly disagree) to 10 (strongly agree). Ask two people at each end: "Why there?"
  2. (1 min) Fold the line so the 10s face the 1s; split into Proposition and Opposition. This mixes opinions — some students will argue against their own view. Say so: that's the skill.
  3. (6 min) Prep with evidence cards. Assign roles: Opener, Evidence 1, Evidence 2, Rebuttal, Closer (doubling up is fine).
  4. (11 min) Debate:
    • Proposition opening — 90 sec · Opposition opening — 90 sec
    • Open floor — 5 min, alternating sides. Anyone may stand and say "Point of information" — the speaker can accept or decline.
    • Opposition closing — 1 min · Proposition closing — 1 min
  5. (4 min) Re-stand on the line by personal opinion. "Who moved? What argument moved you? What would make you move further?"

Words students may misunderstand

  • liberal — in Aristotle it means generous, open, not political (the textbook makes this point).
  • utility — not the electricity or water bill; here it means usefulness/happiness.
  • prudence / prudent — wise judgment, not "prudish" and not only "careful with money."
  • categorical — absolute, not "about categories."
  • means — a method or tool (plural and singular are the same), not "money" and not the verb "mean."
  • virtue / virtual — completely different words.
  • justice — fairness in general, not only courts and police.

Model journal (Prompt A, ~190 words)

If I were Yijing, I would accept the disguise at first, but I would plan to remove it. My main reason is practical: without the disguise, the business might fail, and her father, her brothers and the workers would all suffer. From a utilitarian perspective, the disguise produces the greatest good for the greatest number, because the firm prospers. However, a Kantian would object that the disguise is a form of deception, and that it could not become a universal rule — if everyone hid who they were, trust in business would collapse. A Confucian view is more complicated: loyalty to her father matters, but Confucius valued loyalty to the truth over blind loyalty. One could argue that Yijing has no real choice and that it is unfair to judge her for adapting to an unjust system. I accept that, but I still think she owes something to the truth. In conclusion, the disguise is defensible in the short term, but a prudent leader would build a reputation strong enough to one day do business as herself.

Chapter 2 items for Quiz 1 (with key)

  1. For Aristotle, the human telos is ___. (eudaimonia / flourishing)
  2. Name the intellectual virtue Aristotle considered most important, which helps find the golden mean. (phrónēsis / practical wisdom / prudence)
  3. True or false: Aristotle condemned profit. (False — he condemned greed.)
  4. In Confucian ethics, the exemplary, balanced, humane person is called a ___. (junzi)
  5. Which statement about Confucius and loyalty is correct? (a) loyalty to the ruler above all (b) loyalty to truth, including giving unwelcome honest advice (c) loyalty only to family. (b)
  6. According to 2.3, the locus of ethics is the ___ for Aristotle and the ___ for Confucius. (individual; family)
  7. Bentham's principle: the greatest ___ for the greatest ___. (happiness; number)
  8. Mill's harm principle says power over someone against their will is justified only to ___. (prevent harm to others)
  9. Give the two parts of Kant's categorical imperative described in 2.5. (act only on a rule you could will as universal law; never treat others merely as a means)
  10. In Rawls's theory, why do people choose behind a veil of ignorance? (Not knowing their own position reduces bias and self-interest, so they choose fair rules.)
  11. Short case: A manager hides a product defect because recalling it would cost jobs. Give one utilitarian and one Kantian assessment. (Model: U might weigh jobs saved against harm to customers and likely conclude the harm outweighs; K would say deceiving customers treats them merely as a means and can't be universalized — wrong regardless of outcome.)
