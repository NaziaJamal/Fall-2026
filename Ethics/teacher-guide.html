Week 4 at a glance

Last week students learned to see and rank stakeholders. This week they meet the three that are easiest to ignore and hardest to satisfy: society, the environment and government. They leave able to say "That claim is greenwashing, because…" and "This problem needs the law, not just good intentions, because…"

Class 1 · "Who Pays for the Planet?"Quick quiz review → a fishing game (tragedy of the commons) → You be the judge: three real court cases → gallery walk of six global environmental cases.
Class 2 · "Green or Greenwash — and Who Decides?"Students' own green claims → five red flags → CSR or greenwashing? card sort with an "EU inspector" round → government toolbox → fishbowl seminar.
The threadClass 1 shows that free resources get overused. Class 2 asks who should stop that: companies themselves (CSR), customers, or governments (law).

Learning goals

By the end of Week 4, students can:

  1. Explain the tension between shareholder primacy and corporate social responsibility, using one court case.
  2. Explain the tragedy of the commons and apply it to a real environmental case.
  3. Distinguish genuine CSR from greenwashing, using clear tests.
  4. Compare government tools (regulation, carbon tax, cap and trade, transparency laws, partnerships) and their risks (lobbying, the revolving door).
  5. Take part in a sustained academic discussion: build on others' points, ask follow-up questions, cite evidence.

Big ethical questions

  • Does a company owe anything to society beyond obeying the law and paying its shareholders?
  • Who should pay for pollution: the company, the customer, the taxpayer, or future generations?
  • Is it ethical to do abroad what is illegal at home?
  • When a company does good and profits from it, does the motive matter?
  • Should governments force businesses to be ethical, or trust them to regulate themselves?

Review 9:00–9:08 · Fix the mistake

PurposeFix the two most common Quiz 1 errors without singling anyone out. GroupingPairs MaterialsSlides 3–4; worksheet §1 BookReview of 3.1 (ethical minimum/maximum, pp. 68–71), 3.2 (categories, pp. 72–77) and 3.3 (matrix, pp. 78–82)

Say: "On the quiz, many of you made the same two mistakes, so let's fix them together." Show four anonymous sentences "from a student." Pairs correct them (4 min), then check (4 min).

MistakeCorrection
"The ethical minimum is to recall every crib and refund customers."That is beyond the law. The ethical minimum = only what the law requires: keep selling, because the paint is legal at home.
"The four stakeholder categories are high power/low interest, low power/high interest…"Those are the matrix quadrants. The four categories are enabling, functional, normative and diffused.
"Regulators are diffused stakeholders."Regulators are enabling: they allow the company to operate.
"A Kantian judges the result."That's utilitarian. A Kantian judges duty and motive: never use people merely as a means.
"Two tools, both with four boxes — easy to mix up. Categories tell you what kind of stakeholder. The matrix tells you how much attention they get."

Engage 9:08–9:22 · The fishing game

PurposeStudents experience the tragedy of the commons before they learn the name, so the concept is felt, not memorized. It also previews the Class 2 question: can people regulate themselves, or do they need rules? GroupingGroups of 4 ("fishing companies sharing one lake") Materials12 tokens per group in a cup; score slips (handouts p.1); slides 5–7; worksheet §2 Book4.2 "Environmental Justice": tragedy of the commons, Hardin, Lloyd, cruise ships (pp. 103–116)

Rules (slide 5)

  • Each group shares a lake with 12 fish. Each player is a fishing company.
  • Each round, every player secretly writes how many fish they want: 0, 1, 2 or 3.
  • Reveal. If the total is less than or equal to the fish in the lake, everyone takes their catch. If the total is more than the fish in the lake, the lake collapses: the game is over for that group.
  • After each round, the fish left in the lake double (but never more than 12).
  • Your score = your total fish. Play 4 rounds. No talking in rounds 1–2. In rounds 3–4, groups may talk and make agreements.
  1. (2 min) Explain and do a practice reveal.
  2. (7 min) Play 4 rounds. Walk around and note which lakes collapse and when.
  3. (5 min) Debrief.

Debrief questions:

  • Whose lake collapsed? When? Why did people take 3 fish when they knew it was risky?
  • What changed when you were allowed to talk? Did anyone break an agreement?
  • If you were the government, what one rule would you make for this lake?
"What you just played has a name: the tragedy of the commons. The idea goes back to the British economist William Forster Lloyd, and ecologist Garrett Hardin made it famous in 1968. When a resource is free and shared — a lake, the ocean, the air — each person gains by taking more, but everyone loses together. The textbook's example: cruise ships carry about 25 million passengers a year and dump about a billion gallons of sewage, often at night. Who owns the ocean?"
Typical result: one or two lakes collapse in round 1 or 2. Lakes that survive usually did so after talking (rounds 3–4) — that's your bridge to agreements and rules, i.e., government.
Language: "I took more because I assumed others would…" · "If everyone had held back, …" · "We agreed to ___, but someone broke the deal." · "What we needed was a rule that…"

Study 9:22–9:38 · You be the judge

PurposeTeach the corporate-law vocabulary of 4.1 through three real court cases. Students rule first, then learn what the court decided. GroupingGroups of 3 ("panels of judges") MaterialsJudge cards (handouts p.2); slides 8–12; worksheet §3 Book4.1 "Balancing the Many Responsibilities of a Corporation" and "The Two Sides of the Corporate Responsibility Debate" (pp. 94–102)
  1. (3 min) Mini-input (slide 8): a corporation gives owners limited liability — they can lose only what they invested. The textbook argues this means society shares the company's risk, so the company owes something back (quid pro quo). Then the big question: shareholder primacy (the company exists to make money for shareholders) vs. CSR.
  2. (8 min) Panels read each case and rule: for the managers or for the shareholders? One sentence of reasoning each (about 2.5 minutes per case).
  3. (5 min) Reveal each ruling (slides 9–11) and the key term it teaches. Count how many panels "agreed with the court."
Case (on the card)What the court decidedTerm it teaches
Dodge v. Ford Motor Company (1919). Henry Ford wanted to keep profits in the company to expand and to lower car prices, instead of paying large dividends. Two shareholders, the Dodge brothers, sued.The Michigan Supreme Court sided with the Dodge brothers: a company is run mainly for its shareholders. But it also said courts should not second-guess ordinary business decisions.Shareholder primacy; the business judgment rule
Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968). The owners of the Chicago Cubs baseball team refused to install lights for night games, partly out of concern for the neighborhood. A shareholder sued, saying the team was losing money.The owners won. Their decision was reasonable and made in good faith, so the court would not interfere. (Lights were installed in 1988, after the team was sold.)Business judgment rule
eBay v. Newmark (2010). The founders of craigslist, a classifieds website, used special measures to stop eBay (a minority shareholder) from gaining more control, to protect craigslist's community-focused, not-very-profit-driven culture.The Delaware court ruled for eBay: directors must promote value for stockholders and cannot defend a strategy that openly ignores it.Fiduciary duty; shareholder primacy
"So is the law clear? The textbook says no — the debate swings like a pendulum. Friedman said business should only make profit; Lynn Stout said shareholders as a group actually want companies to behave well. And the textbook's evidence: CSR does not necessarily reduce profits."
Link to Week 3: The textbook uses the term moral minimum — the basic level of ethical behavior a business must meet. It's the same idea as last week's ethical minimum.
Left out on purpose: Burwell v. Hobby Lobby (2014), also in 4.1, involves religion and contraception. It's a valid case but can derail the lesson; skip it unless your class is ready for it.
Language: "We rule in favor of ___, on the grounds that…" · "The company has a duty to…" · "The court would be overstepping if…" · "Strictly speaking, the law requires…, but…"

Study 9:38–9:46 · The sustainability toolbox

PurposeGive students the five concepts they need for the gallery walk, briefly. GroupingWhole class MaterialsSlides 13–15; worksheet §4 BookCh. 4 intro (p. 93); 4.2 (pp. 103–116)
  • Sustainability — a long-term approach to how business affects the environment and other stakeholders.
  • Carbon footprint — the amount of carbon dioxide and other carbon compounds a person or company releases by using fossil fuels.
  • Corporate personhood — a corporation has some of the legal rights of a person (e.g., it can sue and be sued).
  • Earth jurisprudence — the idea that nature itself should have legal rights. Ecuador's 2008 constitution was the first to recognize "Rights for Nature." (Link to Week 3: "Should Trees Have Standing?")
  • Is sustainability good business? The textbook's evidence: companies on the Global 100 list of sustainable companies outperformed a typical index fund (2005–2015); Dell's flexible-work program avoids about 7,000 metric tons of emissions and saves about $12 million a year.
"If corporations can be 'persons' with rights, why can't a river? Keep that question in your head for the gallery walk."

Activate 9:46–10:30 · Gallery walk: global environmental ethics cases

PurposeSyllabus gallery walk. Students apply stakeholders, the minimum/maximum and the commons to six real cases from five regions, and build on each other's written ideas. Grouping6 groups (2–4 students); one station each, then rotate MaterialsStation posters A–F (handouts p.3–5) on the walls; sticky notes; worksheet §5 Book4.2 Environmental Justice; Ethics Across Time and Cultures: toxic waste; Environmental Economics and Policy (pp. 103–116); 4.3 Cases from the Real World: Deepwater Horizon (from p. 117)

The six stations

StationCase (textbook facts on the poster)Key idea to draw out
A · Côte d'Ivoire, 2006The energy company Trafigura dumped hundreds of tons of toxic waste near Abidjan after searching other countries for a place to dispose of it. Amnesty International reported more than 100,000 people sickened and 15 deaths.Exploiting weak laws; voiceless victims
B · Nigeria, mid-1990sA reporter found about 10,000 barrels of toxic waste, including PCBs, stored near homes with children. Disposal cost about $3,000 per ton in the U.S. and Europe vs. about $3 per ton in West Africa.Legal there ≠ ethical; ethical minimum vs. maximum
C · Ecuador, 2008Ecuador's constitution gives "Rights for Nature": an ecosystem can be a party in a lawsuit, and citizens can enforce its rights. The U.S. has not adopted this idea. Should the Gulf of Mexico have been able to sue BP?Earth jurisprudence; voiceless stakeholders
D · Gulf of Mexico, 2010The Deepwater Horizon rig exploded: 11 workers killed, at least 5 million barrels of oil released. An inexpensive backup shutoff valve could likely have stopped the flow. The U.S. didn't require it, partly because of industry lobbying; Norway and Brazil do.Government's role; lobbying; legal gaps
E · The oceansAbout 25 million people take cruises each year. Cruise ships dump about a billion gallons of sewage a year, often at night (Friends of the Earth, using EPA data).Tragedy of the commons; "invisible" pollution
F · West TexasFracking (breaking rock to release oil and gas) increased truck traffic tenfold, damaging roads that small towns cannot afford to repair. It can also pollute water, spill chemicals and may trigger earthquakes.Externalities: who pays the cost?

Procedure

  1. (3 min) Explain. Each poster has three questions: (1) Who benefits, and who pays the cost? (include the voiceless) (2) Legal minimum or ethical maximum? Is there a gap in the law? (3) Who should fix it: the company, customers, government — or all three? How?
  2. (24 min) Groups rotate every 4 minutes (6 stations). At each station they read the case and add at least two sticky notes: one new idea and one reply to an existing note ("I agree, and…" / "But what about…?"). Ring a bell to rotate.
  3. (7 min) Groups return to their first station, read all the notes and prepare a 1-minute summary: the strongest idea, the biggest disagreement, and their answer to question 3.
  4. (10 min) Six 1-minute summaries + debrief.

Debrief questions:

  • Which cases are the tragedy of the commons? (E clearly; also F and D.)
  • In A and B, the waste went where disposal was cheap and laws were weak. Is "legal there" an ethical defense?
  • Which station needed government most? Which could a company fix on its own?
  • Across all six, who was most often voiceless?
Sensitivity: Stations A and B involve Africa, and the textbook mentions Nigeria's ranking on a corruption list. Keep the focus on the companies' choices, not on judging countries. Don't ask students from these countries to explain or defend them.
Language for sticky notes: "The hidden cost here is…" · "This was legal, but…" · "Building on the note above, …" · "I'd push back: …" · "The voiceless stakeholder is…" · "A law requiring ___ would…"

Reflect 10:30–10:40 · Exit ticket & bridge

  1. (6 min) Exit ticket (worksheet §5 or slip): (1) Explain the tragedy of the commons with an example from today. (2) Which case should the government fix, and how? (3) One question.
  2. (4 min) Bridge: "Take out the company claim you brought for homework. Over the break, decide: do you believe it? After the break, we test it."

If students forgot their example, they can find one on any product package or company website during the break.

Study 11:18–11:28 · Spotting greenwashing: five red flags

PurposeTurn "greenwashing" from a word into a usable test. GroupingWhole class → pairs MaterialsSlides 24–26; red-flag strip (handouts p.6); worksheet §6 Book3.4 "CSR as Public Relations Tool" (from p. 83). Red flags adapted from the EU rules (outside source).

Recap (2 min): CSR = responsibilities beyond products and profits. Triple bottom line = people, planet, profit. Greenwashing = CSR used as public relations to hide real problems. The textbook warns: don't "blindly accept" company websites or advertising.

Five red flags (5 min). Since September 27, 2026, new EU rules (the Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive) ban several kinds of green claims aimed at EU consumers — including from non-EU companies that sell there. They make a good checklist:

  1. Vague words with no proof — "eco-friendly," "green," "natural." (Banned in the EU unless the company can prove excellent environmental performance.)
  2. "Carbon neutral" because of offsets — claiming a product is neutral because the company paid for trees or other offsets. (Banned in the EU.)
  3. Homemade labels — a sustainability logo the company created itself, with no independent certification. (Banned in the EU.)
  4. One part presented as the whole — a claim about one small feature presented as if it applied to the whole product or company. (Banned in the EU.)
  5. Future promises without a plan — "Net zero by 2050" with no detailed, checkable plan. (Can be misleading under the EU rules.)

Quick practice (3 min): pairs apply the five flags to their own claims from 11:10.

Source note: The EU rules are an outside source, not in the textbook. A separate, broader "Green Claims Directive" was proposed and later withdrawn; the rules above are from the Empowering Consumers Directive, which does apply. They cover claims made to consumers, not company reports for investors.

Activate 11:28–11:58 · CSR or greenwashing? card sort

PurposeSyllabus group project. Students evaluate 16 claims, argue in groups, then test them against a real law. GroupingGroups of 3–4 Materials16 claim cards (handouts p.7–8, cut); three pile labels: Genuine CSR · Greenwashing · Need more information; worksheet §6 BookCards 1–8 from 3.4, 4.1, 4.2, 4.3; cards 9–16 are fictional
  1. (12 min) Sort. Groups place each card in a pile. Rule: you must agree, or the card goes to "Need more information" with a note saying what information.
  2. (8 min) EU inspector round. Each group becomes an EU consumer-protection inspector. For every card in "Greenwashing," which red flag (1–5) does it break? Which fictional claims would be illegal in the EU today?

  4. (5 min) Debrief.

Study 11:58–12:08 · The government toolbox

PurposeGive students the tools and risks they need to argue in the fishbowl. GroupingPairs MaterialsToolbox match (handouts p.9); worksheet §7 (self-check); slides 29–30 Book4.2 "Environmental Economics and Policy" (pp. 103–116); 4.3 (from p. 117)

Pairs match each tool to an example (5 min), then check and discuss risks (5 min). Handout key: 1-D · 2-F · 3-E · 4-B · 5-A · 6-C.

ToolExample from the textbook
Direct regulation (rules and penalties)Clean Air Act (1970); in 2017 the Houston company Vopak agreed to install pollution controls and pay a $2.5 million penalty for violations.
Carbon tax / Pigovian tax (pay to pollute)Supported even by Exxon Mobil, Shell, BP and Total through the Climate Leadership Council. Named after economist A. C. Pigou.
Cap and trade (a limit + permits that can be bought and sold)A cap limits total greenhouse gas emissions; companies that pollute less can sell their unused allowances.
Transparency law (companies must report honestly)Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002), passed after the Enron and WorldCom frauds. (Outside source: the EU's new green-claims rules.)
Public-private partnership (government and business work together)NASA working with SpaceX.
Voluntary standards (self-regulation)ISO 14000 environmental standards; LEED building certification.

Three risks the textbook names:

  • Lobbying — industry pressure weakened safety rules before Deepwater Horizon.
  • The revolving door — people move from an industry into the government agency that regulates it, and back again ("the fox guarding the hen house").
  • Citizens United (2010) — the U.S. Supreme Court allowed corporations to spend unlimited money on political advertising.

Activate 12:08–12:42 · Fishbowl seminar: government's role in business ethics

PurposeSyllabus discussion. A structured, sustained academic discussion in which students build on each other's ideas and cite evidence — a different skill from Week 2's debate and Week 3's role-play. GroupingInner circle of 5–6 + one empty "hot seat"; outer circle of observers. Swap halfway. MaterialsEvidence cards (handouts p.10); observer sheets (handouts p.11); worksheet §8 Book4.1 Friedman vs. CSR (pp. 94–102); 4.3 (from p. 117)

Central question: "Should governments force businesses to behave ethically — or should businesses be trusted to regulate themselves?"

  1. (4 min) Everyone prepares individually (worksheet §8): first position (1–10), two pieces of evidence from today, one question to ask.
  2. (12 min) Round 1. Inner circle discusses. No teacher, no hand-raising — speakers build on each other. An outer-circle student may sit in the hot seat to make one point, then leave. Each outer-circle student observes one inner partner and records: one argument with evidence, one good question, one strong phrase.
  3. (2 min) Observers give their partner quick feedback.
  4. (12 min) Round 2. Swap circles. New sub-question (see below).
  5. (4 min) Whole-class debrief.

Sub-questions (use if discussion slows)

  • Round 1: The fishing game: did your group need a rule, or did talking work? What does that say about companies?
  • Round 1: Why would Exxon support a carbon tax? Is that ethics or strategy?
  • Round 2: Norway and Brazil require the backup valve; the U.S. didn't. Who is responsible for the Deepwater Horizon deaths — BP, the government, or the lobbyists?
  • Round 2: If the regulators come from the industry (the revolving door), can regulation be trusted?
  • Round 2: Is it ethical for a company to follow weak laws in one country and strict laws in another? (Stations A and B; the EU rules.)

Words students may misunderstand

  • commons — shared land or resources, not "common" (ordinary) and not the British House of Commons.
  • liability — legal responsibility (often for debts or damage), not "reliability."
  • green — environmentally friendly, not only the color; also "green" = inexperienced.
  • offset — to balance out, not "set off" (start a journey or trigger).
  • neutral in "carbon neutral" — zero net emissions, not "having no opinion."
  • person in "corporate personhood" — a legal idea, not a human being.

Grammar in use: hedging and claims

  • Hedging evidence: "The data suggest that…" · "It appears that…" · "This may be greenwashing, but we'd need to know…"
  • Passive for responsibility: "The waste was dumped at night." — Ask: by whom? (Passive can hide the actor — a greenwashing trick, too!)
  • Modals of obligation: "Companies should…" (advice) vs. "Companies must…" (law) vs. "Companies are required to…" (rule).

Model journal (Prompt B, ~185 words)

I believe it is unethical for a company to dump toxic waste in a country simply because it is legal or cheap there. The main reason is that the harm to people is the same wherever the waste goes; only the price changes. In stakeholder terms, the local community is a voiceless stakeholder with very low power, so it cannot protect itself. A Kantian would say that choosing a place because its people are poor and its laws are weak treats them merely as a means to save money. Even a utilitarian would struggle to defend it: in Côte d'Ivoire, the textbook reports more than 100,000 people sickened and 15 deaths. One could argue that the company obeyed local law and that profits create jobs at home. That is true, but obeying a weak law is only the ethical minimum. If saving $1 million required harming others, I would still refuse, because a company's reputation and its duty to people are worth more. In conclusion, companies should apply their strictest standard everywhere.

Possible Quiz 2 items (Chapter 4)

  1. Dodge v. Ford (1919) supported which principle? (shareholder primacy)
  2. The rule that courts won't second-guess reasonable, good-faith business decisions is the ___. (business judgment rule)
  3. Explain the tragedy of the commons with one example. (shared free resource overused because each user gains by taking more; e.g., cruise-ship sewage in the ocean, overfishing)
  4. Which country first recognized rights for nature in its constitution? (Ecuador, 2008)
  5. What is the difference between a carbon tax and cap and trade? (tax = fee per unit of emissions; cap and trade = total limit + tradable permits)
  6. What is the "revolving door"? (people moving between an industry and the agency that regulates it)
  7. Which law was passed after Enron and WorldCom, and what does it require? (Sarbanes-Oxley, 2002: transparent financial reporting, independent auditors, executive responsibility, whistleblower protection)
  8. Define greenwashing and give one sign of it. (misleading environmental claims; e.g., vague "eco-friendly" with no proof)

Open questions & flags

  1. Keystone XL is outdated. The textbook's What Would You Do? in 4.3 describes the pipeline as approved in 2017 and unresolved. In fact, the U.S. permit was revoked in 2021 and TC Energy terminated the project in June 2021. I left it out.
  2. Other dated facts in Chapter 4: the Global 100 and ISO figures are from around 2015–2018; the Tesla prices and the statement about the Paris Climate Accord reflect 2017–2018. U.S. participation in the Paris Agreement has changed more than once since then — check current status before mentioning it.
  3. Possible textbook date error: the book dates Cormac Cullinan's Wild Law to 1992; it was published in 2002. The materials don't use the date.
  4. Sensitive topics left out: 4.1's Hobby Lobby case (religion and contraception) and 4.3's guns and opioids examples. They're valid but can derail a language class; use them only if you choose to.
  5. Outside source: the EU green-claims rules (in force since September 27, 2026) are not in the textbook; they're marked as outside material on every student page.
  6. Homework reminder: the card sort works best if students bring their own claims. If many forget, give the class 5 minutes at the start of Class 2 to find one online.
