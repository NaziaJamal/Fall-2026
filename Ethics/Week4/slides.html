Who is a company for?

Limited liability

Owners can lose only what they invested. Society shares the risk — so the textbook says companies owe something back (quid pro quo).

Shareholder primacy vs. CSR

Is the company only for its shareholders — or also for workers, communities and the environment?

You are judges. Read each case. Rule: for the managers or for the shareholders? 8 min

Class 1 · You be the judge8
Case 1

Dodge v. Ford Motor Company (1919)

Henry Ford wanted to reinvest profits and lower car prices instead of paying big dividends. Two shareholders — the Dodge brothers — sued.

The court sided with the Dodge brothers.

Shareholder primacy — but courts shouldn't second-guess ordinary business decisions.

Class 1 · You be the judge · 4.19
Case 2

Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968)

The Chicago Cubs' owners refused to install lights for night games, partly to protect the neighborhood. A shareholder sued: "We're losing money!"

The owners won.

Business judgment rule — courts don't overrule reasonable, honest business decisions.

Class 1 · You be the judge · 4.110
Case 3

eBay v. Newmark (2010)

craigslist's founders tried to stop eBay, a shareholder, from gaining more control — to protect craigslist's community-focused culture.

The court ruled for eBay.

Fiduciary duty — directors must promote value for stockholders.

So is the law clear?

No — it swings like a pendulum.

Milton Friedman

Business should make profit within the law. CSR is unelected regulation.

Lynn Stout

Shareholders as a group actually want companies to behave well.

The textbook's evidence: CSR does not necessarily reduce profits.

Corporate personhood

A corporation has some legal rights of a person: it can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued.

Study · 3.4

CSR or greenwashing?

CSR

Responsibilities beyond products and profits: environment, community, workers.

Triple bottom line

People, planet, profit — John Elkington, 1994.

Greenwashing

CSR used as public relations to hide real problems.

The textbook: don't "blindly accept" company websites or advertising.

Class 2 · Study24
The real world · since September 27, 2026

Five red flags

Based on new EU rules for claims made to EU consumers, including by non-EU companies. Outside source, not in the textbook.

  1. Vague words with no proof — "eco-friendly," "green" banned in the EU
  2. "Carbon neutral" because of offsets banned in the EU
  3. Homemade labels, not independently certified banned in the EU
  4. One part presented as the whole banned in the EU
  5. Future promises without a plan may be misleading

"Should governments force businesses to behave ethically — or should businesses be trusted to regulate themselves?"

Prepare 4 min: your position (1–10), two pieces of evidence, one question.

Class 2 · Fishbowl31
Fishbowl

How it works

Inner circle

Discuss. No hand-raising — build on each other. One empty hot seat: anyone from outside may sit, make one point, and leave.

Outer circle

Observe one partner. Note: one argument with evidence, one good question, one strong phrase.

Try: "Building on what ___ said, …" · "Can I push back on that?" · "What's your evidence?" · "So are you saying that…?" · "___, what's your take?"
