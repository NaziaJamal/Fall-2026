3 · You be the judge

Does a company exist only for its shareholders? Read each case and decide before you see the court's answer.

4.1 pp. 94–102

Read: 4.1 "The Advantages of Corporate Status," "Balancing the Many Responsibilities of a Corporation" and "The Two Sides of the Corporate Responsibility Debate."

Before you read: If you owned shares in a company, would you want it to give money to the community?

Glossary: limited liability — owners can lose only what they invested · dividend — profit paid to shareholders · shareholder primacy — the company exists mainly to make money for shareholders · business judgment rule — courts don't second-guess reasonable, honest business decisions · fiduciary duty — a legal duty to act in the best interest of the people whose money you manage · moral minimum — the basic level of ethical behavior a business must meet

Look for: Why does the book say companies owe society something in return for limited liability? Is the law clear about shareholder primacy? Does CSR lower profits, according to the book?

Dodge v. Ford (1919): The Michigan Supreme Court sided with the Dodge brothers — a company is run mainly for its shareholders (shareholder primacy). But it also said courts should not second-guess ordinary business decisions.
Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968): The owners won. Their decision was reasonable and in good faith (business judgment rule). Lights were installed in 1988, after the team was sold.
eBay v. Newmark (2010): The Delaware court ruled for eBay: directors must promote value for stockholders (fiduciary duty).

The textbook's conclusion: the law is not settled — opinion "swings like a pendulum." And CSR does not necessarily reduce profits.

4 · The sustainability toolbox

Chapter 4 intro and 4.2 pp. 93–116

Read: the Chapter 4 introduction (p. 93) and 4.2 "Environmental Justice" and "Why Sustainability Is Good for Business" (pp. 103–116).

Glossary: sustainability — using resources so they last for the long term · carbon footprint — the carbon dioxide a person or company releases · corporate personhood — a corporation has some legal rights of a person · earth jurisprudence — the idea that nature should have legal rights · ecosystem — a natural system such as a river or forest

Look for: What did Ecuador do in 2008? What evidence does the book give that sustainability is good for business (Global 100, Dell)?

Sustainability — a long-term approach to how business affects the environment and people.
Carbon footprint — the carbon dioxide a person or company releases by using fossil fuels.
Corporate personhood — a corporation has some legal rights of a person: it can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued.
Earth jurisprudence — nature itself should have legal rights. Ecuador's 2008 constitution was the first to recognize "Rights for Nature."
Good business? The textbook reports that "Global 100" sustainable companies did better than a typical index fund (2005–2015), and Dell's flexible-work program saves about $12 million a year.

Five red flags of greenwashing — based on the EU rules that apply since September 27, 2026 to claims made to EU consumers (including by non-EU companies)
  1. Vague words with no proof — "eco-friendly," "green," "natural." (banned in the EU)
  2. "Carbon neutral" because of offsets (banned in the EU)
  3. Homemade labels — a green logo the company made itself, not independently certified (banned in the EU)
  4. One part presented as the whole (banned in the EU)
  5. Future promises without a plan — "Net zero by 2050!" with no checkable plan (may be misleading)

The three cases

A · Houston flooding — 4.2, box What Would You Do? pp. 103–116
You are on Houston's urban planning commission. Since 2001, hurricanes and storms have caused about $75 billion in damage. The solution can't rely only on taxpayers. The proposal: developers must set aside green space at least equal to the land they cover with concrete, which raises their costs. How would you persuade developers, investors, homeowners, politicians and the media? Should local, state or federal government regulate this? Who should pay for flood damage?

B · Toxic waste — 4.2, box Ethics Across Time and Cultures pp. 103–116
In Nigeria in the mid-1990s, about 10,000 barrels of toxic waste were stored near homes; disposal cost about $3,000 per ton in the U.S. and Europe but about $3 per ton in West Africa. In 2006, Trafigura dumped waste near Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire; Amnesty International reported over 100,000 people sickened and 15 deaths. Should a company take advantage of a country's weak laws? Would your answer change if it saved your company $1 million?

C · Project Shakti — 4.1, box Cases from the Real World pp. 94–102
Unilever trains rural women in India to become small entrepreneurs selling its products; the program has enlisted over 100,000 participants. Does Unilever do this to help women, to increase profits, or both? Do mixed motives discredit a good program?

0 words · target 150–200
Model journal (Prompt B)

I believe it is unethical for a company to dump toxic waste in a country simply because it is legal or cheap there. The main reason is that the harm to people is the same wherever the waste goes; only the price changes. In stakeholder terms, the local community is a voiceless stakeholder with very low power, so it cannot protect itself. A Kantian would say that choosing a place because its people are poor and its laws are weak treats them merely as a means to save money. Even a utilitarian would struggle to defend it: in Côte d'Ivoire, the textbook reports more than 100,000 people sickened and 15 deaths. One could argue that the company obeyed local law and that profits create jobs at home. That is true, but obeying a weak law is only the ethical minimum. If saving $1 million required harming others, I would still refuse, because a company's reputation and its duty to people are worth more. In conclusion, companies should apply their strictest standard everywhere.

Language bank

Key words in simple English
  • limited liability — owners can lose only what they invested
  • shareholder primacy — the company exists mainly for its shareholders
  • fiduciary duty — a legal duty to act in the best interest of the people whose money you manage
  • sustainability — using resources so they last
  • carbon footprint — the carbon dioxide you release
  • tragedy of the commons — shared free resources get overused
  • externality — a cost that falls on people outside the deal
  • greenwashing — misleading green claims
  • offset — paying to "balance out" pollution
  • carbon tax / cap and trade — a fee per unit of pollution / a pollution limit with tradable permits
  • lobbying — trying to influence lawmakers
  • commons — shared resources (not "common" = ordinary!)
  • liability — legal responsibility (not "reliability")
