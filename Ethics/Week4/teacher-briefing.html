1The big picture in five sentences

  1. Society, the environment and government are stakeholders (groups affected by a business) that companies easily ignore, because they rarely buy anything or own shares.
  2. The law protects company owners in many ways, so the textbook argues companies owe society something in return.
  3. Shared natural resources — air, water, oceans — get overused when they are free; this is the tragedy of the commons (everyone takes a little too much of a shared thing until it's ruined).
  4. Some companies act responsibly on their own (CSR (corporate social responsibility — caring for society and the environment, not only profit)); others only pretend to (greenwashing (fake or exaggerated green claims)).
  5. Government can step in with laws, taxes and partnerships — but it can also be influenced by the industries it regulates.
Say it in class: "Who pays for the planet? Usually the people with the least power — unless someone changes the rules."

2Corporations and the law (4.1, pp. 94–102)

Why owners like corporations

A corporation gives its owners limited liability (if the company fails or is sued, owners lose only the money they invested — not their house or savings). This makes investing safer, so businesses can raise money more easily.

The textbook's argument: because society shares the company's risk (losses are partly "socialized" (spread across everyone)), the company owes something back. This exchange is a quid pro quo (Latin: "something for something" — a fair trade).

The big debate: who is a company for?

  • Shareholder primacy (the idea that a company's main duty is to make money for its shareholders). Milton Friedman supported this view.
  • CSR view (a company also has duties to employees, communities and the environment). Lynn Stout argued that shareholders as a group actually want companies to behave well.
  • The textbook's conclusion: the law is not settled — opinion "swings like a pendulum." And CSR does not necessarily lower profits.

Three court cases (used in "You be the judge")

CaseWhat happenedWhat it teaches
Dodge v. Ford (1919)Henry Ford wanted to reinvest profits and lower car prices instead of paying big dividends (profits paid out to shareholders). Two shareholders, the Dodge brothers, sued — and won.Shareholder primacy. The court also said it wouldn't interfere with ordinary business decisions.
Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968)The Chicago Cubs' owners refused to install lights for night baseball games, partly to protect the neighborhood. A shareholder said this lost money. The owners won.The business judgment rule (judges don't second-guess reasonable, honest business decisions).
eBay v. Newmark (2010)The founders of craigslist tried to stop eBay (a shareholder) from gaining more control, to protect craigslist's community-focused culture. The Delaware court ruled for eBay.Fiduciary duty (directors must act in the best interest of the people whose money they manage — here, the shareholders).

The textbook also uses the term moral minimum (the basic level of ethical behavior a business must meet) — the same idea as Week 3's "ethical minimum."

Project Shakti (box in 4.1): Unilever trains rural women in India to become small entrepreneurs (people who run their own small business) selling Unilever products; over 100,000 participants. The textbook asks: is it to help women, to increase profits, or both — and do mixed motives matter?

3CSR and greenwashing (3.4, from p. 83)

  • CSR (a company's responsibilities beyond making good products and profits — e.g., caring for the environment and community).
  • Triple bottom line (measuring success in three ways: people, planet, profit) — described by John Elkington in 1994.
  • Externality (a cost or benefit that falls on people outside the business deal — e.g., pollution that harms neighbors).
  • Greenwashing (using CSR as public relations to hide real problems). The textbook warns readers not to "blindly accept" company websites or advertising.
  • Textbook examples: Ben & Jerry's has a social mission, but as it grew, much of its milk came from CAFOs (confined-animal feeding operations — very large, crowded industrial farms). Coca-Cola talks about "stakeholder dialogue," but the textbook describes heavy water use at its plant in Chiapas, Mexico, where only about half the people have running water.

4Business and the environment (4.2, pp. 103–116)

Rights for people, companies — and nature?

  • Corporate personhood (the legal idea that a corporation has some of the rights of a person — it can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued).
  • Earth jurisprudence ("jurisprudence" = legal thinking; the idea that nature itself should have legal rights). Leading voices: lawyer Cormac Cullinan and scholar Thomas Berry.
  • Ecuador's 2008 constitution was the first to recognize "Rights for Nature": an ecosystem (a natural system such as a river or forest) can be a party in a lawsuit. The U.S. has not adopted this.

The tragedy of the commons

A commons (a resource everyone shares and no one owns — a lake, the ocean, the air) tends to be overused, because each user gains by taking more while the damage is shared by all. The idea comes from British economist William Forster Lloyd; ecologist Garrett Hardin made it famous in a 1968 article. Example from the book: cruise ships dump about a billion gallons of sewage into the ocean each year, often at night.

Is sustainability good for business?

  • Sustainability (running a business so resources last for the long term).
  • Carbon footprint (how much carbon dioxide a person or company releases by using fossil fuels like oil, gas and coal).
  • Evidence in the book: the Global 100 list of sustainable companies outperformed a typical index fund (2005–2015); Dell's flexible-work program saves about $12 million a year and avoids about 7,000 metric tons of emissions; Cisco requires suppliers to follow a code of conduct; surveys say the main reason companies go green is cost savings.
  • Voluntary standards: ISO 14000 (international environmental management standards; companies can be certified) and LEED (a U.S. rating system for green buildings; e.g., the Empire State Building is LEED Gold).

Paying for pollution

  • Pigovian tax (a tax on an activity that harms others, named after economist A. C. Pigou).
  • Carbon tax ("pay to pollute": companies pay a fee for each unit of carbon they release). Surprisingly, Exxon Mobil, Shell, BP and Total supported one through the Climate Leadership Council.
  • Cap and trade (the government sets a total pollution limit — the "cap" — and gives out permits; companies that pollute less can sell their spare permits — the "trade").
  • Fracking (hydraulic fracturing: breaking underground rock with high-pressure liquid to release oil and gas) — in West Texas it increased truck traffic tenfold and damaged roads.
Link to Week 2 frameworks: the textbook notes that a deontological (duty-based, Kantian) view says users of resources must pay and must consider future generations, while a utilitarian (results-based) view stresses jobs, taxes and products — but often focuses on the present.

5Government and business (4.3, from p. 117)

  • Regulation (rules made and enforced by government). Example: the Clean Air Act (1970) lets the EPA (Environmental Protection Agency, the U.S. environmental regulator) enforce air-quality rules. In 2017, the Houston company Vopak paid a $2.5 million penalty for violations.
  • Public-private partnership (government and companies working together on a public goal, e.g., NASA and SpaceX).
  • Public health as sustainability: the textbook discusses alcohol, tobacco, guns, opioids and animal welfare. Examples of voluntary action: CVS stopped selling tobacco (2014); Net-a-Porter banned fur.
  • The revolving door (people move from an industry into the government agency that regulates it, then back to the industry) — critics call it "the fox guarding the hen house." U.S. law has some limits, e.g., a one-year ban on former officials lobbying their old agency.
  • States' rights vs. federal power: some argue the national government should have limited power (based on the Tenth Amendment (the part of the U.S. Constitution that leaves unlisted powers to the states)); others favor a strong central government, using the Commerce Clause (the constitutional power to regulate trade between states).
  • Citizens United (2010) (a U.S. Supreme Court decision allowing corporations to spend unlimited money on political advertising, as "free speech").
  • Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) (a U.S. law passed after the Enron and WorldCom frauds, requiring honest financial reporting, independent auditors, personal responsibility for top executives, and protection for whistleblowers).
  • Lobbying (trying to influence lawmakers): before Deepwater Horizon, industry lobbying helped stop a requirement for a cheap backup safety valve.

7Outside updates (not in the textbook)

  • New EU rules on green claims (since September 27, 2026). The EU's Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive bans, for claims made to consumers in the EU: vague claims like "eco-friendly" without proof; "carbon neutral" claims based on offsets (paying for tree-planting or similar projects to "balance out" your pollution); sustainability labels not based on independent certification; and claims about one part presented as if they applied to the whole product. Future promises such as "net zero by 2050" may be misleading without a detailed, checkable plan. The rules also apply to non-EU companies selling to EU consumers. (Sources: Latham & Watkins; Climate Focus.)
  • Keystone XL pipeline. The textbook presents it as approved in 2017 and unresolved. In 2021 the U.S. permit was revoked and the company, TC Energy, terminated the project in June 2021. (Source: Pipeline & Gas Journal.)
  • Dated figures. Many numbers in Chapter 4 (Global 100, ISO counts, Tesla prices, the Paris Climate Accord) are from 2015–2018. Present them as "the textbook reports…"

8Questions students may ask you

"If something is legal in that country, how can it be wrong?"

Legal is the ethical minimum (Week 3). The harm to people is the same wherever the waste goes; only the price and the law change. Ask: would the company do it at home?

"Why would Exxon support a carbon tax?"

Possible reasons: a predictable tax may be better for business than lawsuits or changing rules; it creates a level playing field; it improves reputation. Students can debate whether that's ethics or strategy — both can be true.

"Isn't all CSR just marketing?"

Not all. Use the tests: is it specific, measurable, independently checked, and does it cost the company something (like CVS giving up tobacco sales)? If yes, it's likely genuine.

"Is 'carbon neutral' always greenwashing?"

Not always, but in the EU it's now banned when based on offsets. A claim based on the product's actual reduced emissions, with evidence, is different.

"Do these EU rules affect companies from other countries?"

Yes — if they sell to consumers in the EU. They don't apply to products sold only outside the EU.

"How can a company be a 'person'?"

It's a legal idea: the law treats a corporation like a person for some purposes (owning property, signing contracts, going to court). It doesn't mean a company has feelings or a vote.

"Is the Keystone XL pipeline still being built?"

No. The project was terminated in 2021.
