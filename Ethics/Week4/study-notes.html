Part 1 · Who pays for the planet? (Class 1)

In one line: Society, the environment and government are stakeholders too — and free, shared resources get overused unless someone takes responsibility.

Corporations and the law (4.1, pp. 94–102)

  • Limited liability: owners lose only what they invested. Society shares the risk, so the textbook says companies owe something back (quid pro quo).
  • Shareholder primacy: the company exists mainly for its shareholders — Dodge v. Ford (1919); eBay v. Newmark (2010).
  • Business judgment rule: courts don't second-guess reasonable, honest business decisions — Shlensky v. Wrigley (1968).
  • Fiduciary duty: directors must act in the best interest of the people whose money they manage.
  • The law is not settled ("a pendulum"), and CSR does not necessarily reduce profits.

The tragedy of the commons (4.2)

A free, shared resource (a lake, the ocean, the air) gets overused because each user gains by taking more, while the damage is shared by everyone. Garrett Hardin, 1968. Example: cruise ships dumping sewage at sea.

"In the fishing game, the lakes that survived were the ones where people talked and agreed. That's what laws and international agreements are."

Is it good business? The textbook says yes, often: Global 100 companies outperformed a typical fund; Dell saves about $12 million a year; the top reason companies go green is cost savings.

Common confusions: "commons" ≠ "common" (ordinary) · "liability" ≠ "reliability" · corporate "personhood" is a legal idea, not a human being · last week's "ethical minimum" = this week's "moral minimum."

Part 2 · Green or greenwash — and who decides? (Class 2)

In one line: Real CSR is specific, measurable, independently checked and often costly. Greenwashing is vague, unchecked, and hides problems. Governments can force honesty — but they can also be influenced by industry.

CSR and greenwashing (3.4, from p. 83)

  • CSR: responsibilities beyond products and profit.
  • Triple bottom line: people, planet, profit (Elkington, 1994).
  • Greenwashing: CSR used as public relations to hide real problems (Ben & Jerry's and CAFOs; Coca-Cola's water use in Chiapas).

Five red flags (outside source: EU rules, since September 27, 2026)

  1. Vague words with no proof — "eco-friendly," "green"
  2. "Carbon neutral" because of offsets
  3. Homemade labels, not independently certified
  4. One part presented as the whole
  5. Future promises without a plan

The first four are now banned for claims made to EU consumers, including by non-EU companies.

ToolExample

When government fails: lobbying (the Deepwater Horizon valve) · the revolving door (regulators from the industry they regulate) · Citizens United (2010: unlimited corporate political ad spending).
