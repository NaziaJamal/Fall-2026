Week 1 at a glance

Students discover they already reason ethically, learn the book's three lenses as names for what they just did, and leave able to say "It depends — on X."

Learning goals

By the end of Week 1, students can:

  1. Explain the difference between a legal and an ethical responsibility, with an example.
  2. Identify whether an argument focuses on consequences (ends), duties or rules (means), or character.
  3. Distinguish stakeholder from stockholder, and short-term from long-term thinking.
  4. Explain ethical relativism and evaluate the book's case for a single ethical standard.
  5. Disagree, hedge and clarify using B2+ discussion language.

Big ethical questions

  • Is "legal" enough?
  • Can a business be both ethical and profitable — or is that a convenient story?
  • Should you keep one ethical standard everywhere, or adapt to the culture you are in?
  • If a company is ethical only because ethics pays, is it still ethical?

0–10 · Arrive and connect

PurposeLearn names and professions fast
GroupingMixed groups of 3–4
MaterialsA4 paper, markers
  1. As students arrive, they fold an A4 name tent: name / field and years / languages.
  2. Seat groups mixing fields and first languages.
  3. Each person answers in 45 seconds: "What is one unwritten rule in your profession that outsiders don't know?"
  4. Take two quick examples to the whole class. These often surface ethics immediately.
Why it works: it tells you who the nurse, engineer and accountant are, so you can call on expertise all term.

10–50 · Ethical, Unethical, or It Depends?

PurposeHook with real tension; reward reasoned changes of mind
Time40 min (≈12 per scenario + debrief)
GroupingGroups of 3–4
MaterialsWarm-up page, verdict cards, markers

Group roles — rotate every scenario

  • Chair — keeps time; makes sure everyone speaks.
  • Devil's Advocate — argues against the group's emerging view for at least one minute.
  • Spokesperson — defends the verdict to the class.

Procedure (per scenario)

  1. 1 min — Project the scenario; students read silently.
  2. 5 min — Start the page timer. Groups discuss using "Push your group."
  3. 1 min — Groups write verdict + single strongest reason on a verdict card. If It Depends, they must finish: "It depends on whether…"
  4. 1 min — "3-2-1 — show!" All cards up at once. Tap the tallies on the page.
  5. 2 min — Two groups with different verdicts defend, one minute each.
  6. 2 min — Reveal the twist. "Which groups want to change?" One changer explains; one non-changer explains.
You won't be marked on the verdict. You'll be marked on the reason. And changing your mind for a good reason is a strength.

What each scenario sets up

ScenarioTensionChapter linkExtra push
1. The Thank-You GiftGift vs bribe; 10 jobs vs the rule1.3 relativism; 1.1 legal vs ethicalAsk anyone in procurement, compliance or government how their organization defines a gift. Home-country law can matter: e.g., the US FCPA has a narrow exception for routine "facilitating payments," while the UK Bribery Act allows none (check current rules before presenting as fact).
2. The Three-Dollar QuestionLegal wage vs fair wage; jobs vs rights1.1 law as minimum; 1.2 stakeholders"Who is missing from this story?" — consumers, shareholders, government, workers' families
3. Helping a FriendLoyalty vs a fair rule1.3 one standard vs manyLink to the syllabus integrity policy: "Where is the line with translation tools and AI?"

Debrief (3 min after Scenario 3)

  • Which scenario was hardest, and what information was missing?
  • Did your group use the same kind of reason in all three, or switch?
  • Is "everyone does it here" a reason, an excuse, or a fact?

Expected outcome

Split verdicts on all three, several groups changing after a twist, and a board full of reasons to harvest in the next stage.

Useful language (on slides)

The crux of the issue is whether…I'd distinguish between a gift and an inducement.That's legally defensible, but is it ethically defensible?If we prioritize the workers, then…I'm revising my position because…

50–65 · Criteria Harvest → the three lenses

PurposeTurn students' reasons into the book's three approaches
Time15 min
GroupingGroups → whole class
MaterialsSticky notes; board with 3 unlabeled columns + LAW / CUSTOM box
  1. 3 min — Each group writes the reasons it actually used tonight, one per sticky note.
  2. 4 min — Spokespeople read notes aloud; you place each in a column without explaining.
  3. 2 min — "What do the notes in each column have in common?" Groups guess labels.
  4. 4 min — Reveal the labels (slide) with the script below.
  5. 2 min — "Name that lens" check.

Teacher script (≈4 min)

  • ENDS — consequences. "Who is helped and who is harmed?" One version is utilitarianism: the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • MEANS — duties and rules. "Is the action itself right, whatever the result?" This is deontology, linked in the book to Immanuel Kant. Deon is Greek for duty.
  • CHARACTER. "What would a person of integrity do? Who am I becoming?" This is virtue theory. Practical wisdom (phronesis) is knowing how to act well in a real situation.
  • LAW / CUSTOM. The book calls law a minimum baseline; ethics goes further.
You were already using all three. This course gives you names for them — and shows you when they disagree.

"Name that lens" — answer key

StatementAnswer
"If the brand raises wages, 3,000 people may lose their jobs."Ends
"Copying is cheating, whatever the reason."Means
"A good friend helps — but a wise friend helps in a way that doesn't hurt you."Character (practical wisdom)
"It's legal there, so the company did nothing wrong."Law/custom — only the minimum, says the book

65–82 · Words for "Right"

PurposeAnswer "What does ethics mean in your culture?" through language
Time17 min
GroupingIndividual → pairs → groups of 4
MaterialsIndex cards (or worksheet)
  1. 3 min — Individually: the word(s) in your first language for ethics or a good person; its literal meaning or root; one saying about honesty or fairness, translated.
  2. 5 min — Pairs with different first languages explain their cards.
  3. 5 min — Groups of four find one word with no neat English equivalent and explain what English loses.
  4. 4 min — Whole class. Add English roots: ethics ← Greek ēthos (character, custom); moral ← Latin mores (customs).
If both of our English words come from "custom," is ethics just whatever my community does? Hold that question — the reading after the break answers it.
Watch for: ask for experience, not representation — "in your family," "where you've worked," never "in your country." The book uses ethics and morals interchangeably; ask whether students' languages distinguish them.

82–94 · Our Discussion Contract

PurposeStudents write the norms for respectful debate (syllabus: Respect & Inclusion)
Time12 min
GroupingGroups → class
MaterialsPaper strips, sticky dots or markers
  1. 4 min — Each group drafts two norms beginning "We challenge ideas, not people, by…"
  2. 4 min — Post them; everyone gets three dot votes.
  3. 4 min — Keep the top five. Add these if missing:
    • Restate the other person's view in a form they accept before you disagree.
    • Changing your mind for a good reason is a strength.
    • No one speaks for a whole country or culture.
    • Ask for evidence, not credentials.

Photograph the contract and show it at the start of every class.

8–33 · Diamond-9 Values Ranking

PurposeRank values (syllabus task); show words mean different things; show rankings shift by context
Time25 min
GroupingIndividual → groups of 3–4
MaterialsDiamond-9 cards (handouts) or worksheet

Cards: Honesty · Integrity · Loyalty · Fairness · Respect for the law · Respect for others · Compassion · Courage · Success. The first six come from the book's list of universal principles in 1.3; Success is the provocation.

  1. 3 min — Individual. Arrange a diamond: 1 at the top, then 2, 3, 2, and 1 at the bottom.
  2. 10 min — Group consensus. One group diamond. Rule: move a card only if you give a reason. Devil's Advocate continues.
  3. 2 min — Definition check. Each group writes a one-line definition of Loyalty and Fairness. Collect them.
  4. 6 min — Context switch. Each group re-ranks as: CEO of a 500-person public company (the book's Figure 1.5 scenario) · owner of a small family business · new manager working in another country · government official who approves permits. Mark which cards moved.
  5. 4 min — Debrief. Read the different Loyalty definitions aloud.

Debrief questions

  • Which card moved most when your role changed? Why?
  • If your ranking changes with your role, do you have one ethical standard or several?
  • Is there any card that should never move, whatever the role?
  • Where did Success end up — and is it a value or a goal?
The reading you're about to do takes a strong position on this. Let's see if you agree.

Useful language

I'd put ___ above ___ because…That depends on how we define loyalty.Loyalty to whom, though?I can live with that compromise if…

65–90 · Equifax crisis meeting + Front-Page Test

PurposeApply stakeholders, legal vs ethical, short vs long term and the lenses to a real case
Time25 min
GroupingGroups of 4–6
MaterialsFact sheet, role cards, Board Resolution sheet
It is early September 2017. You are Equifax's leadership. Nobody outside knows about the breach yet. You have 12 minutes.

Fact sheet (from the textbook only)

  • From mid-May to July 2017, hackers accessed Equifax servers and the personal information of nearly half the US population.
  • After learning of the hack in late July, executives sold nearly $2 million of their own company stock, weeks before the public announcement.
  • The company plans to offer free credit monitoring and identity-theft protection.

Agenda

  1. What do we announce, and when?
  2. What do we offer affected people? Is credit monitoring enough?
  3. What do we do about the executives who sold stock before the announcement?

Procedure

  1. 3 min — Students read role cards silently.
  2. 12 min — The CEO chairs. Each role must speak from its assigned lens at least once.
  3. 2 min — Write three decisions on the Board Resolution sheet.
  4. 3 min — Front-Page Test. Groups swap resolutions and write the headline an "unfriendly but intelligent reporter" would write (Buffett's test, section 1.2). Read headlines aloud.
  5. 5 min — "What happened next" (slide; outside sources): in July 2019 Equifax reached a settlement with the CFPB, FTC and 48 states worth up to $700 million; about 147 million consumers were affected. A former Equifax CIO, Jun Ying, was sentenced to four months in prison for insider trading, and a second employee was also convicted.
Accuracy note: the book's "nearly $2 million" refers to executives' sales. The people later convicted were separate cases — don't merge the two.

Debrief questions

  • The book (2018) said few expected managers to face penalties. What changed — and does that strengthen or weaken "ethics pays"?
  • Were the later penalties about law or about ethics?
  • Which decision would you defend on the front page? Which would you quietly change?
  • Which lens did your group rely on most? Which stakeholder had the weakest voice?

I'd like to put on record that…Legally we're covered, but…In the short term ___, but in the long term ___.Can we agree on ___ and revisit ___?I move that we…

Grammar in context

  • Past modals for judging: Maya shouldn't have paid. She could have reported it. She might have had no choice.
  • Second / mixed conditionals: If I were Ali, I would… If Maya hadn't paid, ten people would be unemployed now.
  • It depends on + wh-clause: It depends on whether it's legal / how much the gift is / who benefits.
  • Hedging: arguably, to some extent, it could be argued that, in most cases.

Homework

Model journal entry

In my previous job as a purchasing manager, a supplier offered to pay for my team's year-end dinner. It was legal, and in that industry it was common. However, I hesitated. From a consequences perspective, the dinner was harmless: my team was happy and the cost was small. From a duty perspective, though, I had a responsibility to choose suppliers fairly, and accepting the dinner could create an obligation, or at least the appearance of one. I asked myself what an unfriendly reporter would write. In the end, I declined politely and our company paid for the dinner. Today I would make the same decision, but I would also suggest a clear written gift policy, so the next manager would not have to decide alone.

Sample Quiz 1 items — answer key

  1. Match: (a) stakeholder (b) stockholder (c) goodwill (d) ethical relativism → (i) owns shares (ii) right and wrong depend only on context (iii) anyone affected by a business decision (iv) positive feeling toward a company.
    Key: a-iii, b-i, c-iv, d-ii.
  2. True or false: According to the textbook, obeying the law is enough to be ethical in business.
    Key: False — compliance is a minimum baseline; ethics goes beyond it.
  3. Lens: "We should not lie to customers, even if the lie would increase sales."
    Key: Means (deontology / duty).
  4. Lens: "This decision helps 900 people and harms 100, so it is right."
    Key: Ends (utilitarianism).
  5. Short answer: Why did Toyota sell cars at a slight loss in the US for many years?
    Key: To build long-term trust and change the belief that Japanese products were cheap and unreliable.
  6. Short answer: What is Buffett's "front-page" test, and why is it useful?
    Model: Do nothing you'd be unhappy to see an unfriendly but intelligent reporter describe on the front page. It is a simple check on whether a decision would damage trust and reputation.
