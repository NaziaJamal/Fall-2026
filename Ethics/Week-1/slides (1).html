Language for the discussion

Sound like a professional, not a textbook

The crux of the issue is whether… I'd distinguish between a gift and an inducement. That's legally defensible — but is it ethically defensible? Activate · Crisis meeting

It is early September 2017. You are Equifax's leadership.

1

From mid-May to July 2017, hackers accessed Equifax servers and the personal information of nearly half the US population.

2

After learning of the hack in late July, executives sold nearly $2 million of their own company stock — weeks before any public announcement.

3

The company plans to offer free credit monitoring and identity-theft protection.

Source: Business Ethics (OpenStax), section 1.2

12 min
The meeting · the CEO chairs

Three decisions

  1. What do we announce, and when?
  2. What do we offer affected people? Is credit monitoring enough?
  3. What do we do about the executives who sold stock early?

Roles

CEO (chair) · General Counsel · Head of Communications · Investor Relations · Customer Advocate · Chief Ethics Officer

Rule: speak from your assigned lens at least once.

Useful: "I'd like to put on record that…" · "Legally we're covered, but…" · "I move that we…"

3 min
The Front-Page Test (Warren Buffett, section 1.2)

Swap resolutions. Write the headline.

Imagine an unfriendly but intelligent reporter reading the other group's three decisions. What is tomorrow's front page?

EQUIFAX BOSSES ___________________

Read them aloud. Would you defend your decisions on that front page?

What happened next · outside sources

The book (2018) said few expected managers to face penalties.

July 2019

Equifax settled with the CFPB, FTC and 48 states for up to $700 million. About 147 million consumers were affected.

Insider trading

A former Equifax CIO, Jun Ying, was sentenced to four months in prison. A second employee was also convicted.

Sources: consumerfinance.gov (Equifax enforcement action) · Fox 5 Atlanta. The convictions were separate from the executives' sales in the book.

Debrief

Ethics, law, or both?

  1. Does what happened strengthen or weaken the claim that "ethics pays"?
  2. Were the later penalties about law, or about ethics?
  3. Which of your decisions would you defend on the front page? Which would you quietly change?
  4. Which lens did your group use most? Which stakeholder had the weakest voice?
