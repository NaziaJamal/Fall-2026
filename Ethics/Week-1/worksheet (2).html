1Ethical, Unethical, or It Depends?

Three real-world problems. No easy answers. Your group decides — and defends the decision. A bare "it depends" is not enough: name the condition.

Group roles (change roles for each scenario): Chair — keeps time and makes sure everyone speaks · Devil's Advocate — argues against the group for at least one minute · Spokesperson — defends the verdict to the class.
  1. Read the scenario (1 min).
  2. Discuss (5 min).
  3. Write your verdict and your single strongest reason.
  4. Show your verdict when the teacher says "3-2-1."
  5. Listen to the twist. Change your verdict only if you have a good reason.

Scenario 1 · The Thank-You Gift

Business ethics and local custom

Maya owns a small coffee company. She wants to open a warehouse in a new country, and she needs an official permit. The official tells her the permit takes six months.

His assistant whispers: "Most businesses give a small thank-you gift of $200. Then it takes one week."

In this country, everyone knows this is "how things work." If Maya waits six months, her company may run out of money and she will have to fire 10 workers.

Maya pays the $200. What does your group say about Maya's decision?

Push your group
  • What is the difference between a gift and a bribe?
  • Who decides what is right: the local culture, or the law in Maya's home country?
  • Is Maya responsible for her 10 workers more than for the official's country's rules?
Our verdict

Scenario 2 · The Three-Dollar Question

Global trade and responsibility

A fashion brand sells T-shirts in Europe. Its factory in another country pays workers $4 a day. This is legal, and it is higher than the average pay for jobs nearby.

A group of activists demands $8 a day. If the brand agrees, each T-shirt will cost more. The brand says it may move production to another country instead, and 3,000 workers would lose their jobs.

"A low-paying job is better than no job," says the brand's manager.

The brand decides to keep paying $4 a day. What does your group say about the brand's decision?

Push your group
  • Is "legal" the same as "ethical"?
  • Who should decide what a fair wage is: the company, the government, or the workers?
  • Should customers pay more for T-shirts? Would you?
Our verdict

Scenario 3 · Helping a Friend

Culture, loyalty, and rules

Ali is an international student. In his home country, students help each other with homework, and sharing answers shows friendship and respect.

At his new school, the rule is clear: all homework must be done alone. His friend Sara is new to the language and is falling behind. She asks to copy his answers.

"Please, Ali. If I fail this class, I have to go home."

Ali knows the rule. He shares his answers anyway. What does your group say about Ali's decision?

Push your group
  • Should you follow the rules of the place you are in, or the values you grew up with?
  • Is loyalty to a friend more important than a fair rule?
  • Is there a better way for Ali to help Sara?
Our verdict

9Equifax crisis meeting

It is early September 2017. You are Equifax's leadership. Nobody outside the company knows about the breach yet. You have 12 minutes.

The facts (from the textbook, section 1.2)

1. From mid-May to July 2017, hackers accessed Equifax servers and the personal information of nearly half the US population.

2. After learning of the hack in late July, executives sold nearly $2 million of their own company stock — weeks before any public announcement.

3. The company plans to offer free credit monitoring and identity-theft protection.

Your meeting must decide
  • What do we announce, and when?
  • What do we offer the people affected? Is credit monitoring enough?
  • What do we do about the executives who sold stock early?
Role cards — read yours only

Each role must speak from its lens at least once. "Private concerns" are role-play pressures, not historical facts.

CEO (chair)Keep the company alive and protect your reputation. You run the meeting.
Lens: character — "What kind of company do we want to be?"
Private concern: the board meets tomorrow, and you must explain every decision.
General CounselLimit legal risk. You know the rules on disclosure and insider trading.
Lens: means — "What are we required to do, and what is right to do?"
Private concern: anything said here could one day be read aloud in court.
Head of CommunicationsProtect public trust. Control the story before the media does.
Lens: ends — "What happens if we wait? If we speak now?"
Private concern: the longer we stay silent, the worse it will look.

11Ethics Journal #1

150–200 words. Describe a grey area from your work or studies (change names and details). Was it legal? Which lens did you or others use? Would you decide the same way today?

See a model journal entry

In my previous job as a purchasing manager, a supplier offered to pay for my team's year-end dinner. It was legal, and in that industry it was common. However, I hesitated. From a consequences perspective, the dinner was harmless: my team was happy and the cost was small. From a duty perspective, though, I had a responsibility to choose suppliers fairly, and accepting the dinner could create an obligation, or at least the appearance of one. I asked myself what an unfriendly reporter would write. In the end, I declined politely and our company paid for the dinner. Today I would make the same decision, but I would also suggest a clear written gift policy, so the next manager would not have to decide alone.
