The four stakeholder categories (Section 3.2)

Enabling — allow the firm to exist and operate (shareholders, board, regulators, lawmakers)
Functional — inputs and outputs: who supplies it and who buys from it (employees, suppliers, unions, customers, distributors)
Normative — shape the industry's norms (competitors, professional associations)
Diffused — less direct ties but real potential impact (media, NGOs, voters, the public)

Stakeholders can also be internal (board, CEO, employees) or external (customers, suppliers, governments). One person can be several stakeholders: an employee who is also a customer and a shareholder.

3 · Ethics thermometer

Ethical minimum = the least a company can do while still obeying the law. Ethical maximum = the strongest ethical action possible in the situation. (3.1)
Normative motive = stakeholders matter in themselves. Instrumental motive = treating stakeholders well because it's good for profit. (3.2)

Ethics Journal #3

150–200 words. Choose one prompt. Structure: Opinion → Reasons → Framework or stakeholder tool → Counterargument → Conclusion.

The three cases

A · Amazon — 3.1, box Cases from the Real World: "Amazon Sets a Demanding Pace on the Job" pp. 68–71
Business students toured an Amazon distribution center. The manager said "pickers" walk 8–9 miles per shift at the "Amazon Pace," and those who can't keep up are usually let go. Is this a reasonable expectation? Should applicants be told before they're hired? Is it ethical for customers to keep buying from the company?

B · Malaysia Airlines — 3.3, box What Would You Do? pp. 78–82
The Malaysian government and individual investors own the airline. It has lost money and gone through three CEOs. The CEO, Peter Bellew, must cut costs and raise revenue. His plan targets Malaysian Muslims — more than 60% of the population — with charter flights for the hajj pilgrimage. Should he prioritize pilgrims' demand for easy, affordable travel or the government's demand for profitable operations?

C · Two views — 3.1, box What Would You Do? "The Social Responsibility of Business" and the Link to Learning on Milton Friedman pp. 68–71
The shareholder-focused view (Milton Friedman, 1970): a business should act ethically but serve only its shareholders. The social responsibility view: stakeholders are ends in themselves, so responsibility to them should be central to the mission. The late law professor Lynn Stout argued that shareholders as a group actually want companies to treat stakeholders well. If you started a business, which would drive it?

0 words · target 150–200
Model journal (Prompt A)

I think walking eight or nine miles a shift can be a reasonable expectation, but only if the company is honest about it before people accept the job. Many jobs are physically demanding, such as nursing or construction, and workers choose them knowingly. The ethical problem is not the distance; it is whether applicants know what they are agreeing to and whether those who can't keep up are treated fairly. From a Kantian perspective, hiding the pace would treat workers merely as a means to fast delivery. In stakeholder terms, pickers are functional stakeholders with low individual power but very high interest, so the company should "keep them informed and respect their interests." One could argue that the company is only meeting the legal minimum and that customers want fast delivery. That is true, but the legal minimum is not the ethical maximum. In conclusion, the pace is acceptable only if it is disclosed, safe, and supported by fair treatment — and customers share some responsibility for the speed they demand.

Language bank

Key words in simple English
  • stakeholder — anyone affected by, or with an interest in, a business
  • stockholder / shareholder — someone who owns shares (≠ stakeholder!)
  • claim — what a stakeholder wants from a company
  • prioritize — decide what comes first
  • key stakeholder — high power + high interest
  • exigency — urgency
  • ethical minimum / maximum — the least the law allows / the best possible action
  • voiceless — affected but unable to speak (a river, future generations)
  • latent → aware → active public — doesn't know → knows → organizes
  • concession — something you give up in a negotiation
  • bottom line — (1) profit; (2) the minimum you'll accept
  • stake — an interest or share ("We have a stake in this"), not a steak!
  • normative — two meanings this week: normative stakeholders (competitors who set norms) and the normative approach (stakeholders matter in themselves)
Negotiation phrases for the town hall
  • On behalf of ___, we'd like to…
  • We could support it provided that / as long as / on condition that…
  • We won't support it unless…
  • Would you be willing to…? / What if we agreed to…?
  • We're prepared to compromise on ___, but not on…
  • That's non-negotiable for us.
  • With respect, that doesn't address…
  • No one here is speaking for…
