1The big picture in five sentences

  1. A business affects many people besides its owners — workers, customers, suppliers, neighbors, even nature. These are its stakeholders (everyone who is affected by the business or cares about what it does).
  2. Obeying the law is only the starting point; a good company often does more than the law requires.
  3. Stakeholders don't all have the same power or the same interest, so a company cannot please everyone equally.
  4. Managers must therefore prioritize (decide who comes first) — and the chapter gives tools for doing this fairly.
  5. The ethical goal is to treat stakeholders as people who matter in themselves, not only as tools for making money.
Say it in class: "A trolley problem has two groups on the tracks. A real business decision has ten — and some of them aren't in the room."

2What is a stakeholder? (3.1, pp. 68–71)

A stakeholder (any person or group with an interest in how a business works and makes its money) can be inside or outside the company. The word comes from "having a stake" (something to win or lose) in the result.

Do not confuse it with a stockholder or shareholder (a person who owns part of the company by owning shares). Every shareholder is a stakeholder, but most stakeholders are not shareholders. This is the most common confusion on the quiz.

Inside and outside

  • Internal stakeholders (people inside the company): the board of directors (a group elected to guide the company, set goals and hire the boss), the CEO (chief executive officer — the top manager), managers and employees.
  • External stakeholders (people outside the company): customers, suppliers, governments and regulators (government agencies that make and enforce rules, like the Environmental Protection Agency), communities, the media, the environment.

One person, many hats. One person can be several stakeholders at once — for example, an employee who also buys the company's products and owns some of its shares.

The unwritten promise

The book says a company has an implicit social contract (an unwritten promise between a company and society: "we will treat you fairly") with its stakeholders. When a company breaks it, trust falls — and so do profits.

Two opposite views of business

Shareholder-focused viewSocial responsibility view
Business should act ethically, but its job is to make money for its owners. Linked to economist Milton Friedman, who wrote a famous 1970 article arguing that "social responsibilities" would damage capitalism (an economy based on private businesses and free markets).Stakeholders are ends in themselves (important for their own sake, not just useful), so caring for them should be part of the company's mission. The book links this to Kant's ethics from Week 2.

The book adds the view of law professor Lynn Stout: shareholders as a group actually want companies to treat stakeholders well, but individual shareholders sometimes push for quick profits at others' expense. The book notes Friedman's view was the majority view in 1970 but has since fallen out of favor.

3Law vs. ethics: the minimum and the maximum (3.1)

TermMeaningExample from the book
Ethical minimum (the least a company can do and still obey the law)Doing only what the law requiresSamsung's first response to its faulty washers: a warning
Ethical maximum (the best, strongest ethical action possible)Going as far as possible to do right by stakeholdersSamsung's final response: full refunds for about 3 million washers and free pickup

The key lesson: laws do not cover every ethical duty. Laws are slow, incomplete and different in each country. A company that does only the legal minimum may still hurt people and lose its reputation.

Link to Week 1: Students already heard "the law is the minimum; ethics goes beyond it." This week puts a scale on that idea — the "ethics thermometer."

4Why do stakeholders matter? (3.2, pp. 72–77)

Researchers Donaldson and Preston described three reasons, or "approaches," for paying attention to stakeholders:

ApproachSimple meaningClassroom shorthand
Descriptive (describing how things are)A company simply is a collection of stakeholder groups."This is what a company looks like."
Instrumental (used as a tool to reach a goal)Treat stakeholders well because it's good for profit."Be nice because it pays."
Normative (about what is right; how things should be)Stakeholders matter in themselves, whether or not it pays. Donaldson and Preston see this as the most appropriate basis."Be fair because it's right."
Watch out — "normative" has two meanings this week. (1) The normative approach above (stakeholders matter because it's right). (2) Normative stakeholders in Part 5 (competitors and associations that shape the rules of an industry). Point this out to students so they don't mix them up.

A useful fact from the book: in a 2005 study, 80% of companies believed they gave customers a "superior" (excellent) experience, but only 8% of customers agreed. Companies often don't know what their stakeholders really think.

Not all claims are equal

A claim (what a stakeholder wants from the company, or a concern about how the company affects them) deserves to be heard, but not every claim gets the same weight (importance). Resources are limited, so managers must choose.

  • Direct claims (claims from groups that can speak loudly): e.g., a union asking for better pay.
  • Indirect or "voiceless" claims (claims from people or things that can't easily speak for themselves): small groups facing big companies, future generations, the environment.
  • In 1972, law professor Christopher Stone wrote "Should Trees Have Standing?" He asked whether nature itself could have legal standing (the right to be represented in court). This gave environmental groups a new way to argue.

How the public "wakes up": Grunig and Hunt's four publics

A public (a group of people connected by a shared problem) can be in one of four stages:

  1. Nonpublic (there is no problem, or no one is affected)
  2. Latent public (a problem exists, but people don't know about it yet; "latent" = hidden)
  3. Aware public (people now know about the problem)
  4. Active public (people know and organize to do something)

The ethical point: if a company knows about a problem before the public does, it should communicate honestly. In the Harbor Bay activity, Event Card 2 (the newspaper story) moves residents from latent → aware → active.

5Sorting stakeholders: the four categories (3.2, Figure 3.4)

CategorySimple meaningExamplesMemory hook
Enabling(they make it possible for the company to exist and operate)shareholders, board of directors, government regulators, lawmakers"They give permission."
Functional(they make the business work day to day — what goes in and what comes out)inputs (what the company needs): employees, suppliers, unions · outputs (where products go): customers, distributors, stores"They keep the machine running."
Normative(they shape the "normal" rules and habits of an industry)competitors, professional associations"They set the standard."
Diffused(spread out and less directly connected, but they can still have a big effect)the media, NGOs (non-governmental organizations — independent nonprofit groups like environmental or human-rights groups), voters"They watch and react."

The first three are always present because the company can't operate without them. Diffused stakeholders change depending on the place and the moment.

The ripple effect (from the book): an NGO points out unequal pay on a rubber plantation that supplies a parts maker. The supplier raises pay. Then either the customer pays more, or the shareholders earn less. One stakeholder's action spreads to others.

Common student mistake: putting regulators in "diffused." Regulators are enabling — they give the company permission to operate.

6Deciding whose claim comes first (3.3, pp. 78–82)

Stakeholder management (understanding stakeholders' claims correctly and responding to them well) starts with listing the stakeholders, then ranking them, then weighing their claims.

The four questions a manager asks

  1. Membership (Is this person really part of an important group?) — e.g., is this angry social-media post from a real customer?
  2. The company's need (What do we need from this group, and when?)
  3. The survival test (Could the company survive without them, or replace them easily? If not, they come first.)
  4. Exigency (urgency — how urgent is their claim right now?) Pronounced EK-si-jen-see.

The power–interest matrix (Figure 3.5)

A matrix (a grid with two lines crossing, making four boxes) that places stakeholders by two things: power (how much they can influence the company) and interest (how much they care about what the company does — not bank interest!).

Low interestHigh interest
High powerKeep satisfied (meet their needs so they stay happy)Manage closely (talk to them often; involve them) — these are the key stakeholders (the most important ones)
Low powerMonitor lightly (keep an eye on them)Keep informed (tell them what's happening; respect their interests)

Stakeholders move. The book's example: a small farmer selling to local shops is low power, low interest. When the farmer sells a whole crop to Kroger (a very large U.S. supermarket chain), the farmer becomes low power, high interest — Kroger now matters hugely to the farmer, so the farmer will likely make price concessions (accept a lower price).

What can move a stakeholder up in power? Organizing, media attention, a new law, a lawsuit, voting.

Managing expectations

Every stakeholder expects something: shareholders want a good return (profit on their investment); employees want interesting, safe work, job security and fair pay; communities want a clean environment and good quality of life. Companies learn these expectations through surveys, focus groups, sales data and social media.

The book's ethical bottom line: ethically, no stakeholder group should be treated as better than another — but limited resources force companies to prioritize. Listening to stakeholders is both right and good for business. Stakeholders also have a duty: to make their wishes known.

7The cases, explained

All from the textbook unless marked "outside source." Use these facts only; don't add details students might quote as fact.

CaseWhat happenedThe teaching point
Starbucks, 2018 (Ch. 3 intro, p. 67)Two Black men waiting for a friend in a Philadelphia store were arrested. Starbucks closed about 8,000 U.S. stores for an afternoon of racial-bias training (training to recognize unfair treatment based on race) and changed its policy so anyone can use its stores and restrooms without buying. It cost billions.Moving from the ethical minimum to near the maximum. Also instrumental: it limited Samsung's legal exposure (risk of being sued).

United Airlines (3.2)The airline removed ticketed, seated passengers to make room for four employees who needed to travel. Overbooking (selling more tickets than seats) is a legal practice.Legal is not the same as acceptable. Which stakeholder did United value more — its staff schedule or its customers?
Ford Edsel, 1958 (3.2)Ford researched and launched a luxury car, but customers didn't see Ford as a luxury brand. The model failed within three years.Misreading stakeholders hurts investors, suppliers and employees too.
Tylenol, 1982 (3.2)Seven people near Chicago died after someone put cyanide (a deadly poison) into Tylenol capsules on store shelves. It was not the company's fault. Market share fell from 37% to 7%, then recovered to 30% within a year. The industry adopted tamper-resistant packaging (packages that show if someone has opened them).The textbook's model of an enlightened (wise, forward-thinking) response — near the ethical maximum.
Volkswagen (3.2)VW's diesel cars produced more pollution than U.S. law allowed. Engineers installed a device that made the cars appear to pass the tests. Costs exceeded $30 billion by 2017; some employees went to jail.Below the legal minimum. Loyal customers became an angry active public.
Malaysia Airlines (3.3, journal option B)Owned by the government and private investors; losing money after two unexplained crashes in 2014. The CEO planned affordable charter flights for the hajj (the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca), targeting Malaysian Muslims (over 60% of the population).Two stakeholders with competing claims: pilgrims (affordable travel) vs. the government (profit). Handle respectfully — it involves religion.
IKEA (3.3)In the U.S., IKEA learned customers thought it sold only European-size beds, so it displayed its king-size beds and sales grew. In China, it allows customers to nap on display furniture.Stakeholder expectations differ by country — a good cross-cultural discussion point.
Harbor Bay / Lumen Data (fictional)A company wants to build an AI data center (a large building full of computers that run online services and AI; it needs lots of electricity and water for cooling) in a small town. It asks for a 10-year tax break (permission to pay less tax).A realistic, current case with every stakeholder type — including voiceless ones (the river, future residents). Students should know it is invented.
Sensitive topics: Starbucks involves racial bias and police; Malaysia Airlines involves religion and two plane crashes. Keep discussion on the company's decisions, and never ask a student to speak for their race, religion or country.

8The trolley problem and the Moral Machine

The trolley problem (a famous thought experiment: a runaway tram will kill five people unless you redirect it to kill one) is not in Chapter 3. The syllabus names it, and a streetcar version is linked in Section 2.4. We use it to start the week because it connects to the Week 2 frameworks:

  • The switch: most people pull it. This is utilitarian thinking (choose what produces the most good for the most people — five lives are more than one).
  • The footbridge: most people refuse to push the man, even though the numbers are the same. This is Kantian thinking (never use a person merely as a means — just as a tool — to reach a goal).
  • The self-driving car: the passenger is a customer; the pedestrians are not. This turns an ethics puzzle into a stakeholder problem.
  • The layoff trolley: real business decisions come with uncertainty (we don't know the result for sure), so "it depends" and "we need more information" are good answers.

The Moral Machine (outside source, not the textbook): an online experiment by MIT researchers (Awad et al., Nature, 2018) that collected 40 million decisions from people in 233 countries and territories about self-driving car dilemmas. Answers grouped into three broad regional clusters (groups of similar countries): West, East and South. For example, participants from China and Japan were less likely to spare young people over old people; participants from the UK and US gave more weight to saving more lives. Caveat (warning): participants were volunteers, so the results describe tendencies, not whole nations.

Quick reminder of Week 2 frameworks: Utilitarianism (judge by results: most happiness for most people — Bentham, Mill) · Deontology (judge by duty and motive; don't use people as tools — Kant) · Justice as fairness (choose rules as if you didn't know who you'd be — Rawls's "veil of ignorance") · Virtue ethics (judge by character: what would a good person do? — Aristotle, Confucius)

9Questions students may ask you

"Isn't every stakeholder just a customer in the end?"

No. Some never buy anything — a river, a neighbor, a future resident, a regulator. That's why the four categories exist.

"If J&J's sales recovered, wasn't its decision just smart business?"

It can be both. The book presents it as normative (right in itself) and good for business. Ask students: would it still have been right if sales had never recovered?

"Who decides who the 'key' stakeholders are?"

Managers do — which is exactly the ethical risk. The matrix describes power; it doesn't say who deserves attention. That's why the book prefers the normative approach.

"How can a river be a stakeholder?"

It is affected by the company's decisions but can't speak. Groups like environmental NGOs can speak for it. Stone's 1972 article asked whether nature should have legal standing.

"Is the Amazon example still true today?"

The book reports what one manager told a student group; it is not a current company policy. Treat it as a case for discussion, not current fact.

"Is Harbor Bay a real town?"

No. It's invented so nobody needs outside knowledge and no real company is being judged. But data centers and their water and electricity use are a real, current issue in many countries.

"Is a stakeholder the same as a stockholder?"

No. A stockholder owns shares. A stakeholder is anyone affected. All stockholders are stakeholders, but not the other way around.

10Coming next week: CSR (3.4, from p. 83 — homework reading)

  • CSR — corporate social responsibility (the idea that a company should care for society and the environment, not only make products and profits).
  • Sustainability (using resources in a way that protects them for the long term).
  • Triple bottom line (measuring success in three ways: people, planet and profit — not just profit), described by British consultant John Elkington in 1994. "Bottom line" (the final profit figure on a financial report).
  • Externalities (costs or benefits a business creates for others but doesn't pay for, like pollution).
  • Greenwashing (pretending to be environmentally responsible for good publicity while hiding real problems). Book examples: Ben & Jerry's and Coca-Cola's water use.

We only introduce this in Week 3. Students bring an example of a company that says it is green for Week 4's "CSR or greenwashing?" card sort.
