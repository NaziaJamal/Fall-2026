Part 1 · Who are the stakeholders? (Class 1)

In one line: A stakeholder is anyone with an interest in how a business operates and makes its profit. A stockholder only owns shares.

Seeing stakeholders (3.1)

  • Internal: board of directors, CEO, executives, employees. External: customers, suppliers, governments, communities, the media, the environment.
  • One person can hold several roles — an employee who is also a customer and a shareholder.
  • All stakeholders matter, but they do not have equal priority or influence.
  • Implicit social contract: an unwritten promise between a company and its stakeholders. Breaking it damages trust and profit.

Ethical minimum vs. ethical maximum (3.1)

Ethical minimumEthical maximum
The least a company can do while still obeying the lawThe strongest ethical action possible in the situation
Samsung's first response: a warning onlySamsung's final response: full refunds for ~3 million washers, free pickup
"Laws don't capture every ethical responsibility. Relying on the legal minimum can harm people and destroy a reputation."

Why do stakeholders matter? (3.2 · Donaldson & Preston)

  • Descriptive: the firm is a set of stakeholder groups.
  • Instrumental: treating stakeholders well is useful — it helps profit.
  • Normative: stakeholders matter in themselves. The textbook (following Donaldson and Preston) calls this the most appropriate basis.

How publics wake up (3.2 · Grunig & Hunt)

Nonpublic (no problem) → latent (a problem exists but isn't recognized) → aware (people recognize it) → active (people organize and act). Example: VW's loyal customers became an active public after the emissions scandal.

Voiceless claims (3.2)

Some stakeholders can't speak easily: small groups facing big bureaucracies, future generations, the environment. In 1972, law professor Christopher Stone asked, "Should Trees Have Standing?" — could nature itself have legal rights?

Common confusions: stakeholder ≠ stockholder · "normative" has two meanings this week: normative stakeholders (competitors) and the normative approach (stakeholders matter in themselves) · "interest" ≠ bank interest.

Part 2 · Whose claim comes first? (Class 2)

In one line: A firm can't satisfy everyone at once, so it must prioritize — ethically, not just by who shouts loudest.

The power–interest matrix (3.3, Figure 3.5)

Low interestHigh interest
High powerKeep satisfied, meet needsKeep engaged, manage closely — key stakeholders
Low powerMonitor lightlyKeep informed, respect interests

Positions move. A small farmer selling to local shops is low power, low interest. After selling a whole crop to Kroger, the farmer is low power, high interest — and will likely make price concessions.

Tests for prioritizing (3.3)

  • Membership: is this person really part of a key stakeholder group?
  • The firm's need: what do we need from them, and when?
  • Survival test: could the firm survive without them, or replace them easily?
  • Exigency: how urgent is the claim right now?

The model case: Tylenol, 1982 (3.2)

Seven people near Chicago died after someone put cyanide in Tylenol capsules in stores. J&J pulled all Tylenol from the U.S. market — about 30 million bottles, over $100 million — guided by its credo. Market share fell from 37% to 7%, then recovered to 30% within a year. The industry adopted tamper-resistant packaging.

"J&J went far beyond the legal minimum. Was it ethical, or smart? The textbook's answer: both — and that's the point."

Coming next: CSR and the triple bottom line (3.4 → Week 4)

CSR: a company's responsibilities go beyond good products and fair wages to the environment and society. Triple bottom line (John Elkington, 1994): measure people, planet and profit. Greenwashing: CSR as public relations that hides real problems.
