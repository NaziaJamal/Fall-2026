Part C · Short case analysis 12 points

Maple & Co. (fictional) is a mid-sized furniture retailer. Its best-selling baby crib is finished with a paint that contains a chemical. The chemical is legal in Maple's home country but banned in the European Union because of possible health risks to children. No child has been reported harmed. Recalling and replacing every crib would cost about $4 million — roughly one year's profit. The supplier says changing the paint will take six months.
  1. 4 pointsIdentify four stakeholders in this decision. Classify each one as enabling, functional, normative or diffused.
    Any four, 1 point each (½ for the stakeholder, ½ for a correct category). Examples: shareholders/owners — enabling; government regulators — enabling; parents/customers — functional; babies (voiceless) — functional/diffused, accept with reason; paint supplier — functional; employees — functional; competitors — normative; furniture industry association — normative; media / parent groups / consumer NGOs — diffused.
  2. 4 pointsWhat would the ethical minimum be here? What would the ethical maximum be? Which do you recommend, and why?
    Minimum (1): keep selling — the paint is legal at home (perhaps stop EU sales). Maximum (1): stop sales now, recall/replace or refund, warn customers, change the paint. Recommendation with a reason (2): e.g., stop sales and offer refunds/replacement because babies are vulnerable, voiceless stakeholders and trust/reputation are at stake (J&J model); or a reasoned middle path (stop sales + warn + free replacement as stock allows). Accept any position that is clearly reasoned.
  3. 4 pointsHow would a utilitarian and a Kantian each assess the decision to keep selling the crib?
    Utilitarian (2): weighs benefits (profit, jobs, low prices) against harms (possible harm to many children, lost trust, future lawsuits); likely concludes the risk to many outweighs a one-year profit, but must count everyone affected. Kantian (2): keeping a known risk hidden from parents treats them and their children merely as a means to profit, and "sell products you suspect are unsafe" could not be a universal rule — wrong regardless of the outcome.
