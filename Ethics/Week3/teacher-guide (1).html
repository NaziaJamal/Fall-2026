Week 3 at a glance

In Weeks 1–2, students judged single decisions. This week they learn that every real decision has a crowd of people standing around it — and that someone has to decide whose claim comes first. They leave able to say "The key stakeholder here is X, because…, but we can't ignore Y, who has no voice."

Learning goals

By the end of Week 3, students can:

  1. Identify the stakeholders in a business decision and classify them as internal/external and as enabling, functional, normative or diffused.
  2. Distinguish an ethical minimum (legal compliance) from an ethical maximum, with real examples.
  3. Place stakeholders on a power–interest matrix and explain how their position can change.
  4. Prioritize competing stakeholder claims using the survival test, urgency (exigency) and power–interest — and recognize "voiceless" stakeholders.
  5. Negotiate a position, propose conditions and make concessions using B2+ language: "We could support it provided that…"

Big ethical questions

  • Is it ever right to sacrifice one group's interests for a larger group's benefit?
  • Do all stakeholders deserve equal consideration, or only some consideration?
  • Who speaks for those with no voice — the environment, future generations, people far away?
  • Is treating stakeholders well ethical if you only do it because it's profitable?
  • When the law allows something, why should a company do more?

Engage 9:00–9:20 · Trolley Problem variations: four corners

PurposeSyllabus case study. Students physically commit to a choice, see the class split, and discover that small changes to a scenario change their answer. It reactivates Week 2's frameworks and sets up the week's question: who counts, and how much? GroupingWhole class, moving; pairs inside each corner MaterialsFour corner signs (handouts p.1–2): ACT · DON'T ACT · IT DEPENDS · I NEED MORE INFORMATION; slides 3–9; worksheet §1 BookNot in Chapter 3. Streetcar video: 2.4 Link to Learning (pp. 44–49). Moral Machine: outside source.

Procedure (about 4 minutes per round): Show the scenario. Students walk to a corner. Inside each corner, pairs agree on their single best reason (1 min). Take one reason from each corner. Then ask: "Who wants to move after hearing that?"

RoundScenario (on the slide)What usually happens / what to draw out
1 · The switchA runaway trolley will kill five workers on the track. You can pull a switch to send it onto a side track, where it will kill one worker. Do you pull it?Most go to ACT. Name it: "That's a utilitarian answer: five lives are more than one."
2 · The footbridgeSame trolley. You're on a bridge next to a man with a heavy backpack. Pushing him onto the track will stop the trolley and save the five. Do you push him?Most move to DON'T ACT. The numbers didn't change. Ask: "What changed?" Draw out Kant: you're using him merely as a means.
3 · The self-driving carYou design the software for a self-driving car. Its brakes fail. It can stay in its lane and hit five pedestrians, or swerve into a wall and kill its one passenger. What should the code do? Would you buy that car?Students often split between "save five" and "would never buy it." That's the gap between ethics and the market — a stakeholder problem: passengers are customers, pedestrians aren't.
4 · The layoff trolleyYour company is losing money. If you lay off 50 people now, your forecast says you will avoid laying off 500 people next year. The forecast is 70% likely to be right. Do you do it?Uncertainty sends people to IT DEPENDS and NEED MORE INFO. Reward this: "Real business trolleys never come with certainty — or with only two tracks."

Cross-cultural reveal (3 min · slide 8)

MIT's Moral Machine experiment collected 40 million decisions from people in 233 countries and territories about self-driving car dilemmas (Awad et al., Nature, 2018). Countries formed three broad clusters (West, East, South). For example, participants from collectivist cultures such as China and Japan were less likely to spare the young over the old; participants from individualistic cultures such as the UK and US placed more weight on sparing more lives. Caveat: participants were self-selected, internet-connected volunteers.

"Who should the car protect? It depends on where the programmer grew up. Now — if a company sells that car in 50 countries, whose ethics does it program? That's a stakeholder question."
Avoid stereotyping: These are statistical tendencies in volunteer data, not facts about any student. Ask "Does this match what you'd expect from where you've lived or worked?" — never "Is this true of your country?"

Bridge to the week (1 min)

"Every trolley problem has two groups on the tracks. Real decisions have ten — and some of them aren't even in the room. Today: how to see all of them, and how to decide whose claim comes first."
Language: "I'd pull the lever, on the grounds that…" · "What changed for me is…" · "I'm in the 'it depends' corner because…" · "Before deciding, I'd need to know…" · "I've moved because ___ made a good point about…"

Study 9:20–9:32 · Starbucks stakeholder race

PurposeStudents define "stakeholder" by finding them, then learn the textbook categories by sorting their own list. GroupingTeams of 3–4 MaterialsSlides 11–13 (the case and categories); worksheet §2 (self-check sort); paper BookCh. 3 intro (p. 67, Starbucks); 3.1 "Stakeholder Relationships" (pp. 68–71); 3.2 "Defining Stakeholder Categories," Figure 3.4 (pp. 72–77)
  1. (1 min) Read the case on the slide (from the Chapter 3 introduction): In May 2018, two Black men were arrested while waiting for a friend in a Philadelphia Starbucks. Starbucks then closed about 8,000 U.S. stores for an afternoon of racial-bias training, and changed its policy so people can use its stores and restrooms without buying anything.
  2. (3 min) Race: "List every person or group affected by Starbucks's decision. One point per stakeholder no other team has."
  3. (2 min) Teams read lists; others call out duplicates. Award points.
  4. (2 min) Mini-input (slide 12): stakeholder = anyone with an interest in how a business makes its profit (≠ stockholder/shareholder, who owns shares). Internal vs. external. One person can hold several roles: an employee who is also a customer and a shareholder (3.1).
  5. (4 min) Teach the four categories (Figure 3.4) and have teams sort their lists (worksheet §2):
CategoryMeaning (3.2)Starbucks examples
EnablingAllow the firm to exist and operateShareholders, board of directors, regulators, legislators
FunctionalInputs (supply it) and outputs (buy from it)Baristas, coffee and tea growers, milk producers, distributors, customers
NormativeShape the industry's normsCompetitors (other coffee chains), industry/professional associations
DiffusedLess direct ties, but real potential impactMedia, NGOs and civil-rights groups, voters, social-media users, the Philadelphia community

Debrief: Which stakeholder did no team list? (Often: growers, the two men's families, other customers of color, police.) Why might the people who lose the most be the easiest to forget?

Sensitivity: The case involves racial bias and police. Keep the focus on the company's decision and its stakeholders, not on individual experiences of policing. Don't call on students to share personal experiences of discrimination.

Study 9:32–9:52 · Ethics thermometer: minimum or maximum?

PurposeTeach the ethical minimum/maximum (3.1) and normative vs. instrumental motives (3.2) through six real textbook cases. Students argue placement on a scale rather than memorize definitions. GroupingGroups of 3–4 MaterialsSix case cards + scale strip (handouts p.3); worksheet §3 Book3.1 "Ethical Responsibilities Often Extend Beyond Legal Requirements" + Amazon box (pp. 68–71); 3.2 "The Ethical Basis of Stakeholders' Claims," United, Tylenol box, Volkswagen (pp. 72–77)

The scale: ❄ Below the law → Legal minimum → Beyond the law → 🔥 Ethical maximum

  1. (1 min) Define the two ends (slide 14): ethical minimum = the least a company can do while still complying with the law; ethical maximum = the strongest possible ethical action in the situation.
  2. (8 min) Groups read the six cards and place each on the scale. For each, they also decide: normative (stakeholders matter in themselves) or instrumental (good stakeholder treatment because it's good for profit)?
  3. (6 min) Thermometer line: Put the scale on the board. One group places a card; any group that disagrees must say "Objection!" and give a reason. Move cards only if the class agrees.
  4. (5 min) Debrief.

Case cards and suggested placement

Case (textbook)Facts on the cardSuggested placement
Volkswagen (3.2)Diesel engines exceeded U.S. emissions standards. Instead of redesigning them, engineers installed a device that made the cars appear to pass. Fines and costs exceeded $30 billion by Sept. 2017; some employees went to jail.❄ Below the law
United Airlines (3.2)Removed ticketed, seated passengers from a flight to make room for four employees who needed to fly to another airport. Overbooking avoids unsold seats.Legal minimum (or below — debate it)
Amazon (3.1)At a distribution center, "pickers" walk 8–9 miles per shift at the "Amazon Pace." Those who can't keep up are usually let go.Legal minimum (debatable: are applicants told?)
Samsung (3.1)2017: a spin-cycle problem in washers. A warning wasn't enough. Final fix: full refund for about 3 million washers ($450–$1,500) with free pickup. Cost billions; limited its legal exposure.Beyond the law (motive partly instrumental)
Starbucks (Ch. 3 intro)2018: closed about 8,000 U.S. stores for an afternoon of racial-bias training; changed its store-use policy. Costly; "believed it was the right thing to do."Beyond the law → near maximum
Johnson & Johnson (3.2)1982: seven people in the Chicago area died after someone put cyanide in Tylenol capsules in stores. J&J pulled all Tylenol from the U.S. market — about 30 million bottles, over $100 million — guided by its credo. Market share fell from 37% to 7%, then recovered to 30% within a year.🔥 Ethical maximum — the textbook's model case

Debrief questions:

  • J&J's market share recovered. Does that make its decision instrumental? Can a decision be both?
  • Which case was hardest to place? What would you need to know?
  • United's overbooking is legal. Why did the public react as if it wasn't?
  • "The law is only the ethical minimum." Where did you hear that idea before? (Week 1: legal vs. ethical.)
Answer note: There is no single right placement except VW (illegal) and J&J (the textbook presents it as the model of enlightened stakeholder management). Grade the reasoning, not the position.
Language: "This sits just above the legal minimum because…" · "Strictly speaking, it was legal; however…" · "Objection! I'd put it lower, since…" · "Their motive seems instrumental rather than normative."

Study 9:52–10:02 · The power–interest matrix

PurposeGive students the main tool for prioritizing (Figure 3.5), and show that positions move. GroupingPairs MaterialsSlides 16–18; worksheet §4 (self-check) Book3.3 "Stakeholder Prioritization," Figure 3.5 (pp. 78–82)
  1. (3 min) Teach the matrix:
    Low interestHigh interest
    High powerKeep satisfied, meet needsKeep engaged, manage closely — key stakeholders
    Low powerMonitor lightlyKeep informed, respect interests
    Add the two tests from 3.3: survival test (can the firm survive without them, or replace them easily?) and exigency (how urgent is the claim right now?).
  2. (4 min) Pairs place six stakeholders of a neighborhood café chain (worksheet self-check): bank that lent the money (high power, low interest) · head barista (low power, high interest) · city health inspector (high power, low interest) · regular customers (low power, high interest) · main investor (high power, high interest) · a tourist who visits once (low, low).
  3. (3 min) Movement (3.3): A small farmer sells seasonally to local shops — low power, low interest. Then the farmer sells a whole crop to Kroger. Where does the farmer move? (Low power, high interest: Kroger matters enormously to the farmer, so the farmer will likely make price concessions.) Ask: "What could move a stakeholder up in power?" (organizing, media attention, a law, a lawsuit).
Answer note: Reasonable students will disagree about the health inspector (power is high, interest rises when there's a problem). That's the point of exigency.

Activate 10:02–10:32 · Map Harbor Bay

PurposeSyllabus stakeholder-mapping activity, on a realistic case. Event cards make the map move, showing that stakeholder priority is not fixed. GroupingGroups of 4 MaterialsHarbor Bay brief (handouts p.5); blank matrix (handouts p.4, A3 if possible); sticky notes; event cards (handouts p.6); worksheet §5 Book3.2 Grunig & Hunt's publics; "Assigning Weight to Claims" (pp. 72–77); 3.3 Figure 3.5 (pp. 78–82). Harbor Bay is fictional.

The case (fictional)

Lumen Data, a technology company, wants to build a large AI data center on farmland outside Harbor Bay (population 18,000), a coastal town with dry summers. Its proposal:

  • A $1.2 billion investment; about 1,500 construction jobs for two years, then 120 permanent jobs.
  • In return: a 10-year property-tax break. After that, about $9 million a year in local taxes.
  • The cooling system would use up to 2 million liters of water a day from the town's water supply.
  • The center would use as much electricity as a small city. The utility says this may raise residents' electricity rates.
  • Lumen offers a $500,000-a-year community fund.

The Town Council will vote next week: approve, approve with conditions, delay for an independent study, or reject.

  1. (10 min) Groups list every stakeholder on sticky notes (one per note) and place them on the matrix. Use one color for stakeholders who can speak for themselves and another for voiceless ones (the river, wildlife, future residents, people who will pay higher electricity bills but don't know yet).
  2. (4 min) Label each note E / F / N / D (enabling, functional, normative, diffused). Circle the key stakeholders.
  3. (10 min) Event cards. Read one card every 2 minutes (slides 21–25). After each, groups move their notes. They write the move in worksheet §5.
  4. (6 min) Quick gallery: groups look at one other group's map. Debrief.

Event cards

#EventExpected move
1The state declares a drought warning for the region.Farmers and water utility: interest ↑. Water becomes urgent (exigency).
2The Harbor Bay Gazette publishes the water figures. Residents had not known.Residents move from a latent public to an aware, then active public (Grunig & Hunt): interest ↑, power ↑ (they vote and organize).
3The building-trades union endorses the project and promises 300 members at the vote.Union: power ↑. Construction workers become a key stakeholder.
4A town 40 km away offers Lumen free land.Lumen's bargaining power ↑ — a credible threat to leave. The council's power ↓.
5River Keepers, an environmental group, files a complaint "on behalf of the river."A voiceless stakeholder gains a voice ("Should Trees Have Standing?" — Stone, 1972, in 3.2). NGO: power ↑.

Debrief questions:

  • Which stakeholder moved the most? Which never moved?
  • Who was voiceless on your first map? Did anyone gain a voice? How?
  • Apply the survival test: which stakeholders could Lumen not do without? Which could the town not do without?
  • Is the power–interest matrix descriptive, instrumental or normative? (It describes; used only to manage the powerful, it becomes instrumental.)
Language: "We'd place ___ in the top right because…" · "After the drought warning, the farmers move up/across…" · "Nobody is speaking for…" · "Their power comes from…"

Activate 11:16–11:56 · Harbor Bay town hall

PurposeSyllabus role-play on competing stakeholder interests. Students argue, negotiate conditions and compromise in role, then the Council must justify whose claims it prioritized. Grouping10 roles; 2–3 students per role (fewer roles with a small class — see note) MaterialsRole cards (handouts p.7–8); town hall agenda and Council decision sheet (handouts p.9); worksheet §7; bell Book3.3 "Stakeholder Prioritization" and "Managing Stakeholder Expectations" (pp. 78–82). The town hall is a fictional simulation.

Roles

RoleCategoryPublic positionSecret (on the card only)
Town Council (chair + members)EnablingNeutral; runs the hearing; votesThe town's budget has a $4 million gap next year. Two members face re-election.
Lumen Data (VP of Site Development)The firmApprove as proposedLumen can accept a closed-loop cooling system (uses ~80% less water, costs 8% more) — but only if the tax break stays. The rival town's offer is real but has weaker power lines.

Smaller class? Essential roles: Council (2), Lumen (2), Residents, Farmers, Union, River Keepers. Add Water Utility, Main Street, Youth Council and Gazette as numbers allow. With 8 students: Council 2, Lumen 1, Residents, Farmers, Union, River Keepers, Gazette.

Procedure

  1. (7 min) Prep. Each group completes worksheet §7: our position · what we most need · our bottom line · one concession we could offer · a 45-second opening statement. The Council prepares three questions and agrees on how it will decide.
  2. (12 min) Round 1 — Opening statements. Chair calls each group: 45 seconds each, strictly timed (bell). The Gazette may ask one question after any statement.
  3. (10 min) Round 2 — The lobby. Free negotiation. Groups move around the room to make deals ("We'll support you if…"). Any agreement must be written on a slip and signed by both groups. The Council listens but may not make deals.
  4. (4 min) Round 3 — Final offers. Each group gives a 20-second final statement, announcing any deals.
  5. (5 min) Round 4 — Council deliberation (fishbowl). The Council deliberates aloud in the middle; everyone else listens silently. They must name (1) the key stakeholders, (2) the test they used to prioritize, (3) one voiceless stakeholder they considered. Then they vote and announce the decision with conditions.
  6. (2 min) The Gazette reads its headline and story.
Teacher role: You are the "state observer." Don't decide anything. Intervene only to keep time, or with one surprise if the room goes quiet: "The Water Utility has just been asked for its drought figures." This reveals the secret if nobody has found it.
Language for the town hall: "On behalf of ___, we'd like to…" · "We could support the proposal provided that…" · "We're prepared to compromise on ___, but not on…" · "With respect, that doesn't address…" · "Would you be willing to…?" · "Our bottom line is…" · "Let me put it another way…"

Reflect 11:56–12:06 · Debrief: whose claim won, and why?

Book link for the debrief: 3.2 normative vs. instrumental (pp. 72–77); 3.3 prioritizing (pp. 78–82).

Out of role first: "Take off your role. Back to yourself." (This matters after an emotional role-play.)

  • Which stakeholders turned out to be the key stakeholders? Were they the ones you put top right on your map this morning?
  • Reveal the secrets: each group reads its secret aloud. Which secrets changed the outcome? Which never came out? What does that say about real hearings?
  • Did the Council use the survival test, urgency, or power? Was that ethical, or just practical?
  • Who spoke for the river and for future residents? Was that enough?
  • Week 2 link: Which framework did the Council's decision reflect — utilitarian (most benefit), Kantian (no one used merely as a means), or Rawlsian (protect the most vulnerable)?

Expected outcome: Most councils approve with conditions (closed-loop cooling, local hiring, a rate guarantee, a review clause). Point out that this is the textbook's normative ideal in action: balancing claims rather than simply obeying the most powerful.

Review 12:06–12:18 · Lie Detector

PurposeA fast, competitive review of Chapters 1–3 just before the quiz. GroupingTeams of 3–4 with mini-whiteboards or paper MaterialsSlides 37–43 BookChapter 1 (from p. 7), Chapter 2 (pp. 27–66), Chapter 3 (pp. 67–92)

Each round, the slide shows four statements. Three are true; one is a lie. Teams have 60 seconds to write the letter of the lie and correct it. 1 point for the lie, 1 bonus point for a correct correction.

RoundThe lieCorrection
1 · Chapter 1"The law sets the highest standard of ethical behavior."The law is the minimum; ethics goes beyond it.

Words students may misunderstand

  • stake — here, an interest or share (as in "have a stake in"), not a steak, a wooden post, or only a bet.
  • stakeholder vs. stockholder — the most common confusion on the quiz.
  • interest — concern or involvement, not bank interest.
  • claim — a demand or request, not only an insurance claim or a statement.
  • bottom line — has two meanings (profit; your minimum position).
  • normative — used twice in Chapter 3: a normative stakeholder (competitors who set norms) and the normative approach (stakeholders matter in themselves). Point this out!

Model journal (Prompt A, ~180 words)

I think walking eight or nine miles a shift can be a reasonable expectation, but only if the company is honest about it before people accept the job. Many jobs are physically demanding, such as nursing or construction, and workers choose them knowingly. The ethical problem is not the distance; it is whether applicants know what they are agreeing to and whether those who can't keep up are treated fairly. From a Kantian perspective, hiding the pace would treat workers merely as a means to fast delivery. In stakeholder terms, pickers are functional stakeholders with low individual power but very high interest, so the company should "keep them informed and respect their interests." One could argue that the company is only meeting the legal minimum and that customers want fast delivery. That is true, but the legal minimum is not the ethical maximum. In conclusion, the pace is acceptable only if it is disclosed, safe, and supported by fair treatment — and customers share some responsibility for the speed they demand.
