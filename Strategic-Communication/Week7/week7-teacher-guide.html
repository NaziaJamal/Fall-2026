A. Class overview

Syllabus blockClasses 7–8 · "Audience — who's really listening." Week 7 covers the stated audience vs. the real audience and register. Week 8 covers measuring success (metrics, the Obama 2012 emails, Got Milk?).
Main objectiveStudents identify the different layers of an audience (addressed audience, decision-maker, end user, influencer, unintended audience, hostile audience, target audience) and explain how they shape strategic choices.
Communication objectiveStudents deliver the same core message to different audiences, changing register and emphasis, and answer a tough question from that audience.
Language objectives(1) Hedged inferences about audience and intent ("The evidence suggests…", "That doesn't necessarily prove…"). (2) Register shifts: informal ↔ formal equivalents and how word choice signals the audience. (3) Defending and challenging a strategic choice.
Strategic-thinking objectiveSeparate the people a message speaks to from the people it is designed to move; recognize that attention is not the same as success; separate the intended message from the message the audience actually receives.
Key vocabularyaddressed/stated audience · target audience · decision-maker · end user · influencer · unintended audience · hostile audience · core customers · stakeholder · demographic · register · alienate · resonate with · backlash
FrameworksGAME, with the A expanded into layers. Audience grid (influence × attitude). Callbacks: United "there is no internal message" (Week 2); euphemism and spin (Weeks 5 and 9).
SLOsSLO 2 (real vs. stated audience), SLO 11 (register and tone), SLO 8 (multi-audience response, early practice), SLO 3 (metrics, preview for Week 8).
Assessment linkUngraded audience practice, as the syllabus says. It builds directly toward A4 (questioning) and A5, the War Room, where teams must answer several audiences at once.
MaterialsSlide deck · student worksheet link (GitHub) or printed copies · projector with sound · Evidence cards A–D printed and taped on four walls · 9 scenario cards (one per team) · 2 sets of audience cards · this guide.

Stage 1 · The Brief Engage

9:00–9:20 · pairs · slides 1–6 · Discover → React

Steps

  1. 9:00 (2 min). Slide 2 (Today's flow). Say only: "Today you're audience strategists." No framework yet.
  2. 9:02 (4 min). Slide 3: the brief. Pairs choose one audience and write a reason (worksheet Step 1). 3:00
  3. 9:06 (2 min). Quick vote by hands: who chose young men, older men, women, athletes? Write the numbers on the board. Do not comment yet.
  4. 9:08 (3 min). Slide 4: play the Old Spice ad twice. The first time, just watch. The second time, students listen for the words that show who he is talking to.
  5. 9:11 (5 min). Slide 5: pairs answer the four React questions on the worksheet. 5:00
  6. 9:16 (4 min). Slide 6: the reveal. Ask two pairs who changed their mind to explain why. Then reveal the insight and the results.
Say: "The man in the towel is talking to women. But the product is for men. So who is this ad for? Hold that question. It's the question of the day."
Answer key · React questions
  • Who is he talking to? Women ("Hello, ladies."; "Look at your man"). Specifically, women in relationships.
  • Who uses the product? Men.
  • Why talk to someone else? Research by the agency (Wieden+Kennedy) found that about 60% of body-wash purchases were made by women. Talking to the buyer, not just the user, reached the person who decides at the store. The media was placed where couples would watch together, so it started a conversation between partners.
  • Results to share: launched online on Super Bowl weekend in February 2010. The goal was a 15% sales increase. By May 2010, sales of the advertised body wash were up 60% from the year before, and by July they had doubled (Wieden+Kennedy case study; Muse by Clios).
Checkpoint: most pairs can say why talking to the buyer can be smarter than talking to the user. If not, ask: "Who buys the dish soap in your home? Who uses it?"

9:20–9:50 · pairs · slides 7–10 · Question → Name

Steps

  1. 9:20 (8 min) · Notice. Slide 7. Pairs answer questions 1–7 in the worksheet table about Old Spice, in plain words. They leave the Term column empty for now. 8:00
  2. 9:28 (7 min) · Name. Slide 8. Pairs match each answer to a term from the term box. Then click through the slide to check together. Model pronunciation: al-ienate, demo-graph-ic, stake-holder.
  3. 9:35 (3 min) · Teacher input. Slide 9, the United callback. This is the one key teaching point of the morning.
  4. 9:38 (5 min) · Apply. Pairs complete the United grid on the worksheet.
  5. 9:43 (7 min) · Language. Slide 10: hedged inferences. Pairs take turns saying three sentences about Old Spice, each with a different hedging frame. Then two volunteers say one each to the class.
Say (slide 9): "Remember the United email in Week 2? It was written for employees. Then CNBC published it. The stated audience was employees. The real audience was everyone. A strategist always asks two questions: who am I talking to, and who else will hear this?"

United grid
  • Addressed: United employees.
  • Unintended: the public, the media, and customers worldwide, after it was published.
  • Hostile: customers who were already angry about the video, and the media.

Stage 3 · Apple "Crush!": Evidence Walk + Face-off Study → Activate

9:50–10:40 · teams of 3 · slides 11–17 · Question → Practice

Steps

  1. 9:50 (3 min) · Set up. Slide 11: "Same goal, opposite results." Ask: "Has anyone seen this Apple ad, or heard about it?" Take two or three answers. Don't reveal the reaction yet.
  2. 9:53 (5 min) · Watch. Slide 12. Play the ad once (1:08). Pairs write the intended message and the received message at the top of worksheet Stage 3, then share two answers.
  3. 9:58 (16 min) · Evidence Walk. Slide 13. Teams start at different walls (Teams 1–2 at A, 3–4 at B, 5–6 at C, 7–9 at D) and rotate clockwise every 4 minutes. At each card they take one piece of evidence and add to their audience map. Use the slide timer and say "Rotate" loudly. Move around and ask: "Who is that evidence about?"
  4. 10:14 (5 min) · Debrief the map. Slide 14. Click to reveal one layer at a time after a team suggests it. The key insight: the target audience and the hostile audience were the same people. Finish on "intended vs. received message."
  5. 10:19 (6 min) · Face-off prep. Slide 15. It's May 9, 2024. Teams 1–4 argue "Stand by it" and Teams 5–8 argue "Apologize and pull it." Team 9 (the pair) are the judges: they prepare two challenge questions for each side and decide what makes an argument "strong." 6:00
  6. 10:25 (8 min) · Face-off. Slide 16. Pairings: 1 vs. 5, 2 vs. 6, 3 vs. 7, 4 vs. 8, all running at the same time. Format: "Stand by it" speaks for 60 seconds, "Apologize" asks one challenge, "Stand by it" answers in 30 seconds, then the teams switch. Each member must speak in at least one part. The judges move between tables and listen.
  7. 10:33 (7 min) · Verdict. Slide 17. Judges name the two strongest arguments they heard and say why. Click to reveal what Apple actually did. Ask: "Which team's plan was closest? What does the new ad tell us about who Apple decided mattered most?"
Say (closing, slide 17): "Old Spice understood its buyer. Apple made an ad for creative people that many creative people hated. The message is what the audience hears, not what you meant. And the strategic question is never 'Was it good?' It's 'Good for whom, and at what cost?' Next week we ask how you would even measure that."
Answer key · Apple audience map
  • Intended message: all of these creative tools now fit inside one very thin, very powerful device. Tim Cook's post said: "Just imagine all the things it'll be used to create."
  • Received message (for many creatives): technology is destroying the things people love and the work they do. Some critics linked it to worries about AI replacing human creativity.
  • Addressed audience: everyone watching the launch and possible iPad Pro buyers.
  • Target audience: creative people: musicians, artists, designers, filmmakers. Apple's own apology says it wants to "design products that empower creatives all over the world."
  • Hostile audience: those same creatives, including well-known actors and artists. The target and the hostile audience were the same group, which is why the reaction hurt so much.
  • Unintended audience: competitors (Samsung used the anger in its own "UnCrush" ad a week later) and the wider public following celebrity posts.
"Did it fail?" (listen for this reasoning)

The ad passed a million views, so it clearly got attention. But attention is not success. Apple's own actions are evidence that it judged the ad a failure with its core audience: it apologized, cancelled the TV plan, and about three weeks later released a creator-focused ad. There is no public sales data that links the ad to iPad Pro sales, so a strong answer says: "We'd need to measure how creative professionals felt about Apple and whether they still bought the iPad Pro." This sets up Week 8.

Face-off · points to listen for
Stand by itApologize and pull it
The intended message is clear and the product is strong. Many iPad buyers are not professional creatives. An apology keeps the story alive longer. The attention is free awareness. The ad hadn't received any paid media yet, so it was still only online.Creatives are Apple's core identity audience; losing their trust costs more than one ad. Speed limits the damage. Pulling it is cheap because no paid media had run yet. Apple can replace it with a better ad. Competitors are ready to use the anger.
Admitted risk: the creative community feels ignored, and competitors take advantage.Admitted risk: Apple looks weak, and the ad stays online anyway.

A strong performance: names the audience that matters most, uses evidence with a date or source, admits one risk, answers the challenge directly, and separates attention from success.

Checkpoint: while teams prepare, check each worksheet. The claim should name an audience, not only an action. Ask: "Who exactly are you protecting with this decision?"

Spot the spin: W says "replace… with" (it sounds like an upgrade, not a cut). Z says "service adjustment" and "reallocating" (euphemisms; link to Week 5). X and Y use plain words ("end," "stop running") because riders and drivers would see through spin immediately.

Model rules: "When the audience is employees, we tell them first and answer their biggest fear (their jobs) before anything else." "When the audience is decision-makers, we lead with evidence and cost, in formal language." "Same facts, different order and emphasis."

Checkpoint: every pair can name at least two language features that signal the audience, such as pronouns, a greeting, or vocabulary.

Stage 5 · Audience Map Lab Activate

11:35–12:05 · teams of 3 · slides 22–23 · Apply

Steps

  1. 11:35 (4 min) · Model. Slide 22. Model the grid with Brightline, thinking aloud (key below). Show that late-night riders seem low-influence but can quickly become high-influence when the media tells their story.
  2. 11:39 (1 min). Hand out the scenario cards, one per team (Team 9 gets card 9).
  3. 11:40 (25 min) · Build. Slide 23 has the timeline and a timer. Teams follow the four steps on the worksheet. At minute 20, each team says both opening lines aloud to itself, with a different member saying each line.
Say: "Your core message stays the same for everyone. What changes is what you say first, the words you choose, and the formality. If your message changes for each audience, that's not strategy. That's inconsistency, and someone will notice."
Model · Brightline audience grid
  • High influence, likely to support: the city council budget committee (it saves money with no extra cost).
  • High influence, likely to oppose: local media looking for a human story; the drivers' union, if it worries about hours.
  • Low influence, likely to support: riders on Routes 4 and 12 (more buses).
  • Low influence, likely to oppose: late-night riders, often shift workers without cars. They are the most affected, and one TV story can move them to high influence.
  • Priority: late-night riders first (most affected), with drivers told before the public.
  • Core message: "Late-night riders will still get home: Night Hop replaces Route 9 after 11 p.m., and no driver loses hours."
What strong team work looks like
  • Five or more specific audiences, not "the public."
  • The priority is justified by impact or power.
  • The two opening lines sound clearly different in register but carry the same core message.
  • An unintended audience is named, such as media or social-media screenshots.
Checkpoint: at minute 15, check each team's core message. If it changes between the two audiences, send them back to fix it.

Stage 6 · Change the Audience Activate

12:05–12:35 · two teams together · slides 24–26 · Practice → feedback → perform again

Steps

  1. 12:05 (3 min) · Set up. Slide 24. Join teams: 1+2, 3+4, 5+6, and 7+8+9 (a three-way rotation: 7 performs for 8, 8 for 9, 9 for 7). Each table gets an audience-card deck, with the hostile cards kept separate for Round 3.
  2. 12:08 (24 min) · Three rounds of 8 minutes. Slide 25 has the round timer. In each round, each team performs once, about 4 minutes per team:
    • The audience team draws a card and reads it aloud.
    • The spokesperson prepares for 1 minute, then delivers in 30–45 seconds.
    • The audience asks one tough question, using the hint on the card. The spokesperson answers.
    • The audience gives 1 minute of feedback using the checklist (slide 26).
    Round 1 uses a regular card. Round 2 uses a new card, a new spokesperson, and the feedback from Round 1. Round 3 uses a hostile card and the third spokesperson.
  3. 12:32 (3 min) · Debrief. Ask: "What did you change between Round 1 and Round 3?" Take two answers. Praise specific language you heard.
Say (before Round 3): "Hostile audiences don't want a speech. They want you to acknowledge their concern in your first sentence. Use 'That's a fair question' and 'Let me address that directly.'"
Listen for
  • Register clearly changes with the card, for example "Hi everyone" for teenagers and "Thank you for the opportunity" for investors.
  • The audience's concern comes first: jobs for employees, cost for retirees, safety for parents.
  • The core message is repeated and stays the same.
  • The tough question gets a direct answer that starts with the answer, not with background.
Language to put on the board for Round 3: "I understand why you're concerned." · "That's a fair question. The short answer is…" · "Let me address that directly." · "What we can promise is…" · "I don't have that number with me, but I'll get it to you today."
Checkpoint: on your own clipboard, record one improvement per team from Round 1 to Round 3. This is your participation evidence.

Stage 7 · Exit Ticket

12:35–12:50 · individual · slides 27–28

  1. 12:35 (8 min). Students answer the four questions on the worksheet.
  2. 12:43 (5 min). Three students read answer 2 aloud (a message that reached an unintended audience). These are usually memorable stories.
  3. 12:48 (2 min). Slide 28: preview of Week 8. "Apple's ad got enormous attention. Was that success? Next week: which metric matters?"
Strong exit answers
  • Q1: "The stated audience is who the message speaks to. The real audience is who it's designed to move or who actually receives it. For example, Old Spice spoke to women, but the goal was to get couples to choose the product."
  • Q4 (preview): mentions iPad Pro sales to creative professionals, how creatives feel about Apple before and after, or switching to competitors, and not just views or likes.

I. Differentiation

Students who need more language supportGive them the worksheet's Language help drawer. In Stage 2, let them do the matching first, then the questions. In Stage 6, let them be spokesperson in Round 1 (a regular card) and read the opening line from the worksheet.
Stronger studentsStage 3: ask them to argue the side they disagree with. Stage 5: add a third audience. Stage 6: the audience may ask a follow-up question. In the exit ticket, ask them to name a metric that would separate attention from success.
Quieter studentsIn the Face-off, give each team member a fixed part (claim, evidence, risk). They prepare that part, so they can speak with confidence. Use pair rehearsal before any whole-class sharing.
Students who dominateMake them the team "timekeeper and coach" in Stage 6: they may not be spokesperson until Round 3, and they must give the feedback. In the Face-off, the rule is that each person speaks only once.

Printables · Evidence cards (tape on four walls)

A

The launch · May 7, 2024

  • At its "Let Loose" event, Apple introduced the new iPad Pro, calling it the thinnest product it had ever made.
  • The same day, Apple posted a 68-second ad, "Crush!", on YouTube. CEO Tim Cook shared it on X.
  • In the ad, a giant press slowly crushes a piano, a guitar, a trumpet, paint, cameras, books, an arcade game and sculptures. When it lifts, only the thin iPad Pro is left. The music is Sonny & Cher's "All I Ever Need Is You."
  • Cook's post said: "Just imagine all the things it'll be used to create."
  • The ad was made in-house by Apple. It was also planned for TV.

Ask yourselves: What was Apple trying to say? Who was it trying to impress?

Sources: Laptop Mag, May 2024; Digital Trends, May 2024; 9to5Mac, May 9, 2024 (citing Ad Age); Macworld, May 2024.

B

The reaction · May 7–9, 2024

  • Replies to Tim Cook's post were mostly negative. Many people called the ad "tone-deaf."
  • Actor Hugh Grant reposted it with: "The destruction of the human experience. Courtesy of Silicon Valley."
  • Some of the loudest critics were musicians, artists, writers and filmmakers, the people who use these tools.
  • Many connected the ad to a bigger worry: that technology and AI could replace human creative work.
  • Some people posted the video played backwards, so the objects were "uncrushed."

Ask yourselves: Who exactly was upset? Were they outside Apple's audience, or inside it?

Sources: Laptop Mag, May 2024; Euronews, May 10, 2024; Lowyat.net, May 2024; Gigazine, May 10, 2024.

C

Apple responds · May 9, 2024

  • Two days after the launch, Tor Myhren, Apple's vice president of marketing communications, gave a statement to Ad Age.
  • He said Apple wants to "design products that empower creatives all over the world."
  • He added: "We missed the mark with this video, and we're sorry."
  • Apple cancelled its plan to show the ad on TV. The ad had not received any paid media yet.
  • The video stayed online on Apple's YouTube channel and Tim Cook's X account.

Ask yourselves: Who is this apology for? Apple rarely apologizes. Why so fast this time?

Sources: 9to5Mac, May 9, 2024 (citing Ad Age); Macworld, May 2024; MacRumors, May 10, 2024.

D

The weeks after · May–June 2024

  • The original ad passed a million views on Apple's YouTube channel.
  • On May 15, Samsung posted its own ad, "UnCrush": a woman finds a damaged guitar in the wreckage and plays it with help from a Samsung tablet. Its message: "We would never crush creativity."
  • Around June 1, Apple released a new iPad Pro ad, "Worlds Made on iPad." It shows three animators using the iPad to create imaginary worlds for an actress.
  • Creative Bloq described the new ad as Apple trying to make up with creatives.

Ask yourselves: What did Samsung understand about the audience? What did Apple change in its new ad?

Sources: Digital Trends, May 2024; Android Authority, May 16, 2024; Creative Bloq, June 1, 2024.
