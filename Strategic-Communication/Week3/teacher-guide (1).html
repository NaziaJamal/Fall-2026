Class overview

TopicThe Art of the Apology (Classes 3–4, part 1 of 2) · GAME + 4A · Assignment 2 launched
Main objectiveStudents evaluate public apologies with GAME and the new 4A framework (Acknowledge · Accept · Apologize · Act) and classify them as success, failure or mixed (SLO 7).
Communication objectiveDeliver a 60-second spoken apology under audience reaction, revise it, and deliver it again (SLOs 10, 11).
Language objectivesNon-apology formulas ("sorry if", "sorry you feel", "any inconvenience", "mistakes were made"); the passive voice hiding responsibility; 4A sentence frames; hedged verdicts ("On balance…", "It worked in the short term, but…"); reporting verbs that show stance (admitted, claimed, denied); falling intonation for sincerity.
Strategic-thinking objectiveSeparate the apology from the actions after it; judge success with evidence, not impressions; understand that accepting responsibility is not the same as admitting guilt (SLOs 3, 4).
Key vocabularyapology · non-apology · acknowledge · accountable · liability · deflect · euphemism (review) · sincere / hollow · compensation · tamper-proof · recall · verdict · primary source · cross-examination
Frameworks4A (new, named in Stage 3) · GAME (review from Week 2)
MaterialsStudent worksheet (GitHub link) · Study slides · this guide · printed appendix: Spectrum signs, four station cards, auction lots, six crisis cards, green/red reaction cards (one pair per student), A2 case cards · sticky notes · masking tape for a line on the floor

What's new this week, and how it uses ESA

New activity types: a standing human spectrum (Stage 1), a sealed individual verdict followed by jury deliberation (Stage 2), a sort-before-you-name card task (Stage 3), rotating audit stations with a Verdict Wall (Stage 4), a strategy auction with hidden scores (Stage 5), live "dial testing" with green and red cards (Stage 6), and a research sprint that leads to a tribunal (Stage 8).

Part A is a straight ESA arc: react (Spectrum, Domino's) → name 4A and the language of non-apologies → apply both to four famous cases. Part B is a game (Engage), then production with feedback (Activate), then the research skills needed for A2 (Study), then the assignment launch (Activate).

Discover → React → Question → Name → Practice → Apply: Discover and React (Stage 1) · Question (Stage 2: decide before the reveal, as the syllabus requires) · Name (Stage 3: 4A; Stage 7: source ladder) · Practice (Stages 4, 5) · Apply (Stages 6, 8).

Teacher talk: about 30 of 200 minutes (Stage 3, Stage 7, the reveals, the auctioneer role).

1

Stage 1 · The Sorry Spectrum

Engage · Discover · – · 15 min
GroupingWhole class standing → trios
Rhythm6 lines × 90 s · notes 2 · trios 4
OutputPositions and reasons; "what accepted apologies share"
Timers:

Steps

  1. "Everybody up. I'm going to read six apologies. After each one, walk to the place on the line that shows how you feel: reject it, accept it, or somewhere in between."
  2. Read each line twice, with neutral delivery. After students move, ask one person at each end: "Why are you standing there?" Keep it moving: 90 seconds per line.
  3. After line 6: "Look at lines 3 and 6, where most of you accepted. What do they have that the others don't?" Collect words on the board (specific, takes the blame, says sorry, does something). Don't name 4A.
  4. Students sit, note their positions, and share the apology they remembered from last week in trios (4 min).

Key · what each line does

#The moveUsual position
1Conditional apology: "if" makes the harm hypothetical and the offense the listener's problem.Reject
2Passive voice: no one made the mistakes; "looking into it" is vague.Reject / lean reject
3Complete: what happened, my fault, sorry, specific fix and prevention.Accept
4Sorry about the listener's feelings, not the action. Often heard as an insult.Reject
5Corporate formula: "any" and "may have" minimize the harm.Lean reject / not sure
6Complete, and the action is already done: strongest of all.Accept

Listen for: cultural differences in what counts as a sincere apology (in some cultures the act of apologizing matters more than the details). Welcome them — they are useful for Stage 6, when audiences are mixed.

2

Stage 2 · Jury Room: Domino's

Engage · React → Question · – · 25 min
GroupingAlone → juries of 4 → class
RhythmContext 2 · video 3 · notes 3 · seal 2 · jury 6 · verdicts 3 · two reveals 6
OutputSealed verdict; jury verdict with vote
Timers:

Steps

  1. Read the case box aloud (worksheet). "You won't see the prank video — trust me, you don't need to. You'll see how the company answered."
  2. Play Patrick Doyle's video once (about 2 minutes). Students take notes in the five rows.
  3. "Now decide alone: success, failure or mixed? Then press Seal my verdict. Once it's sealed, it's locked — like a real juror's vote."
  4. Juries of four: "Six minutes. Try to agree. Choose a foreperson who announces your verdict and your vote." Write each jury's verdict on the board.
  5. Reveal 1 (students open the gold box): the next two weeks. Ask: "Does this change anything?" Then Reveal 2: the Pizza Turnaround. Final question: "Was the apology the success — or what came after it?"

Key · what strong juries notice

  • For success: fast response (within about two days); the president himself, on camera; the same channel as the crisis (YouTube, with the same title and tags so searchers found it); concrete actions (store closed and sanitized, employees fired and charged).
  • Against: it partly reads as a statement of defense ("two individuals", "isolated"), so Accept is weak; it talks about "our great system" and franchisees as much as customers; reputation still dropped sharply in the short term.
  • The best answer is usually "mixed in the short term, success in the long term". The apology stopped the story; the Pizza Turnaround — an apology for the pizza itself — rebuilt trust and sales. This sets up the key idea of 4A: Act is where apologies are won.
  • Not at fault? Domino's corporate didn't make the video. Ask: "Should you apologize for something you didn't do?" Keep the answers — the Tylenol station returns to this question.

Sources: TIME, 18 April 2009 · NBC Washington: charges, 16 April 2009 · "Domino's Special Delivery" case study (page.org, hosted by the Institute for Public Relations): timeline, Doyle quotes, YouGov, franchise sales · Nation's Restaurant News, 27 April 2009 · Columbia Business School: Pizza Turnaround and 2010 sales

Stage 3 · Four Piles → 4A · The Non-Apology Autopsy

Study · Notice → Name → Practice · – · 20 min
GroupingPairs → class
RhythmSort 5 · name 4A 3 · autopsy 6 · passive 4 · intonation 2
OutputFour named piles; six tricks + rewrites; three active sentences
Timers:

Steps

  1. Slide 2 (5 min): pairs sort the twelve strips into four piles and name each pile in their own words.
  2. Slide 3 (3 min): take the pairs' names first. Then click the four cards to reveal Acknowledge · Accept · Apologize · Act. "Last week we named GAME. This is our second tool: 4A. GAME tells you how any message works; 4A tells you if an apology is complete." Students open the 4A reveal on the worksheet.
  3. Slide 4 (1 min): map Doyle's video onto 4A in one minute as a class.
  4. Slides 5–6 (6 min): the non-apology autopsy. Pairs name each trick and rewrite. Click each line on slide 5 to reveal its trick.
  5. Slide 7 (4 min): passive voice. "Who made the mistakes? The sentence doesn't say. That's the point."
  6. Slide 8 (optional, 3 min): the Weinstein example — see the content note below. Skip it if you prefer; the stage still works.
  7. Slide 9 (2 min): intonation. Model "I'm SORry ↘" (falling, sincere) vs. "I'm sorry ↗" (rising, sounds like a question or sarcasm). Choral drill of 4A stress.

Key · the twelve strips

Acknowledge: 1, 5, 9 · Accept: 2, 6, 10 · Apologize: 3, 7, 11 · Act: 4, 8, 12. Accept any defended alternatives (for example, 10 also acknowledges). Common student pile names: "what happened / facts", "blame / fault", "sorry", "solution / promise".

Key · the non-apology autopsy

Non-apologyThe trickA real apology (sample)
I'm sorry if anyone was offended.Conditional "if": harm becomes hypothetical; the problem is the listener's sensitivity.I'm sorry. What I said was offensive, and it was wrong.
I'm sorry you feel that way.Apologizes for the listener's feelings, not the speaker's action.I'm sorry I ignored your concerns in the meeting.
I apologize, but the media has exaggerated this."But" cancels the apology; shifts blame to others.I apologize. Whatever the coverage says, we got this wrong.
We regret that the situation occurred."Regret" ≠ sorry; "the situation" is vague; it just "occurred" — no one did it.We are sorry that our system charged you twice.
We apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused."Any" and "may have" minimize the harm; "inconvenience" is often too small a word.We apologize for the missed flights and the nights you spent at the airport.
Mistakes were made.Passive voice hides who made them.I made a mistake. / We made serious mistakes.

Passive → active: "We / I made mistakes." · "We exposed our customers' data." / "Our system exposed…" · "We overbooked the flight, and our staff removed a passenger." When passive is chosen fairly: when the person responsible genuinely isn't known yet, or to protect a junior employee's identity; it becomes dishonest when it is used to hide the organization's own responsibility.

Content note · optional Weinstein example (slide 8). The syllabus lists Harvey Weinstein. His case involves allegations of sexual harassment and assault, so this guide keeps it to a short, teacher-led language example rather than group research. Tell students first: "This next example involves sexual harassment allegations. We'll look only at one sentence of the statement and how it works."
  • Facts: on 5 October 2017, after a New York Times investigation, Weinstein issued a statement. It included: "I came of age in the 60's and 70's, when all the rules about behavior and workplaces were different." It said he "sincerely" apologized for causing "a lot of pain", announced therapy and a leave of absence, and also mentioned unrelated plans (a scholarship fund; turning his attention to the National Rifle Association). It named none of the women. Three days later, on 8 October, his company's board fired him.
  • The one question: "Is the 'came of age' sentence an explanation or an excuse? Which A does it damage?" (It is an excuse that weakens Accept; the unrelated promises are a classic distraction from Act.)
  • Sources: Deadline: full statement, 5 Oct 2017 · NPR: fired by the board, 8 Oct 2017
  • If you skip it: use Tony Hayward's "I'd like my life back" (BP, 2010, from Week 1) as the "excuse" example instead.

Stage 4 · Apology Audit Stations

Activate · Practice · – · 40 min
Grouping4 teams (3–5 students), rotating clockwise
RhythmSet-up 2 · 4 stations × 7 · Verdict Wall reveal 10
OutputFour scorecards; four sticky-note verdicts on the Verdict Wall
Timers:

Steps

  1. Send each team to a different wall. "Seven minutes per station. Read the card, score each A from 0 to 2 with evidence, answer the station question, and stick your verdict on the wall. When the timer rings, move clockwise."
  2. Circulate. Push teams to quote the card as evidence, not to give impressions.
  3. Verdict Wall reveal (10 min): stand at each wall in turn. Read the sticky notes aloud ("Three teams said success, one said mixed"), then reveal the outcome (key below). Students fill in "What really happened".
  4. Close with: "Does a high 4A score guarantee success?" (Zuckerberg is the best evidence that it doesn't, and Tylenol that timing and action matter most.)

Stage 5 · The Apology Auction

Engage · React · – · 15 min
GroupingTeams (same as the stations)
RhythmRead crisis 1 · plan bids 2 · 12 lots × 40 s · reveal and scores 4
OutputMoves won; team score
Timers:

Steps

  1. Read the FreshCart crisis aloud. "You have $1,000. I'm auctioning twelve apology moves. Some help, some hurt. Every move has a secret score. Highest total score wins — not the team that buys the most."
  2. Two minutes for teams to plan their maximum bid for each move.
  3. Be a lively auctioneer: hold up the lot card (appendix), start at $50, go up in $50 steps, "going once, going twice, sold!" Teams record what they won and the price. Keep each lot to about 40 seconds.
  4. Reveal the hidden scores one by one (students type them in; the worksheet adds up their score). Winner announced. Ask: "Which move did you overpay for?"

Hidden scores (keep secret until the reveal)

#MoveScoreWhy
1Name the harm exactly+3Acknowledge, specifically.
2"We're sorry if anyone was inconvenienced."−3Conditional + minimizing. A trap.
3Full refund in 24 hours + bank fees covered+3Act: repairs the real harm.
4CEO video within 6 hours+2Speed and the right messenger.
5A joke−2People lost money. Compare KFC: there, nobody was harmed.
6"Our payment partner caused this error."−3Blame before responsibility. Even if true, customers chose FreshCart.
7"This was our failure…"+3Accept, in the active voice.
8Long technical explanation−1Sounds like an excuse; the audience wants to know what happens to their money.
9"Without admitting any legal liability…"−2Lawyer language kills sincerity (remember United).
10Personal email to every affected customer+2Right channel, right audience.
1120% discount on next order+1Nice, but it isn't repair, and it asks them to spend more.
12"This will never happen again." (no details)−1An empty promise. Specific prevention beats a big promise.

Note: the scores are a teaching judgment, not a research finding. Invite teams to challenge a score — a good argument can win a point back. Maximum possible score: 14.

6

Stage 6 · Crisis Clinic: Take 1, Take 2

Activate · Apply · – · 30 min
Grouping4 teams; one team moderates each delivery
RhythmPrep 8 · Take 1 (4 × 2.5) 10 · fix 4 · Take 2 (4 × 1.5) 6 · debrief 2
OutputTwo deliveries per team; moderator notes
Timers:

Steps

  1. Hand out crisis cards (one per team) and green/red cards (one pair per student).
  2. "Eight minutes. Plan a 60-second apology from your CEO with all four A's. Key words only."
  3. Take 1: the CEO stands at the front. Everyone else is the focus group: "Raise green when a sentence makes you believe them. Raise red the moment a sentence loses you. Keep it up for two seconds, then lower it." The moderating team (the next team in order) notes the red moments. After 60 seconds, the moderator gives 30 seconds of feedback: the biggest red moment and the best green moment.
  4. Four minutes to fix. Take 2 with a different speaker. Ask the class after each: "Hands up if Take 2 was better."
  5. Debrief: "What made the reds turn green?" (Usually: specifics, active voice, removing excuses, a date.)

What to listen for

  • Red moments: "we take this very seriously" with nothing after it; blaming a supplier or a junior employee; "any inconvenience"; legal phrasing; reading from a phone.
  • Green moments: naming the people affected; "I"/"we" + active verb; a specific action with a date; contacting affected people directly; a pause after "I'm sorry".
  • Model (C2 · NovaBank): "For 40 minutes yesterday, our app showed some of you other customers' transactions. That was our failure: an update we released wasn't tested properly. I'm sorry — your financial information should never be seen by anyone else. Every affected customer will hear from us personally by Friday, we've stopped all updates until an independent review is complete, and we'll publish what we find."
  • C3 and C1 are the most serious (people were hurt or humiliated): humor is never right, and speed matters most.

Stage 7 · Investigate Like a Journalist

Study · Name → Practice · – · 10 min
  1. Slide 11 (4 min): the source ladder. Pairs place the eight sources on the worksheet; check with the slide. "Start at the top: find the original apology. Then find two reliable reports. Never quote a quote you can't trace."
  2. Slides 12–13 (5 min): reporting verbs. Show the stance scale, then pairs choose verbs for the four sentences.
  3. 1 min: "In the Tribunal, the other team will ask: 'Where did you get that?' Have the answer."

Key

Source ladder. Top: 1 (the company's own statement — top for what they said, not for whether it was true), 5 (the original video). Middle: 2 (Reuters/AP), 7 (business school case study), 4 (Wikipedia — use it as a map to find sources, don't cite it as the final source). Bottom: 3 (reaction video), 6 (anonymous blog), 8 (quotes website with no source).

Reporting verbs. 1 admitted / acknowledged (agrees the fact is true, after resisting) · 2 claimed / insisted (the writer doubts it) · 3 insisted (or "denied any wrongdoing", which needs a different sentence) · 4 announced (neutral, new information).

Stage 8 · Assignment 2 Launch · Research Sprint · Opening Statements

Activate · Apply · – · 40 min
GroupingA2 teams of 3–4 (new mixes)
RhythmBrief 4 · sprint 30 · opening statements 6
OutputCase file; 45-second first verdict
Timers:

Steps

  1. Brief A2 from the worksheet callout (4 min). "Next week is the Apology Tribunal. Your team is the expert witness. You present your verdict; another team is the opposing counsel and argues the opposite verdict."
  2. Assign one case card per team (A2-1 to A2-5, or an approved own case).
  3. Research sprint (30 min). Visit each team twice. First visit: "Have you found the original apology?" Second visit: "What's your best outcome fact, and where is it from?"
  4. Opening statements: 45 seconds per team, standing. Announce the Class 4 pairings: who cross-examines whom.

✓

Exit Ticket and Class 4 Bridge

Reflect · – · 5 min
  1. Students answer the three exit questions (the third shows their sealed Domino's verdict automatically), then download their answers.
  2. "Next week: the Apology Tribunal. You'll have time at the start to finish your case file. Bring the same device."

Checkpoint · objective achieved if…

  • Most exit answers to question 2 remove "regret", "any" and "may have" and add a specific harm or action.
  • Every team's case file has a 4A score with evidence and at least one primary source by the end of the sprint.
  • Take 2 was better than Take 1 for most teams (show of hands in Stage 6).

Assignment 2 · Apology Tribunal format and rubric (for Class 4)

Format: each team presents for 5 minutes (case → original apology → 4A + GAME audit → outcome evidence → verdict), then faces 3 minutes of cross-examination from an assigned opposing team, which argues the opposite verdict. Everyone speaks. Suggested pairings: Team 1 ⇄ Team 2, Team 3 ⇄ Team 4.

CriterionWhat a top score looks likePoints
Case and sources (team)Accurate facts and dates; the original apology quoted or played; at least two reliable sources.2
4A audit (team)Each A scored with evidence from the apology itself; gaps named precisely.4
GAME (team)Real goal and real audience identified; expression (tone, channel, timing) judged.2
Verdict and evidence (team)Clear verdict supported by outcome facts and figures, not impressions; short-term vs. long-term separated.3
Delivery (individual)Clear, confident, professional register; uses reporting verbs and hedged verdict language accurately.2
Cross-examination (individual)Answers directly, cites evidence, concedes a fair point without abandoning the verdict.2

Differentiation

LearnerWhat to do
Needs more language supportIn the autopsy, they name the trick using the drawer's words; in the Clinic, give them the Acknowledge line (short, factual); in the sprint, they own the "facts and dates" row.
