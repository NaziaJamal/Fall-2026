The task

What you will do on the day
Every student has a different real case. You research it, decide whether the communication was mostly a success or mostly a failure, and prove it. You give a 4-minute talk, then your teacher asks you questions for 2 minutes. You can't prepare the questions word for word, but if you really know your case, you will be ready.

3

What your 4 minutes must include

Five parts, in this order. The times are a guide.
1 · The caseabout 0:40

What happened, in a few sentences: who, when, what they said or showed. Show or quote the original message.

"In December 2015, Chipotle's founder published a full-page apology in 61 newspapers. The key sentence was…"

2 · GAMEabout 1:00
  • Goal: the stated goal and the real goal.
  • Audience: the stated audience and the real audience.
  • Message: what people were meant to take away, and what they actually took away.
  • Expression: tone, channel, timing, the messenger.
3 · The languageabout 1:00

Choose the word or phrase doing the most work and explain its effect: euphemism, word temperature (cold, neutral, warm), passive vs. active responsibility, hedging, a slogan or tagline, or an image that works like a sentence.

If your case is an apology, also give your 4A audit: Acknowledge, Accept, Apologize, Act. Which A was strongest, and which was weakest?

4 · My verdictabout 0:50

State your verdict (mostly a success or mostly a failure) clearly and support it with at least two specific pieces of evidence: numbers, reactions, what happened next. Name your sources aloud. Name the strongest point against you, and answer it.

"I'll admit that the apology was fast. However, speed doesn't help if…"

5 · What I would have doneabout 0:30

One concrete, realistic alternative: a different sentence, channel, messenger, timing or action. Explain why it would work better for the real audience.

If your verdict is "success", say what you would still improve. Even a success could be better.

4

The rules

Notes

One key-word card

One index card (or half a sheet of paper), one side, 25 words maximum, no full sentences. No reading from a phone, laptop or script. Your teacher may check the card.

Visual (optional)

One slide

You may show one slide with the original message: a quote, a screenshot or an image. Send it to your teacher by 8:00 p.m. the evening before. No other slides.

Sources

At least three, named aloud

At least three credible sources. The starter sources in the case file count, but at least one must be a source you found yourself. Name them as you speak: "According to CNBC…"

Time

4:00, then a hard stop

You'll see a 1-minute and a 30-second card. At 4:30 your teacher stops you, and anything you haven't said doesn't count.

Your verdict

Choose a side, and be fair

Choose "mostly a success" or "mostly a failure". Don't hide facts that go against you: conceding one point and then rebutting it makes your argument stronger.

Your words

Your own analysis

The talk must be in your own words. You may get help checking your English, but the questions after your talk will show whether you really know your case.

5

The questions

2 minutes, from your teacher

After your talk, your teacher asks you two or three questions about your case. Some check your evidence; some ask you to apply GAME or 4A in a new way. Some examples of the type of question (these are not the real questions):

  • "What is the strongest argument for the opposite verdict? How do you answer it?"
  • "Change the audience: explain your verdict in 20 seconds to the company's employees."
  • "Fix one sentence from the original message. Say your better version."
  • "What one number, six months later, would prove your verdict right?"

A strong answer is short and direct: answer first, then give one reason or piece of evidence. It's fine to take a second to think: "That's a fair question. Let me think…"

Language help

Opening and signposting

  • "Today I'm going to argue that… was mostly a success / failure."
  • "First, let me show you what happened."
  • "Let's look at this through GAME."
  • "That brings me to my verdict."
  • "Finally, here's what I would have done."

GAME analysis

  • "The stated goal was…, but the real goal seems to have been…"
  • "On paper, the audience was…; in reality, the people who mattered most were…"
  • "The message people actually took away was…"
  • "The choice of channel worked against them because…"

Language analysis

  • "The word doing the most work here is…"
  • "Notice the passive voice: '…', so nobody is responsible."
  • "'…' is a euphemism for…"
  • "The tone is cold / neutral / warm, which…"
  • "Acknowledge was strong, but Act was missing."

Evidence and verdict

  • "According to…, …"
  • "The numbers tell a clear story: …"
  • "The strongest evidence for my verdict is…"
  • "I'll admit that…. However, …"
  • "On balance, I'd call this a success / failure because…"

Recommending

  • "A more effective approach would have been to…"
  • "If I had been advising them, I would have…"
  • "Instead of…, they should have…"

Answering questions

  • "That's a fair question. The short answer is…"
  • "I see why you'd say that, but the evidence shows…"
  • "I'd question the premise of that question."
  • "Let me address that directly."
  • "If I may clarify, my point was…"

Buying a few seconds

  • "Let me think about that for a second."
  • "That's an interesting angle. I'd say…"
  • "Good question, and it's not a simple one."

Answering a hard question

  • "The evidence doesn't support that, because…"
  • "That's true, but it doesn't change my verdict, because…"
  • "I'd put it differently: …"
