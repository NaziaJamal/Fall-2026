How this class uses ESA

Part A is a straight ESA arc: experience (their own stories, then United) → name the framework they have been using (GAME) and the language → use it in decisions and start the assignment.

Part B is a "boomerang" (Engage → Activate → Study → Activate): students build first, discover what they can't yet do under questioning, then get the language for it exactly when they need it, then rehearse and perform. This is the rehearse → feedback → perform cycle the course depends on.

Discover → React → Question → Name → Practice → Apply: Discover (Stage 1, the four questions) · React and Question (Stage 2) · Name (Stage 3: GAME; Stage 8: Acknowledge → Answer → Bridge) · Practice (Stage 4, Stage 9) · Apply (Stages 5, 7, 10).

Teacher talk: about 35 of 200 minutes (Stage 3, Stage 8, briefings, reveals). Everything else is student talk.

1

Stage 1 · Strategic Story Swap: The Four Questions

Engage · Discover · – · 15 min
GroupingPairs → stand up → new pairs
Rhythm2 rounds × (60 s story + 90 s interview) × 2 people, + 3 min class
OutputAnswers to the four questions about two partners' stories
Timers:

Steps

  1. "Last week you promised me a story: a time you communicated strategically. You have 60 seconds to tell it. Then your partner interviews you with four questions." Show the four questions (worksheet, Stage 1). Model it first: tell a 30-second story of your own and let one student ask you the four questions.
  2. Round 1, swap roles, then everyone stands and finds a new partner for Round 2.
  3. 3 minutes, whole class: "Which question was hardest to answer about your own story?" Take 3 answers. Write the four questions on the board in a column, in order, and leave them there for Stage 3.

Listen for

  • The hardest question is usually 1 (what you really wanted) or 2 (who really decided): people remember what they said, not what they wanted or who held the power. That is today's whole lesson. Say so: "Hold on to that. In 15 minutes you'll see a CEO with the same problem."
  • Question 4 answered with content instead of form ("I told him we needed more time" is the one thing to remember; "I asked face to face, calmly, after the meeting" is how).
  • Students who didn't prepare a story can use the most recent important email or message they sent.
  • Don't name GAME. If a student asks whether the questions are a framework, say: "Good instinct. Keep that thought for half an hour."

Stage 2 · One CEO, Three Messages

Engage · React → Question · – · 20 min
GroupingAlone → pairs → class
RhythmRead and rank 4 · analysis table 10 · question 3 · reveal and discuss 3
OutputRanking; 3-column analysis table; one adviser question
Timers:

Steps

  1. "Last week you saw Oscar Munoz's first statement. He actually sent three messages in just over a day. Read all three, alone, and rank them."
  2. Pairs complete the analysis table (plain questions, no framework names yet). Circulate and push on the two audience rows: "addressed to, on paper" vs. "who actually read it."
  3. Pairs write one question for Munoz as his adviser. Take 3–4 aloud.
  4. Reveal (worksheet gold box) what United did on 27 April. Ask: "Was Message 3 enough? And why did Message 2 make things worse when it was only sent to employees?"

Key · the three messages (row labels show the GAME letter each worksheet question becomes in Stage 3)

1 · Public statement, Mon 10 Apr2 · Email to staff, Mon 10 Apr3 · Email to staff, Tue 11 Apr
What he really wanted (G)Calm the public while admitting as little as possibleProtect staff morale; defend the crewTake responsibility; stop the crisis; show action
Addressed to, on paper (A)The public, customersEmployeesEmployees
Who actually read it (A)The whole internet, which had seen the video; also lawyersThe whole world: it was obtained by CNBC and publishedThe public, customers, investors, media — written knowing it would be published
The idea people took away (M)"This was a booking procedure, not a person being hurt.""The passenger was the problem.""We failed, and here is what we'll fix by 30 April."

Ranking: most students put 3 first and 2 last. Push: "3 is the best message — but is it the best strategy, coming after 1 and 2?" The first message sets the frame; corrections cost more than getting it right the first time.

Strong adviser questions: "Who else will read this email?" · "Is your goal to win the legal case or to keep your customers?" · "What would you say if it were your father on that plane?"

Big takeaway to say aloud: "There is no such thing as an internal message. Write every email as if it will be on the news."

Stage 3 · Name GAME · Word Temperature

Study · Name → Practice · – · 20 min
GroupingWhole class → pairs
RhythmName GAME 5 · Level 2 4 · temperature 5 · rewrites 4 · pronunciation 2
OutputGAME guesses and notes; sorted words; rewrites
Timers:

Steps

  1. Slide 2 (1 min): the United timeline, as a bridge from Stage 2.
  2. Slide 3 (5 min) · Name it. Point to the four questions still on the board. "Strategists ask exactly these four questions. Each one has a one-word name, and the first letters make a word. You have 60 seconds with your partner: guess the four words." Take guesses, then click each card on the slide to reveal it: Goal · Audience · Message · Expression. Write G-A-M-E next to the questions on the board. "You've been using a framework since this morning. It's called GAME, and you'll use it every week." Students then open the GAME reveal on the worksheet.
  3. Slides 4–5 (4 min): GAME Level 2 and the audience lesson. Students complete the Level 2 column from their United notes, not from you. "Level 1 is what they say. Level 2 is what's really going on. A strategist always checks both."
  4. Slide 6 (5 min): pairs sort the 16 words into cold / cool / warm. Then click words on the slide into columns as pairs call them out. Accept any placement they can defend.
  5. Slide 7 (4 min): warmer and colder rewrites. Take two of each. Then ask the key question: "When is cooler language the right strategic choice?"
  6. Slide 8 (2 min): choral drill: belLIGerent · emPHATically · re-aCCOMmodate · responsiBILity · mitiGAtion · EUphemism.

Key · Name it (worksheet Part 1)

1 → Goal · 2 → Audience · 3 → Message · 4 → Expression (tone + channel). Good guesses to praise: purpose / aim (Goal), target / listener (Audience), point / idea (Message), style / delivery / tone (Expression).

Key · Word Temperature (accept defended alternatives)

Cold · distant, protectiveCool · neutral, carefulWarm · accountable, human
re-accommodate · disruptive and belligerent · procedures were followed · situationupsetting event · incident · we regret · we will review · customers · the passengertruly horrific event · mistreated · full responsibility · we will do better · we are deeply sorry · Dr. Dao (a name makes a person real)

Sample rewrites. Warmer: "We are deeply sorry that a passenger was hurt on one of our flights yesterday. This should never have happened, and we take full responsibility." Colder: "We are aware of an incident involving a passenger on one of our flights and are reviewing it."

When cooler language is strategic: the facts aren't known yet; there is a police or legal investigation; protecting someone's privacy; the issue is small and warmth would sound exaggerated or fake. Key distinction: cool is honest but careful; cold hides the truth (euphemism). "Incident" is cool; "re-accommodate" is cold.

Stage 4 · Strategic Choice Battles

Activate · Practice · – · 25 min
GroupingA1 teams of 3–4 (form them now)
RhythmSet-up 2 · Round 1 11 · Round 2 11 · scores 1
OutputTwo decisions defended aloud; challenge questions
Timers:

Steps

  1. Announce the A1 teams. Give each team one choice card (printed or by number on the worksheet).
  2. Round 1: 3 minutes prep for all teams at once. Then each team in turn: 45-second defense ("We chose A instead of B because…", at least two GAME letters) → the next team asks one challenge question → a different member answers in 30 seconds.
  3. After all teams: show of hands for strongest defense (not the favorite option). Winner gets 2 points; the best challenge question gets 1 point.
  4. Round 2 twist: "Pass your card to the team on your left. You must now defend the option they rejected." Same format, new speakers.

Key · there is no single right answer. Listen for reasoning.

CardStrong defense of AStrong defense of BChallenge to model
SC1 Price riseFast, simple; 30 days meets the contract. Weak if it relies on "nobody reads email on Friday."Notice and a face show respect; a discount protects the most valuable customers. Usually stronger."Isn't a Friday-evening email just hiding bad news?"
SC2 App crashNo one was harmed, the brand voice is playful, humor spreads (KFC from Week 1). Strong if it includes a fix.Safer; compensation shows respect for people's time."Would the joke still work if people had lost money?"
SC3 NursesConsistency and fairness: everyone gets the same facts at once.People hear change from their manager and can ask questions; avoids rumors. Strong if managers get the same script."What if ten managers explain it ten different ways?"
SC4 Recycling appGoes where 18–30s actually are; peers are trusted messengers. Metric: active users, not views.Credible and cheap; posters reach non-social-media users. Weak for this audience."How will you know the influencer didn't just get likes?"
SC5 Viral reviewFalse claims (especially about safety or hygiene) left public become "true." A calm, factual reply shows other customers you care.A public reply can spread the story further and look defensive; solve it privately."What if your public reply makes the review go even more viral?"
SC6 Bank errorTrust is a bank's product; the CEO shows it's taken seriously.Proportional: no money lost, short error; a CEO video could make it look bigger than it is."Wouldn't a CEO video tell customers this was worse than you say?"

Strong defenses: name the audience explicitly; mention the trade-off; use "instead of." Weak: "because it's better" or personal taste ("I like funny ads").

If you run long: do Round 2 with two teams only, or skip the vote and give your own one-line verdict.

RhythmBrief 3 · read 3 · choose 2 · Goal and Audience 12

Timers:

Steps

Stage 6 · What Does an A Look Like?

Engage · Question · – · 8 min
  1. "Two minutes. As a team: what three things do you think will earn the most points?"
  2. Take one prediction per team, then everyone opens the rubric reveal. "What did you miss?" Most teams miss the success metric and the individual Q&A points.
  3. Explain grading in one sentence: "10 points are for the team's thinking; 5 points are yours alone: how you present and how you answer."

Teams build; you circulate with checkpoints. No scripts.

Stage 7 · Build the Campaign Plan

Activate · Apply · – · 30 min
GroupingA1 teams
RhythmCanvas 12 · risk and metric 8 · choices and roles 10
OutputComplete canvas, three choices, running order
Timers:

Checkpoints · ask every team

  1. Minute 10: "Say your message in one sentence without looking down." If they can't, the message isn't finished.
  2. Minute 20: "Is your metric measuring the real goal, or just attention?" Likes, views and "awareness" are not enough on their own.
  3. Minute 28: "Who is answering questions about the risk?" Make sure the quietest member has a defined part.

Watch for scripting: if a student is writing full sentences, ask them to turn it into five key words. The syllabus assesses thinking out loud.

Stage 8 · Answering Under Pressure

Study · Name → Practice · – · 10 min
Timers:
  1. Slide 10 (2 min): show the three bad answers. "Which one is worst, and why?" (Usually "No comment": it sounds like hiding something.)
  2. Slide 11 (3 min): name Acknowledge → Answer → Bridge. Model it with the slide question.
  3. Slide 12 (5 min): pairs plan an answer to the practice question in 2 minutes; two students answer live in 60 seconds. Give one piece of feedback each: did they actually answer?

Model answer · "Isn't this whole campaign just spin to make your client look good?"

Acknowledge: "That's a fair question, and people are right to be skeptical of campaigns."

Answer: "Our job is to make our client look good, yes — but only by doing something real. Every claim in this campaign is something they're actually doing."

Bridge: "And that's exactly why our success metric is first-time donors who come back, not likes."

Listen for: an actual answer in sentence two (most students skip it and go straight to the bridge, which sounds evasive); calm tone; short sentences.

Link forward: if anyone says "Sorry if anyone was offended," flag it: "Hold that sentence. It's the first thing we take apart in Class 3."

ACTIVATEApply · rehearse → feedback → perform–

Rehearse against a Red Team, fix, then perform for the grade.

Stage 9 · Red Team Rehearsal

Activate · Practice · – · 12 min
Timers:
  1. Pair the teams (Team 1 + Team 2, Team 3 + Team 4). Give each pair the Red Team bank card.
  2. 2-minute pitch → 2 minutes of Red Team questions → swap → 4 minutes to fix the weakest point.
  3. "Red Teams: your job is to find the weakest point, not to be polite. You're helping them. A question that hurts now saves them points later."

With an odd number of teams: you are the Red Team for the extra team.

Stage 10 · Live Presentations · Assignment 1

Activate · Apply · – · 35 min
Format5 min pitch + 3 min Q&A per team
Fits4 teams in 32 min + votes. 5 teams: 4 + 2 min.
OutputGraded presentation; peer cards; two votes
Timers:
  1. Assign peer cards: each student gives feedback to the team after theirs (the last team reviews the first).
  2. Show the "1 MINUTE" card at 4:00 and "STOP" at 5:00. Stop teams on time: a fair system for everyone.
  3. Q&A: you ask the first question (the hardest), the class asks the rest. Make sure at least three different presenters answer.
  4. After all teams: votes for Strongest defense and Best strategy. Announce winners with a one-sentence reason each.

Your hardest first question, by brief

  • Ad-1: "Won't vegans see a butter bakery's plant-based line as insincere?"
  • Ad-2: "Isn't marketing to their children treating older people as if they can't decide for themselves?"
  • Apology-1: "Why did it take a journalist to find this?"
  • Apology-2: "Will you honor the acceptances? Yes or no?"
  • Advocacy-1: "Why spend money on bike lanes when the roads are full of potholes?"
  • Advocacy-2: "Isn't it irresponsible to make blood donation look like a game?"

Exit Ticket and Class 3 Bridge

Reflect · – · 5 min
  1. Students answer the three exit questions, then click Download my answers.
  2. "Next week: The Art of the Apology. Come with one public apology you remember. Did you believe it?"

Checkpoint · objective achieved if…

  • Every team scored at least 2/4 on GAME analysis with a stated and real audience.
  • Most students' exit answer 1 names a technique (acknowledged, answered directly, bridged), not just "I was confident."
  • Exit answer 3 averages 3 or more. If it is lower, add a 10-minute Press Conference warm-up to Class 3.

Language support (also in the students' Language help drawer)

For Stages 2–3 · Analyzing

The stated goal is…, but the real goal seems to be… · On paper, this is addressed to…; in reality, it reached… · The word "…" signals…

For Stage 4 · Choosing and justifying

We chose X instead of Y because… · The trade-off is…, but… · On balance, we'd go with…

For Stages 4, 9, 10 · Challenging

I'm not entirely convinced that… · What evidence supports that assumption? · But wouldn't that risk…?

For Stage 10 · Presenting

Our recommendation in one sentence is… · Moving on to our audience… · I'll now hand over to…

For Stages 8–10 · Under pressure

That's a fair question. The short answer is… · I understand the concern. What we're doing about it is… · I don't have that figure with me, but what I can tell you is…

Differentiation

LearnerWhat to do
Needs more language supportIn Stage 2 they complete only the Goal and Real audience rows. In battles they answer the challenge question (prepared with the team) rather than give the opening defense. In A1, give them the opening or the close: short, predictable parts with key-word notes.
Stronger / needs challengeIn Stage 2: "Write the message Munoz should have sent on Monday morning, in three sentences." In A1: they take the risk section and the hardest Q&A topic. In battles: Round 2 opening speaker.
Quieter studentsTell them their Q&A topic in advance (for example, "you answer anything about the metric"), so they can prepare. Speaking first in pairs in Stage 1 lowers the stakes.
Students who dominateMake them the team's Red Team captain in Stage 9: they ask, not answer. In the presentation, the rule "different people answer" applies, and you can direct questions by name.
