Class overview

TopicThe Art of the Apology (Classes 3–4, part 2 of 2) · Assignment 2 launched, researched and presented in one class
Main objectiveTeams research a real public apology, judge it with GAME and 4A, and deliver and defend an evidence-based verdict (SLOs 7, 10).
Communication objectivePresent for 5 minutes, then defend the verdict under 3 minutes of cross-examination; ask effective cross-examination questions as opposing counsel.
Language objectivesOpen, closed and leading questions; negative questions and tag questions with falling vs. rising intonation; question chains; conceding, rebutting and refusing a premise; courtroom phrases; reporting verbs (review).
Strategic-thinking objectiveFind and weigh evidence quickly; separate short-term from long-term outcomes; find patterns across cases (SLO 3).
Key vocabularytribunal · verdict · evidence · witness · cross-examination · leading question · premise · concede · rebut · primary source
MaterialsStudent worksheet (GitHub link) · study-note.html (cross-examination language) · Week 4 slides · this guide · tribunal-printables.html: A2 case cards, role placards, jury ballots, time cards, debate cards · students' own devices for research

What changed from the first Week 4 plan

  • Assignment 2 is launched today (it didn't fit into Week 3): case cards, a 30-minute research sprint before the break, and 30 minutes of preparation after it.
  • The old "countdown / sparring" stage is gone. Stages 1–3 are longer (20 minutes each), with a sixth debate statement, a question-chain task, and a second fishbowl round with students as witnesses.
  • "The court is in session" is now a full briefing with roles, rules, procedure, grading and phrases on the students' worksheet (Stage 6).
  • The Verdict Matrix is shortened to 10 minutes; the word-hunt preview of Classes 5–6 is now one line in the exit ticket.

How this class uses ESA

Part A: argue (Engage) → name the question and answer moves (Study) → try them in the fishbowl (Activate) → apply research skills to a real case (Activate). Part B: prepare (Activate) → a short Engage that turns the room into a courtroom → the performance (Activate) → a Study stage that turns the hearings into general rules.

Discover → React → Question → Name → Practice → Apply: React (1) · Name (2) · Practice (3) · Apply (4, 5, 7) · Name again, at a higher level (8).

Stage 1 · Speed Debate Lines

Engage · React · – · 20 min
GroupingTwo facing lines (odd number: you join a line)
RhythmSet-up 3 · 6 rounds × 2 min · debrief 5
OutputSix arguments, each answer opening with a concession
Timers:
  1. "Two lines, facing each other. Line A agrees with the statement, Line B disagrees — whatever you really think. A speaks for 45 seconds; B answers for 45 seconds, starting with 'That's a fair point. However…'" Model one concession before round 1.
  2. Show each statement on slide 4 (Next statement). After each round, Line B moves one place left. The side labels on the slide swap automatically after rounds 2 and 4.
  3. Debrief (5 min): "Which statement was hardest to argue? Did arguing the other side change your mind? Who used a case from last week as evidence?"

Listen for

  • Answers that skip the concession. Model it: "That's a fair point — speed matters. However, a fast apology with wrong facts is worse than a slower, accurate one."
  • Good use of Week 3 evidence (KFC for humor, Tylenol for "not your fault", Domino's for speed). Praise it publicly: that's what the Tribunal needs.
2

Stage 2 · The Language of Cross-Examination

Study · Name → Practice · – · 20 min
GroupingPairs → class
RhythmSort 4 · tags 3 · leading 4 · concede/rebut 4 · question chain 5
OutputSorted questions; leading questions; a two-sentence answer; a 3-question chain
Timers:
  1. Slide 5 (4 min): pairs sort eight questions; click each to reveal the type. "Which type is best for cross-examination? Why?"
  2. Slide 6 (3 min): tag questions, falling vs. rising. Choral drill of the falling version.
  3. Slide 7 (4 min): turn closed questions into leading ones.
  4. Slides 8–9 (4 min): concede, rebut, refuse the premise; pairs answer the practice question; two pairs perform.
  5. Slide 10 (5 min): question chains. Model the example, then pairs write a chain against "Domino's was a success". They use it in the fishbowl.
  6. Point students to the study note for the full explanation and the self-check.

Key

Question sort: 1 open · 2 closed · 3 leading · 4 open · 5 leading · 6 closed · 7 leading · 8 open.

Tags: falling ↘ = "I'm sure; agree with me" · rising ↗ = a genuine question.

Leading versions: "The CEO didn't apologize on the first day, did he?" · "Isn't it true that sales fell after the apology?" · "Wouldn't you agree that there was no compensation at all?"

Concede + rebut: "That's true: the apology came after the video spread. However, it came within about two days, on the same channel, and the actions that followed rebuilt trust."

Sample chain (Domino's): "The video was online for about two days before the president responded, correct?" → "And in those days, surveys showed Domino's reputation turned negative, didn't they?" → "So the apology didn't stop the damage, did it?"

Stage 3 · Fishbowl: Two Rounds

Activate · Practice · – · 20 min
Set-up1 witness chair (2 in round 2) facing 4 chairs in a half-circle + 1 empty chair; everyone else in an outer ring
RhythmSet-up 2 · round 1: verdict 1 + questions 3 · feedback 2 · round 2: prep 2 + verdict 1 + questions 3 · debrief 5
OutputLive questions; observer checklists
Timers:
  1. Set up (2 min). If desks can't move: your chair at the front, four chairs facing you in the first row, everyone else at their desks.
  2. Round 1 · you are the witness. Deliver the 60-second verdict below. Four volunteers cross-examine for 3 minutes using their question chains. Outer students can tap a shoulder to swap in, or sit in the empty chair for one question. Concede once, refuse one premise, and give one deliberately bad, defensive answer.
  3. Feedback (2 min): "What did I do well? Where did I fail?"
  4. Round 2 · students are the witnesses. Two volunteers take the witness chairs and defend "The Domino's apology was a failure" (2 minutes to prepare with the outer circle's help, a 60-second verdict). Four new volunteers cross-examine for 3 minutes.
  5. Debrief (5 min): observers share the best question and the best answer from each round.
Your 60-second verdict: "Our case is Domino's, April 2009. Two employees at a franchise in North Carolina posted a video of themselves mishandling food. Within about two days, Patrick Doyle, president of Domino's USA, posted his own apology on YouTube, with the same title and tags so people searching for the prank would find it. On 4A: he acknowledged what happened and said the store had been closed and sanitized; he apologized; and he acted: the employees were fired and charged, and the company reviewed its hiring. Then, in December 2009, Domino's went further and admitted in its 'Pizza Turnaround' ads that customers thought its pizza was bad, and changed the recipe. In the first three months of 2010, same-store sales rose 14.3%. Our verdict: success."

Expect these questions · model answers

  • "Isn't it true that Domino's reputation turned negative within days?" → "Yes, in the short term. That's why we call the apology a first step. Our verdict rests on the long-term evidence."
  • "Didn't he blame 'two individuals' instead of accepting responsibility?" → "That's a fair point; Accept is his weakest A. However, the company really didn't make the video, and it still acted."
  • "Wasn't the recipe change already planned before the prank?" → Concede honestly: "Yes; the marketing chief had started that work in 2008. But it shows the same strategy: admit the problem and fix it."
  • "Can you prove the apology caused the sales increase?" → "No, and we don't claim that." Afterwards: admitting a limit made the witness more credible.
  • Round 2 witnesses (failure) should use: reputation turned negative within days; about 50% sales drops reported at some franchises near Charlotte; "isolated" and "two individuals" as weak Accept.

Sources: TIME, 18 April 2009 · Domino's case study (page.org, via Institute for PR) · Columbia Business School

Activate · Apply · – · 40 min
GroupingA2 teams of 3–4 (new mixes)
RhythmBrief 4 · cases and pairings 2 · research rules 2 · sprint 32
OutputCase file with sources (to be finished after the break)
Timers:
  1. Slide 13 · Brief (4 min). "Last week you audited four famous apologies at the stations, and you heard the names of some of today's cases. Now each team gets one case to investigate in detail. After the break, you present your verdict to the Tribunal, and another team cross-examines you." Show the format and the rubric.
  2. Slide 14 · Cases and pairings (2 min). Give each team a printed case card and read out the pairings. Students record their case, their opposing team and the team they will question.
  3. Slide 15 · Research rules (2 min). Original apology first; two reliable reports; a source for every number. If Week 3's "Investigate like a journalist" stage was skipped too, spend 2 extra minutes on the source ladder here.
  4. Slide 16 · Research sprint (32 min). "Split the work: one person finds the original apology, one finds what happened, one finds the outcome." Visit every team twice: at about 10 minutes, "Have you found the original apology?"; at about 25 minutes, "What's your best outcome fact, and where is it from?"
  5. Remind them: "You have 12 more minutes after the break, so get facts and sources now."

Stage 5 · Prepare for the Tribunal

Activate · Apply · – · 30 min
GroupingA2 teams (sitting in their courtroom seats)
RhythmFinish 12 · plan 8 · opposing counsel 10
OutputFinished case file; running order; 5 questions; 3 predicted answers
Timers:
  1. Finish (12 min). Teams complete the case file and tick the evidence locker. "If you can't find a source for a number, don't use the number."
  2. Plan (8 min). Teams fill in the running order (every member speaks) and say their opening sentence and verdict out loud once. Key words only, no scripts.
  3. Opposing counsel (10 min). Teams read the case card of the team they will question and look up one or two facts. They write five questions (mostly leading, one chain) and predict the three hardest questions they will face.

Quick fixes while you circulate

  • No original source: send them to the company's newsroom or the earliest report that quotes the statement.
  • Verdict based on feelings ("it felt fake"): ask for one number or one consequence that proves it.
  • One student doing everything: reassign the running order now.
  • Weak questions: turn open questions into leading ones; use the case card's numbers.

PurposeEveryone knows exactly what to do before the first hearing starts

1 · Set up the room (during the break)

Screen: docket and phase timer (slide 21)
Judge
(you, with this guide's scoring sheet)
Expert witnesses
front table, facing the jury
Opposing counsel
side table, angled toward the witnesses
Jury rows · everyone else, facing the front
  • Put the Expert Witnesses placard on the front table and the Opposing Counsel placard on the side table; the Judge placard at your seat.
  • Leave 3–4 chairs at each table; teams move there only for their own hearing and then return to the jury rows.
  • Place a pile of jury ballots on each row (or students use the worksheet).
  • Have the time cards (5:00, 1 MINUTE, STOP, 3:00) at your seat.

2 · The 5-minute briefing (slides 19–20)

  1. Call order (30 s). "The court is now in session. Everyone open Stage 6 on your worksheet." A firm, formal tone helps; students enjoy the role-play.
  2. Roles (1.5 min, slide 19). "The expert witnesses present their verdict for five minutes. Opposing counsel argue the opposite verdict, but only through questions — no speeches. Everyone else is the jury: you listen in silence and fill in a ballot after each hearing. I am the judge: I keep time, keep order, and grade."
  3. Procedure (1 min, slide 20). Walk through one hearing: "Thirty seconds to sit down. Five minutes of presentation; I show '1 MINUTE' at four minutes and 'STOP' at five. Three minutes of cross-examination. Then one minute of silence while the jury fills in ballots."
  4. Rules (1 min). Read the five rules aloud. Stress two: "Every presenter answers at least one question — that's part of your individual grade. And when you see STOP, finish your sentence and stop."
  5. My roles (30 s). Read out the docket: who presents in which hearing and who questions them. Students write their hearing numbers in "My roles today".
  6. Award (30 s). "After the last hearing, the team the jury found most persuasive wins Most Persuasive Case. It doesn't change your grade — but it's yours for the week."

Check before you start hearing 1

  • Every student can tell you their hearing number for presenting and for questioning.
  • The first two teams are at the front and side tables with their notes.
  • The docket on slide 21 shows hearing 1 highlighted; your scoring sheet is open.

Stage 7 · The Apology Tribunal (Assignment 2)

Activate · Apply · – · 50 min
Format30 s seats · 5 min presentation · 3 min cross-examination · 1 min ballots
Fits5 teams in 50 min · 4 teams leaves 10 min to spare · 6 teams: 4 + 2 + 1
OutputGraded presentations; jury ballots
  1. On slide 21, click the current hearing, then Presentation 5:00 → Cross-exam 3:00 → Ballots 1:00, then Next hearing. Show the 1 MINUTE and STOP cards as well.
  2. Score the team criteria during the presentation and the individual criteria during cross-examination (sheet below).
  3. If opposing counsel runs out of questions, ask one yourself, aimed at a student who hasn't answered yet. If someone starts a speech: "Your question, please?"
  4. Most Persuasive Case (2 min, after the last hearing): for each team, "Hands up if you gave this team a 4 or 5." Count and announce the winner with one sentence of reason.

Judge's notes · what separates the scores

  • Top teams quote the original apology, score each A with evidence, separate short- and long-term outcomes, and concede a fair point without dropping their verdict.
  • Middle teams describe the case well but judge from impressions or use one source.
  • Be realistic about research time: teams had about 45 minutes. Reward accurate, well-sourced basics over ambitious claims.

Stage 8 · Verdict Matrix · Our Rules of Apology

Study · Name · – · 10 min
  1. Slide 22 (5 min): one spokesperson per team gives verdict, strongest A, weakest A and key outcome in one breath each; type them into the slide. Ask: "What pattern do you see?"
  2. Slide 23 (5 min): three teams each propose one rule starting with a verb; type them in. "We'll add to these rules after the next blocks and use them in the War Room in Class 11."

Patterns students usually find · sample rules

  • Most often missed: Accept (blaming "a couple of software engineers", a supplier, or "isolated" individuals) and a specific Act.
  • Long-term successes made a costly, visible change: a Customer Bill of Rights, store closures for training, a recall.
  • Compensation must match the harm: CrowdStrike's $10 gift card is the class's favorite counterexample.
  • Sample rules: "Name the harm in the first sentence." · "Take responsibility before you explain." · "Act visibly, with a date."

Differentiation

LearnerWhat to do
Needs more language supportIn research, they own "what happened" (dates, facts). In the presentation, they give "the apology itself" (quotable). Agree in advance which predicted question they will answer; as opposing counsel, they read a prepared leading question.
Stronger / needs challengeA witness chair in fishbowl round 2. In research, they own the outcome evidence. In cross-examination, ask them the judge's follow-up question.
Quieter studentsThe empty fifth chair in the fishbowl lets them ask one question without being "on stage". In the Tribunal, agree with them which topic they will answer.
Students who dominateRule: once you've answered, the next answer comes from a teammate. Make them the team's timekeeper in Stage 5.
