What's new this week, and how it uses ESA

New activity types: speed debate lines (Stage 1), a fishbowl in which students cross-examine you (Stage 3), opposing-counsel research and sparring rounds (Stage 4), a live tribunal with jury ballots (Stage 6), a co-built verdict matrix and class rules (Stage 7), and a word hunt that previews Classes 5–6 (Stage 8).

Part A follows ESA as preparation for performance: argue (Engage) → name the question and answer moves (Study) → try them on you, then on each other (Activate). Part B is the performance itself, then a Study stage that turns twelve minutes of evidence per team into general rules, then a short Engage that opens the next block.

Discover → React → Question → Name → Practice → Apply: React (Stage 1) · Name (Stage 2) · Practice (Stages 3–4) · Apply (Stage 6) · Name again, at a higher level (Stage 7) · Discover the next topic (Stage 8).

Teacher talk: about 30 of 200 minutes, much of it as the witness in Stage 3 and the judge in Stage 6.

1

Stage 1 · Speed Debate Lines

Engage · React · – · 15 min
GroupingTwo facing lines (odd number: you join a line)
RhythmSet-up 2 · 5 rounds × 2 min · debrief 3
OutputFive arguments, each answer opening with a concession
Timers:

Steps

  1. "Two lines, facing each other. Line A agrees with the statement, Line B disagrees — whatever you really think. A speaks for 45 seconds; B answers for 45 seconds, starting with 'That's a fair point. However…'"
  2. Show each statement on slide 3 (Next statement button). After each round, Line B moves one place left. After rounds 2 and 4, the lines swap sides.
  3. Debrief (3 min): "Which statement was hardest to argue? Did arguing the other side change your mind?" Point out: "In two hours you'll do this for real, with evidence."

Listen for

  • Students who skip the concession and go straight to "No, because…". Model it once: "That's a fair point — speed matters. However, a fast apology with wrong facts is worse than a slower, accurate one."
  • Good use of Week 3 evidence (KFC for humor, Tylenol for "not your fault", Domino's for speed). Praise it publicly.

Stage 2 · The Language of Cross-Examination

Study · Name → Practice · – · 15 min
GroupingPairs → class
RhythmSort 4 · tags 3 · leading 4 · concede/rebut 4
OutputSorted questions; three leading questions; a two-sentence answer
Timers:
  1. Slide 5 (4 min): pairs sort eight questions into open / closed / leading. Click each question to reveal its type. "Which type is best for cross-examination? Why?" (Leading: you control the answer. Open questions let the witness give a speech.)
  2. Slide 6 (3 min): tag questions. Say the sentence twice, falling then rising. Students write what each means. Choral drill with the falling version.
  3. Slide 7 (4 min): turn closed questions into leading ones; take two versions of each.
  4. Slides 8–9 (4 min): conceding and rebutting, plus "don't accept the premise". Pairs answer the practice question; two pairs perform.

Key

Question sort: 1 open · 2 closed · 3 leading · 4 open · 5 leading · 6 closed · 7 leading · 8 open.

Tags: falling ↘ = "I'm sure this is true; agree with me" (the cross-examiner's version). Rising ↗ = a genuine question; the speaker doesn't know.

Leading versions (samples): "The CEO didn't apologize on the first day, did he?" · "Isn't it true that sales fell after the apology?" · "Wouldn't you agree that there was no compensation at all?"

Concede + rebut (sample): "That's true: the apology came after the video spread. However, it came within about two days, on the same channel, and the actions that followed are what rebuilt trust."

First on you, then on each other, then on their real opponent's case.

3

Stage 3 · Fishbowl: Cross-Examine the Teacher

Activate · Practice · – · 15 min
Grouping4 chairs in an inner circle; everyone else around them
RhythmPrep 3 · your verdict 1 · cross-examination 3 (+3 second round) · debrief 5
OutputLive questions; observer checklists
Timers:
  1. Pairs get 3 minutes to write two leading questions against the verdict "Domino's was a success" (they know the case from Week 3).
  2. Four volunteers take the inner chairs. You stand as the expert witness and deliver the 60-second verdict below.
  3. 3 minutes of cross-examination. Anyone outside can tap a volunteer's shoulder and swap in. Run a second 3-minute round if energy is high.
  4. Model on purpose: concede at least once, refuse one premise ("I wouldn't put it that way…"), and bridge back to the verdict every time. Answer one question badly (a long, defensive speech), then ask the class what went wrong.
  5. Debrief: observers share the best question, a failed question, and the moment you conceded.
Your 60-second verdict: "Our case is Domino's, April 2009. Two employees at a franchise in North Carolina posted a video of themselves mishandling food. Within about two days, Patrick Doyle, president of Domino's USA, posted his own apology on YouTube, using the same title and tags so people searching for the prank would find it. On 4A: he acknowledged what happened and said the store had been closed and sanitized; he apologized; and he acted: the employees were fired and charged, and the company reviewed its hiring. Then, in December 2009, Domino's went further and admitted in its 'Pizza Turnaround' ads that customers thought its pizza was bad, and changed the recipe. In the first three months of 2010, same-store sales rose 14.3%. Our verdict: success."

Expect these questions · model answers

  • "Isn't it true that Domino's reputation turned negative within days?" → "Yes, in the short term it did. That's why we call the apology a first step, not the whole story. The long-term evidence is what our verdict rests on."
  • "Didn't he blame 'two individuals' instead of accepting responsibility?" → "That's a fair point. Accept is the weakest of his four A's. However, the company really didn't make the video, and it still took action."
  • "Wasn't the recipe change already being planned before the prank?" → Concede honestly: "Yes, the new marketing chief had started work on it in 2008, so the turnaround wasn't caused by the apology. But it shows the same strategy: admit the problem and fix it." (Columbia Business School says the CMO arrived in September 2008 and the recipe was tested over two years.)
  • "So can you prove the apology caused the sales increase?" → "No, and we don't claim that. We claim the apology stopped the damage and the actions that followed rebuilt trust." Point out afterwards: admitting a limit made the witness more credible.

Sources: TIME, 18 April 2009 · Domino's case study (page.org, via Institute for PR) · Columbia Business School

Stage 4 · Countdown · Opposing Counsel · Sparring · Patch

Activate · Apply · – · 55 min
GroupingA2 teams; sparring pairs never meet their real opponent
RhythmCountdown 20 · opposing counsel 15 · sparring 15 · patch 5
OutputFinished case file + running order; 5 questions; 3 tested answers
Timers:
  1. Countdown (20 min). Students import their Week 3 case file (button in the worksheet) or type it in. They work through the Evidence Locker checklist and the running order. Visit every team: "Show me your original source."
  2. Opposing counsel (15 min). "Now research the case you will attack. Find the evidence for the opposite verdict and write five questions, mostly leading." Remind them: their individual cross-examination grade depends on answering, but the quality of their questions affects the opponent's hearing and everyone's learning.
  3. Sparring (15 min). Pair teams that are not opponents (for example 1 + 3, 2 + 4). Team X: 2-minute fast pitch → Team Y: 2 minutes of questions → swap → 3 minutes of feedback to each other.
  4. Patch (5 min). Each team fixes the answer that went worst.

What to listen for · quick fixes

  • No original source: send them to the company's newsroom or the earliest news report that quotes the statement in full.
  • Verdict based on feelings ("it felt fake"): ask for one number or one consequence that proves it.
  • One student doing everything: reassign the running order now. Everyone must speak in the presentation and answer at least one question.
  • Weak arsenal questions: turn open questions into leading ones; the best questions use the opponent's own numbers.

Stage 5 · The Court Is in Session

Engage · – · 5 min
  1. Slide 14. Explain the three roles. Hand out jury ballots (one sheet per student; they can also use the worksheet).
  2. "Rules of the court: opposing counsel asks questions only, no speeches. Every member of the presenting team answers at least one question. I'll stop anyone who goes over time — it's fair for everyone."
  3. Announce the "Most Persuasive Case" award: the team with the highest average jury persuasion score.
6

Stage 6 · The Apology Tribunal (Assignment 2)

Activate · Apply · – · 55 min
Format5 min presentation · 3 min cross-examination · 1 min ballots
Fits5 teams in 45 min + transitions; 4 teams leaves 15 min to spare
OutputGraded presentations; jury ballots
  1. Use slide 15: the docket shows who is on, and the phase timer runs Presentation 5:00 → Cross-examination 3:00 → Ballots 1:00 with one click per phase. Show the 1 MINUTE and STOP time cards as well.
  2. Score on the sheet below as you watch (team criteria during the presentation, individual criteria during cross-examination).
  3. If opposing counsel runs out of questions, ask one yourself, aimed at the student who hasn't spoken yet.
  4. After the last hearing, collect ballots (or ask for a show of hands per team) and announce "Most Persuasive Case". Keep it light; the grade comes from the rubric, not the vote.

Judge's notes · what separates the scores

  • Top teams quote the original apology, score each A with evidence, separate short- and long-term outcomes, and concede a fair point without dropping their verdict.
  • Middle teams describe the case well but judge from impressions ("it felt sincere") or use one source.
  • Watch for unverifiable numbers or quotes. Ask: "Where is that figure from?" It's a fair cross-examination question from the judge too.

Stage 7 · The Verdict Matrix · Our Rules of Apology

Study · Name · – · 20 min
Timers:
  1. Slide 16 (7 min): fill in the Verdict Matrix with the class, one row per case, typing straight into the slide. Add the Week 3 station cases too if time allows (Tylenol, Armstrong, Zuckerberg, KFC).
  2. Patterns (5 min): teams answer the three pattern questions on the worksheet; take one answer per question.
  3. Slide 17 (8 min): each team proposes one rule beginning with a verb. Type the best five into the slide. "These are our rules. You'll use them again in the War Room in Class 11."

Patterns students usually find · sample rules

  • Most often missed: Accept (deflecting to "a couple of software engineers", a supplier, "isolated" individuals) and specific Act.
  • Speed helps but isn't enough: Domino's was fast but still lost reputation in the short term; Zuckerberg's best words came weeks late.
  • Long-term successes (Tylenol, Domino's, often JetBlue and Starbucks) all made a costly, visible change: a recall, a new recipe, a Customer Bill of Rights, store closures for training.
  • Compensation must match the harm: CrowdStrike's $10 gift card is the class's favorite counterexample.
  • Sample rules: "Name the harm in the first sentence." · "Never use 'if' or 'but' in an apology." · "Take responsibility before you explain." · "Act visibly, with a date." · "Make the compensation match the harm."

Stage 8 · Which Word Does the Persuading?

Engage · Discover · – · 15 min
Timers:
  1. Slide 18 (6 min): pairs find the persuading word in each invented line, decide fear or desire, and write a neutral version. Click each line on the slide to reveal a suggested word.
  2. Slide 19 (5 min): show the syllabus's three-step pattern: find a fear or desire → give it a word → repeat. "Last week, words hid the truth. Next week, words do the persuading — before any argument is made."
  3. Exit (slide 20).

Key (accept defended alternatives)

LineWordFeelingNeutral version
Hackers are flooding the internet with fake accounts.floodingFear…creating many fake accounts
Our pure, natural spring water.pure / naturalDesireOur filtered spring water
The tax relief hard-working families deserve.reliefBoth: tax becomes a pain to be relievedThe tax cut…
Join the millions of smart investors who have already switched.smart / millionsDesire (belonging)Many investors have switched
This reckless scheme will destroy local jobs.reckless / scheme / destroyFearThis plan may reduce local jobs
Give your children the safe, healthy start they deserve.safe / deserveDesire (and a hidden fear)A good start for your children

Euphemism vs. persuasive word: a euphemism makes something sound smaller or softer to hide it; a persuasive word adds emotion to push the audience toward a feeling or action. All six lines are invented for teaching.

Language support (also in the students' Language help drawer)

Asking (Stages 2–4, 6)

Isn't it true that…? · Wouldn't you agree that…? · The apology came late, didn't it? ↘ · Can you show us the evidence for that?

Conceding (Stages 1–4, 6)

That's a fair point. However… · I accept that…, but that doesn't change our verdict because… · Partly, yes. The full picture is…

Rebutting

I'd put it differently. · That's not quite what the evidence shows. · With respect, the figures suggest the opposite.

Presenting a verdict

Our case concerns… · Measured against 4A, it… · On balance, our verdict is…, because…

Differentiation

LearnerWhat to do
Needs more language supportIn the presentation, give them "what happened" or "the apology itself" (factual, quotable). In cross-examination, prepare their answer to one predicted question in sparring. As opposing counsel, they read a prepared leading question.
Stronger / needs challengeAsk them the judge's follow-up question in cross-examination. In Stage 7, they write the rule that explains the exception (for example, why fast didn't mean successful).
Quieter studentsIn the fishbowl, let them observe first, then tap in. In the Tribunal, agree with them in advance which topic they will answer.
Students who dominateRule: once you've answered, the next answer must come from a teammate. Make them the team's timekeeper in sparring.
